## Almost all shallow-water coral reefs now face extinction risk, study warns

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoA school of fish swim above a finger coral colony as it grows on the Great Barrier Reef off the coast of Cairns, Australia [File: Lucas Jackson/Reuters]By AFP and The Associated PressPublished On 22 Oct 202622 Oct 2026Almost all of the world’s shallow-water coral reefs face an extremely high risk of collapse in the next 50 years, the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) has warned in a new global assessment.

In a report released on Thursday, the world authority on threatened wildlife classified 96 percent of the planet’s coral reef systems as “critically endangered”, its highest risk category, with total extinction possible by 2075.

“The future threat of climate change is looming and severe, and that poses a very high risk to the survival of these ecosystems,” said Mishal Gudka, scientific lead of the IUCN Red List of Coral Reef Ecosystems assessment.

Corals are heat-sensitive and particularly vulnerable to human-driven climate change, which is warming the world’s oceans and increasing the frequency and intensity of damaging bleaching events.

When a reef collapses, hard corals disappear and are replaced by algae, rubble and sponges. As a result, the reef no longer supports a diverse ecosystem that can include everything from tiny invertebrates to reef fish like grouper.

The emergence this year of a “super” El Nino, the natural warming phenomenon, also presents a risk to corals, Gudka said at a news conference at the UN’s COP17 biodiversity summit in Yerevan, Armenia.

It’s the latest dose of bad news for coral reefs, which are critical sources for seafood and tourism and often serve as buffers against coastal erosion and storms.

Sometimes dubbed “rainforests of the sea”, they have some of the world’s richest biodiversity and support a quarter of marine species as well as coastal communities and businesses.

Most of the world’s famous reef systems – including the Great Barrier Reef in Australia, the Coral Triangle in the Western Pacific Ocean and Southeast Asia, the Mesoamerican Barrier Reef System and the Red Sea coral reefs – are critically endangered, according to the assessment.

For its report, the IUCN used the “middle-of-the-road” scenario from the UN’s panel of climate experts that projects global temperatures rising 2.7C above pre-industrial levels by 2100.

Its analysis examined 127 tropical and subtropical coral systems covering 188,362 square kilometres.

A week ago, another IUCN study in the journal Nature found that nearly 44 percent of reef-building coral species face an elevated risk of extinction due to climate change. A year ago, a survey found that harmful bleaching of the world’s coral had grown to include 84 percent of the ocean’s reefs, the most intense of its kind in recorded history.

Despite its warning, the IUCN said coral reefs in most parts of the world “are not yet lost”.

“Coral reefs have shown a remarkable ability to bounce back from devastating bleaching events,” said Gudka. “Pockets of surviving, resilient coral reefs can still remain in the future, so we must do everything within our reach to set them up for success.”

## Why measles outbreak in Pennsylvania poses risks beyond US borders

Matt Ferrari, director of Pennsylvania State University’s Center for Infectious Disease Dynamics, said the US was so successful at wiping out measles that it had “the luxury to forget” it was a serious problem.

“It’s been a long time, and for many of us in the United States, and in fact, throughout the Western Hemisphere, we have been able to forget how nasty measles is,” Ferrari told Al Jazeera.

Pennsylvania has been hit especially hard in the most recent outbreak. In September, the state sought emergency support from the CDC to control the spread, even as state and federal health officials quarrelled over the measles-associated death tally — differing over how deaths should be classified. State officials have recorded more than 1,200 cases and over 240 hospitalisations in 42 counties so far this year.

This spike in cases has raised concerns in neighbouring US states. On October 5, New York Governor Kathy Hochul declared a state disaster emergency in response to rising cases in rural areas, expanding the number of health professionals authorised to vaccinate and test people for measles.

But experts warn that the risks extend far beyond US state borders. The US resurgence comes as measles cases increase globally, particularly in communities that are vaccine-sceptical or where there are gaps in healthcare access.

Vaccination coverage among US kindergartners has decreased from 95.2 percent during the 2019-2020 school year to 92.4 percent in the 2025-2026 school year. Experts warn that declining vaccination rates in one geographic location can leave people thousands of miles away vulnerable, making measles prevention a shared global responsibility.

Pennsylvania reported in August that two unvaccinated people died of measles in Lancaster County, which is located in the South Central part of the state. Lancaster is considered the centre of Pennsylvania Dutch Country and home to the nation’s oldest Amish and Mennonite communities, both of which have under-vaccinated populations.

In Pennsylvania, public health officials have worked with community leaders to share information and expand access to vaccination. Joshua Good, principal of Ephrata Mennonite School in Lancaster County, where two students contracted measles earlier this year, worked with state officials to organise a pop-up vaccination clinic at the school.

He said officials also provided communication materials that the school adapted for its community and helped identify at-risk families.

While Good said the response was productive, he told state officials that the Mennonite community still holds a lot of vaccine scepticism.

“I said, look, if we do this, keep in mind, our community by and large…made that decision not because they don’t have access to the vaccines. They made the decision based on their own best judgement,” he told Al Jazeera.

Good, who is vaccinated and vaccinated his children, sees himself as an advocate within the community. If families have questions about measles shots, he will answer them as best he can.

“I have honest conversations with people, try to engage in respectful, charitable ways,” he said. “I’ll try to understand what their hesitancies are. That said, I respect their concerns.”

Although multiple community members received the measles vaccine at the Ephrata clinic, vaccination rates remain below the threshold for herd immunity.

Public health officials advocate for investing in robust community outreach before outbreaks happen.

“You can’t just show up in a community in an emergency– an emergency in which they might feel like you’re there to kind of blame them or shame them,” Jennifer Nuzzo, director of the Pandemic Center at the Brown University School of Public Health, told Al Jazeera. “You need to know them so that they see public health as a resource rather than law enforcement.”

She said low vaccination coverage and distrust of the government allow measles to spread even when vaccines are available. But that shouldn’t stop governments from building rapport with vaccine-sceptical communities.

“I worry that by framing it around their demographics, it lets governments off the hook in terms of what they’re supposed to be doing, which is stopping the spread of this dangerous disease,” Nuzzo said.

Experts explain that declining vaccination rates put not only these communities but also immunocompromised people at greater risk for disease. Herd immunity in the wider population formerly helped shield those most vulnerable to infection.

“Those are communities that have been at risk for years,” Ferrari said. “But higher rates of vaccination in the communities surrounding them offered a barrier.”

The continental Americas was the first World Health Organization (WHO) region to eliminate endemic measles transmission in 2016. Maintaining that status requires high and consistent vaccination coverage. The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) says coverage across the region remains below the target for both doses of the measles vaccine, contributing to noticeable consequences.

PAHO holds an annual RVC Measles Elimination Status Conference where an independent technical body of international experts monitors and reviews whether countries in the Americas have successfully eliminated measles, rubella, and congenital rubella syndrome. A country can lose its “elimination status” when the same measles virus genotype has circulated continuously for at least 12 months.

Last year, Canada became the first country in the continental Americas to lose its status after an outbreak that began in 2024. Some have warned that the US may be on track to lose its status this year.

But Dr Daniel Salas, executive manager of the Special Program for Comprehensive Immunisation at PAHO, told Al Jazeera that it’s not just the US that will be under review.

“We have Mexico, we have Bolivia that is going to be closely assessed because they could potentially lose status as well,” he said.

US Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F Kennedy Jr has repeatedly defended the US response to measles.

“We’re doing a better job than any country in the world at controlling the measles outbreak,” Kennedy said at an August 26 event in Florida. He cited higher case numbers in Mexico, Canada and Britain.

But Nuzzo said the repeated outbreaks across the US point to a country struggling to contain transmission.

“We have not seen it this bad,” she said. “It’s like this perpetual game of Whac-A-Mole. As long as there are vulnerable communities, as long as we don’t have strategies for early detection and rapid response and containment, measles will just keep on happening over and over and over again.”
