## Iran’s calculations as US escalation looms: Trust, midterms, Hormuz

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoA woman holds an Iranian flag as part of a pro-government campaign in downtown Tehran, Iran, on Monday, October 26, 2026 [Vahid Salemi/AP Photo]By Usaid SiddiquiPublished On 27 Oct 202627 Oct 2026As regional powers step up diplomatic efforts to prevent another escalation in the eight-month United States-Israel war on Iran, Tehran is showing little sign of backing down from its red lines.

The announcement by China on Tuesday that Beijing will be sending an envoy to relevant countries in the region soon to help avoid another round of escalation highlights the growing urgency of finding a peaceful solution to a conflict that has disrupted global energy markets and spilled over across the region.

On Monday, senior officials from Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkiye – the three nations which signed the Mecca defence pact in August – met with high-ranking Iranian officials, promising to continue discussions related to “peace and security” in the region.

However, Tehran appears determined not to budge on its demands, particularly as it holds considerable leverage over the future of maritime trade in the Strait of Hormuz, through which one-fifth of global oil and liquefied natural gas (LNG) were shipped before the war.

“Iran’s priority is not simply to secure another ceasefire, but to end the war permanently and establish deterrence so that the cycle of military attacks by the US and Israel does not keep repeating itself,” Negar Mortazavi, editor and host of The Iran Podcast, told Al Jazeera.

Tehran continues to insist on sanctions relief, an end to the US naval blockade on its ports in the Gulf and guarantees that the US will not renew attacks, in exchange for helping to resume normal traffic in the Strait of Hormuz. The US wants Iran to give up more before a final peace settlement is negotiated – including making concessions about its nuclear programme.

Experts say Iran’s current calculations are being driven by several factors, including its deep distrust of Washington – which has launched strikes on Iran three times since June 2025 while conducting talks – the belief that continued economic pressure could force more attractive US concessions, and much-anticipated elections in Israel and the US this week and next.

At the heart of Iran’s position is a belief that previous diplomatic efforts have failed to provide sufficient protection against renewed US and Israeli attacks. The US and Israel launched the first strikes of the war on February 28, just as peace talks were ongoing in Oman. Last year, Israel launched its 12-day war on Iran in June, which the US joined.

Pakistan and Qatar helped broker a memorandum of understanding in June this year aimed at reopening the Strait of Hormuz, ending the US blockade of Iranian ports and laying down the conditions for the start of negotiations over Iran’s nuclear programme.

But the arrangement quickly unravelled as both sides resumed attacks, leading to sustained US attacks on Iran for several weeks in July, followed by retaliatory strikes from Iran on US military assets and energy infrastructure in neighbouring Gulf countries, as well as in Jordan.

Sina Toossi, a senior non-resident fellow at the Center for International Policy (CIP), said the failure of that arrangement had hardened Tehran’s position.

“Iran believes previous negotiations and ceasefire arrangements failed to protect it from renewed military attacks, and it is therefore seeking more tangible and durable guarantees,” he told Al Jazeera.

US President Donald Trump, meanwhile, has remained ambiguous over what course of action he will take in the near future. “My question is, do I make a deal with them or do I finish them off?” Trump told reporters on Sunday. He had previously said attacks on Iran would not take place until after the US midterms on November 3.

Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council, said last week that the country’s armed forces were prepared to respond to any new attacks, including by invading Tehran’s neighbours and hitting bases used in US military operations as part of its new “offensive” strategy in its war with the US.

He also said Iran would only reopen the Strait of Hormuz and begin peace talks once Washington took concrete steps to rebuild trust, including meeting Iran’s latest set of conditions – suspending sanctions, unfreezing Iranian assets, lifting the US naval blockade of Iranian ports and giving concrete assurances that it will not begin strikes again.

The US midterms next week, when all 435 House of Representatives seats and 35 of the 100 Senate seats will be up for grabs, will determine which party has control of the US Congress for the final two years of Trump’s presidency. The war, already deeply unpopular with Americans, is becoming an increasingly contentious domestic issue.

A Reuters/Ipsos survey earlier this month found just 34 percent of Americans approved of Trump’s handling of the conflict – his lowest rating during either of his presidential terms. Republican majorities in both houses of Congress are already wafer-thin – at 53-47 (including two independents who vote with Democrats) in the Senate and 218-214 in the House.

The war has also driven up energy prices – particularly diesel, which has nearly doubled in price, causing a major burden for farmers and small businesses – adding to concerns about inflation and affordability that are dominating the election campaign.

Some reports citing Iranian officials say Tehran may be deliberately prolonging negotiations to deny Trump any sort of political victory before the elections.

A Republican setback could further constrain Trump’s room for manoeuvre, particularly if Democrats gain congressional control and increase scrutiny of the administration’s conduct of the war.

“Tehran wants to teach a lesson to Washington, including future American presidents, that attacking Iran comes with a high price,” analyst Mortazavi said.

“Its deterrence strategy is multilayered, aimed at imposing military, economic and political costs that extend well beyond the battlefield.”

Before the US midterms is Israel’s election on Tuesday, which introduces another dimension to Iran’s calculations. A defeat for Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu could complicate renewing joint attacks with the US on Iran, as experts say he was the main force behind Trump greenlighting the war.

While some believe an opposition victory led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot could potentially ease regional tensions, Netanyahu’s removal would not necessarily translate into a softer Israeli position towards Iran.

Eisenkot is himself a former military chief, and has repeatedly expressed concerns about Iran’s nuclear programme and regional influence.

Nevertheless, Mortazavi said, while Iranian officials are closely watching the political consequences of the war for both Washington and Israel, they recognise the danger of renewed escalation after the elections, “making a durable diplomatic settlement even more urgent”.

Iran’s control over shipping through the Strait of Hormuz remains one of its strongest bargaining chips.

The waterway carried about one-fifth of the world’s energy supplies before the war began on February 28, giving Tehran considerable leverage over global oil markets.

October has recorded the highest number of attacks on commercial shipping around Hormuz, according to data collected by the Washington Institute for Near East Policy.

Brent crude surged above $117 a barrel last week – compared with about $72 pre-war – following Iranian attacks on tankers, while shipping through the strait remains nowhere near pre-war levels.

## Sudan’s war steals children’s future with school closures and displacement

Nine-year-old Abkar Mohammed works cleaning and polishing shoes on the streets of Port Sudan every day. He arrived in the eastern Sudanese city with his mother and three siblings from Khartoum days after the country’s armed conflict erupted on April 15, 2023.

Abkar lost his father before the war began and the family live in a rented a home in al-Kasara, a low-income neighbourhood in Port Sudan.

Due to the family’s financial situation, he doesn’t go to school but does attend classes at a traditional “khalwa” where he learns the Quran.

“When my lesson finishes, I pick up my shoe-cleaning equipment and head to busy areas to work,” he told Al Jazeera. “In the evening, I take public transport home, making sure I get back before sunset.”

He gets around 1,000 Sudanese pounds ($0.11) to clean or stitch a pair of shoes, and by the end of the day, he earns around 10,000 pounds ($1.08). He gives the money to his mother to cover the family’s daily needs.

“My 23-year-old brother works on casual jobs in the markets to cover most of the household expenses,” he said.

Abkar’s story echoes the lives of eight million of the country’s 17 million school-age children who are out of school, according to UNICEF.

In Darfur, one of the areas of Sudan hardest hit by the war, about 80 percent of schools remained closed with 70 percent of children in the area not attending classes.

Save the Children estimates that children in Sudan have missed on average about 484 days of classroom learning since the war began in April 2023, due to prolonged school closures, fighting, displacement and damage to education facilities.

The Rapid Support Forces (RSF), which has been in conflict with the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) since 2023, has been accused of targeting schools and universities in drone strikes, along with other civilian infrastructure.

In September 2026, a drone attack on two warehouses in North Kordofan destroyed more than 330,000 school textbooks and other learning materials.

Sulaima Ishag, head of the Government Advisory Council for Women and Children, told Al Jazeera that children who live in conflict zones, such as Darfur and parts of Kordofan, are at greatest risk of permanently losing access to education. This can have a dire impact on their future, particularly for girls.

“[There are] cases of school dropouts and child marriage reported, alongside sexual violence. [Girls] face additional risks, including adolescent pregnancy and increased domestic responsibilities, which can further limit their chances of returning to education,” Sulaima told Al Jazeera.

“The absence of schools as safe and protective environments leaves children increasingly vulnerable to a range of threats, including child labour, early marriage, recruitment and use by armed groups, exploitation, sexual violence, trafficking and family separation. It also takes a significant psychological and social toll on children … [particularly those] from the poorest families … even in areas considered relatively safe.”

Eva Hinds, UNICEF’s head of advocacy and communication in Sudan, said that nearly half of all school buildings in the country no longer function as classrooms, due to war damage.

“Displaced children, many of whom have been forced to flee several times, face additional barriers, as repeated displacement disrupts their learning and separates them from teachers and classmates, often leaving them without learning materials or a safe place to study,” Hinds told Al Jazeera.

Children who no longer attend school lose access to a safe space and are exposed to the risks associated with child labour, recruitment by armed groups, and other abuses.

Hinds warned that the longer children remain out of school, the harder it will become for them to make up for lost learning. This disruption can deepen existing inequalities and mean that a generation of Sudanese children and young people will be unable to contribute to the country’s future peace, recovery and development.

“The conflict in Sudan continues to have a devastating impact on children’s education. The consequences extend far beyond the classroom,” said Hinds.

Psychologist Hanaa Mohamed told Al Jazeera that prolonged displacement and interruptions to schooling have taken a significant psychological toll on children. Many are in a constant state of fear, experience sleep disturbances and have difficulty focusing.

“These pressures have also led to changes in behaviour, including increased aggression or social withdrawal,” she told Al Jazeera. “Being out of school deprives children of an important protective environment. The absence of that protection has also helped bring cases of violence and sexual exploitation against children to light.”

Sudan’s economy has been set back 30 years by the war, with 60 percent of Sudanese now living in poverty.

Families fleeing the conflict often enter new towns or camps where they have few social connections and sometimes are just carrying the clothes on their backs. Sudan’s dire financial situation means that there are few jobs available for adults, forcing parents to turn to their children for essential income.

Saliha Omer, who sells tea on a street in Port Sudan, said her meagre finances prevented her from enrolling her 11-year-old son in school.

He currently works at a grocery shop in Port Sudan, but Saliha plans to return to Khartoum in the coming days so he can attend school.

“I couldn’t enrol him because of our financial circumstances after we were displaced,” Saliha told Al Jazeera.

Mahmoud al-Ghali, 16, has not been to school since the war broke out when he was aged 13, and has been displaced several times during the war.

For the past three years he has sold sweets and crisps on the streets of Port Sudan to provide his family with extra income.

Mahmoud remembers his final days at school, when he and his classmates sat their end-of-year exams. They were preparing for intermediate school and a new chapter in their lives, but the war changed their plans.

“After reaching Al-Fao in December 2023, I worked as a street vendor until I managed to save some money. Then I came to Port Sudan with my uncle in July 2025, hoping to earn more so I could help my family with household expenses,” he told Al Jazeera.

## King Charles’s Caribbean tour: Will he discuss slavery reparations?

This comes as demands for reparations over Britain’s notorious role in the slave trade grow louder, and as the monarchy faces questions about its future in the region.

His tour begins on Tuesday with the archipelagic country of the Bahamas, where he will be staying for three days.

Then he will make his way to Guyana, a former British colony that had more than 100,000 slaves when the trade was abolished in 1807.

After two days in Guyana, where he is expected to meet the president and elected leaders of Amerindian villages, he will head to Antigua and Barbuda for the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting (CHOGM).

The meeting, from November 1-4, is expected to include discussions about the environment and trade and development, although the question of British reparations for slavery is also likely to feature.

Government officials in the Bahamas and in Antigua and Barbuda – both of which are part of the British Commonwealth with King Charles as their ultimate head of state – have signalled that they want to become republics. Guyana gained independence from the UK in 1966, and has a president as its head of state.

Guyanese President Mohamed Irfaan Ali said in an interview with ITV News that while Charles “rightfully” recognised slavery as the “darkest days in human history” during a visit to Barbados in 2021 when he was still the prince of Wales, an apology should be his next step.

“You exploited the region. You destroyed lives. You created underdevelopment,” Ali said, in a reference to the lingering legacy of the British Empire and the slave trade.

In 2023, a Lord Ashcroft Poll revealed that 51 percent of surveyed respondents in the Bahamas wanted to become a republic, while 27 percent preferred to retain the British monarchy as head of state.

Niambi Hall Campbell-Dean, chair of the Bahamas National Reparations Committee, was critical of the king ahead of his arrival. “Why do we still have a king? What does this person really represent to us? Why is he coming and how will that benefit us?” she asked.

Jamaica, which is not on the itinerary for this trip by King Charles, made its stance clear in September, when it filed a $10bn slavery reparations petition in London, addressing it to the king.

That was the first time a British Commonwealth state has taken legal action pursuing “the cause of reparatory justice”, as the Jamaican delegation called it.

The petition is due to be heard by a UK privy council in March next year. The UK has, so far, refused to formally apologise or pay reparations for slavery.

Also in September, Caribbean Community and Common Market (CARICOM) Secretary-General Carla Barnett said reparations are “fundamentally about justice”.

“It is about recognition, accountability and transformation, and about building a just and equitable future for the descendants of those who, as the chattel of colonial owners, endured untold suffering, loss of life and freedom,” she said.

The transatlantic slave trade was recently declared the “gravest crime against humanity” in a United Nations resolution in March this year.

The last time a British royal visited Jamaica was when Prince William and Kate Middleton, then the duke and duchess of Cambridge, toured the island in 2022 during their Caribbean Platinum Jubilee tour.

The more memorable moment of the trip was when the couple was pictured shaking hands with Jamaican children with a fence separating them, which many criticised for being “tone-deaf”.

The meeting in Antigua and Barbuda is expected to cover climate resilience as well as trade and development.

Leaders and representatives of 56 Commonwealth countries will be attending. Many want an apology for slavery from the king, but Buckingham Palace recently announced that will not be happening.

“The visit programme will…not shy away from ‘the darkest days of our past’, as his majesty has described them, but – in keeping with the wishes of our host nations – ‌it ⁠will principally be oriented to the vibrant present and promising future of each country,” a palace spokesperson said in a statement.

The palace added that it is “important” to acknowledge the “creative” ways that Charles has tried to right “enduring inequalities”.

## The Ballon d’Or has a narrow definition of footballing greatness

British-Nigerian journalist, broadcaster and lawyer, covering international football and its politics.

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoEngland and Bayern Munich forward Harry Kane reacts after receiving the Ballon d'Or award for the best male footballer during the 2026 Ballon d'Or France Football award ceremony at the Palladium Theatre in London on October 26, 2026 [AFP]The Ballon d’Or is football’s most prestigious individual honour. On Monday, Bayern Munich striker Harry Kane became the first Englishman to win it since Michael Owen in 2001. But his victory also raises a fundamental question about the award: How can individual excellence be fairly assessed in a team sport?

For much of the past two decades, that question has been overshadowed by the extraordinary rivalry between Lionel Messi and Cristiano Ronaldo. From 2008 to 2017, one or the other won the Ballon d’Or every year, turning the annual award into a contest between the two superstars. Messi eventually accumulated a record eight trophies and Ronaldo five, together accounting for 13 of the 17 awards presented between 2008 and 2025.

Their rivalry generated unprecedented excitement around an award first presented in 1956, when England’s “wizard of the dribble”, Sir Stanley Matthews, became its inaugural winner. But it also pushed several important questions into the background. Has the growing commercialisation of the Ballon d’Or distracted from its purpose? Why does it overwhelmingly reward attacking players? And why does a supposedly global award remain so heavily focused on European football?

The commercialisation of the Ballon d’Or reflects the transformation of football into a global entertainment industry, in which individual players have become brands in their own right.

“The whole dynamic of the Ballon d’Or has changed because the dynamic of the sports and entertainment industry has changed with the way individual athletes are marketed,” Steve Martin, founding partner of MSQ Sport + Entertainment, formerly of M&C Saatchi, told The Athletic.

“They’ve found a way as an individual athlete or player to build their own profile and their own fanbase, as opposed to relying on their clubs. They control their own destiny, and they market themselves.”

Former Switzerland defender Ramon Vega has been more critical of this development. “The commercialisation and monetisation of this award is not taking it in the right direction,” he told the writer.

The Ballon d’Or has undoubtedly become a bigger spectacle, but the attention surrounding the award should not obscure the question of what constitutes individual excellence. And commercialisation is not its only problem. The award has also long favoured players in attack over those in other positions.

A goalkeeper’s or defender’s performance is as important as that of midfielders and strikers, in a sport where a team is supposed to be as great as the sum of its parts. Yet Russia’s Lev Yashin remains the only goalkeeper to have won the award, in 1963, while Italy’s Fabio Cannavaro was the last defender to do so, in 2006, after captaining his country to World Cup victory. Germany’s Franz Beckenbauer and Matthias Sammer were among the few other defenders to have received the honour.

“Goalkeepers and defenders are also part of the spectacle. Attackers don’t just play amongst themselves,” Cannavaro told France Football after winning the award in 2006. “If a goalkeeper has saved 15 or so matches in a year, it’s only fair to highlight him.”

But the Italian’s observations have done little to change the pervasive bias of voters for the award, as strikers and midfielders on the European club and national team stage remain the yardstick for measuring global excellence.

That focus on European football points to another longstanding weakness of the Ballon d’Or: Its failure to adequately recognise outstanding players from elsewhere in the world.

For nearly four decades after the award was established, only European players were eligible to win it. This was a great injustice to exceptional footballers from other continents, even those competing in Europe’s leading leagues. The late Diego Maradona was perhaps the most notable victim of this exclusion.

The restriction was finally lifted in 1995, allowing players of any nationality at European clubs to compete for the prize. It took another 12 years, until 2007, for the award to be opened to players competing anywhere in the world.

It could be argued that when the award began, the club and national team game outside Europe was beyond the close observation and expert knowledge of the Ballon d’Or selectors. But that argument no longer holds water. Knowledge of the global game has grown exponentially, yet recognition of excellence beyond Europe’s leading competitions remains limited.

When Liberia’s George Weah became the first non-European player to win the Ballon d’Or in 1995, following outstanding performances for Paris Saint-Germain and AC Milan, his victory was expected to open the door for exceptional players from parts of the footballing world that had long been denied recognition.

But 31 years on, the former president of Liberia remains the only person outside Europe and South America to have won it.

“When I started playing football, I never thought I would ever win the Ballon d’Or and emerge as the best player in the world. I just had a passion for the game and I worked hard. Every day. I would rather train than eat or sleep,” Weah told the writer in Monrovia.

“I am surprised that no other African player has won the award since I did so. I think there have been others that deserve to win. But it is down to those who select the winners.”

Cameroon’s Samuel Eto’o, the former Barcelona and Inter striker, now president of his country’s football federation, is one person with a right to feel aggrieved that he didn’t win the award before retiring in 2019.

“It’s difficult to understand how Samuel Eto’o never won the Ballon d’Or with the impressive career he had,” Jose Mourinho, who managed Eto’o at Inter, said in a 2019 interview with Cameroon Radio.

“He played in three Champions League finals, winning two with Barcelona and scoring in both finals. He also won one Champions League at Inter and won many league titles. He was the best striker in the world for several years, and I think he deserved a Ballon d’Or, but these are things out of our control.”

Eto’o’s omission, like Weah’s continued status as the only African winner, raises questions about how the award’s selectors and voters assess the achievements of players from outside football’s traditional European and South American powerhouses.

The Ballon d’Or already has an international voting panel, with journalists from 100 countries selecting the winner from a shortlist of 30 players. But geographical representation among voters does not necessarily translate into recognition of the world’s footballing talent.

France Football, the award’s organisers, should ensure that the process of selecting nominees and winners draws on a genuinely global understanding of the game and gives due recognition to exceptional players, regardless of nationality or where they compete.

The award must also address its other shortcomings. Football is a team sport in which goalkeepers, defenders, midfielders and strikers all contribute to success, and individual recognition should reflect that. Nor should the growing commercial appeal of the Ballon d’Or distract from the footballing achievements it was created to honour.

The Ballon d’Or should be a celebration of individual excellence across all positions and all continents, not an award dominated by football’s biggest stars and Europe’s leading competitions. Only then can it truly claim to recognise the best footballer in the world.
