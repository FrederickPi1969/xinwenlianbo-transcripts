## Palestinian citizens of Israel face intimidation after election success

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoProtesters hold up placards during a demonstration on Habima Square in Tel Aviv on October 31, 2026, calling for the formation of a Jewish-Arab coalition government following Israel's general election [Jack Guez/AFP]By Mohammad MansourPublished On 4 Nov 20264 Nov 2026Israel has officially certified its election results – delivering a blow to right-wing claims of a stolen vote – but the record 19 seats captured by Palestinian-led parties have triggered aggressive police raids, arbitrary arrests, and targeted interrogations by state security apparatuses.

The electoral gains made by the parties representing Palestinian citizens of Israel – the Joint List and the United Arab List (Ra’am) – could give the Zionist opposition to Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu the upper hand in forming the next government, if they choose to work with the Arab parties.

Meanwhile, Netanyahu and his allies have portrayed the increased vote share for the Palestinian-led parties as the result of illegality, a+nd say they represent Jewish Israelis.

Political analyst Mamoun Abu Amer told Al Jazeera that the political backlash was preplanned.

“From the beginning, it was clearly a systematic campaign when Arab citizens mobilised heavily, especially in the villages, the Naqab and the Bedouin areas,” Abu Amer said. He noted that the heavy turnout deeply shocked the Israeli political establishment, prompting Netanyahu to launch a smear campaign to discredit the democratic process.

Ahmad Tibi, leader of the Ta’al Party – which is part of the Joint List – shared a similar analysis.

“There is no indication that the elections were manipulated in the Arab towns, as everyone expected the voting rate to be high this time in advance due to the bad reality created by this government for our public,” Tibi told Al Jazeera. “On the contrary, there was a press report on Israel’s Channel 12 television revealing that the Likud party, led by Netanyahu, had prepared and readied a file before the elections to invent this falsehood when the results were issued, because they know with absolute certainty that they will lose the elections.”

The intimidation campaign against Palestinian citizens of Israel materialised through aggressive police actions on election day. As reported by The Jerusalem Post, police arrested several Ra’am supporters for alleged vote-buying, which Likud immediately invoked to claim an attempt to steal the election. However, many of the suspects were quickly released, with a judge noting that suspicions had weakened and that investigators had failed to explain why restrictions were necessary. Defence lawyers maintained the seized funds were intended for charitable assistance.

“The best proof of this is that the detainees were released,” Tibi said. “We said from the beginning that the mountain laboured and brought forth a mouse.”

The security crackdown extended beyond party activists to media personnel. Abd Abu Shahadeh, a journalist and political activist, was arrested on Tuesday night by the Israeli police and the domestic security service, the Shin Bet.

Sami Abu Shehadeh, the chairman of the Balad Party, another member of the Joint List, called it an attempt by Netanyahu to “steal the elections”.

Human rights and Israeli affairs expert Mustafa Ibrahim argued that Netanyahu’s approach is deeply rooted in discriminatory state policy.

Ibrahim pointed to the treatment of Palestinians in Israel since the formation of the state in 1948 – when hundreds of thousands of Palestinians were forced out of their homes in the Nakba – including the period of military rule that was imposed on them until 1966.

Palestinian citizens of Israel have always been viewed as a “fifth column”, Ibrahim said, “and they are described as enemies of the state.”

Tibi emphasised that the suppression of Palestinian rights in Israel was part of a broader ongoing pattern.

“Political intimidation began on October 7 itself, when celebrities from our community were summoned for investigation simply for liking a post or writing a Quranic verse whose interpretation was distorted,” Tibi told Al Jazeera, referring to the October 7, 2023, Hamas-led attack on Israel.

He added that the decision in late September by the Israeli electoral committee to disqualify the Palestinian-led parties from the elections – later overturned by the Israeli Supreme Court – was part of that campaign.

“The tools multiplied and the goal is one, which is indeed to intimidate us from political work as candidates and voters,” Tibi said.

The political pressure from Netanyahu and his allies has engulfed Israeli state institutions. According to The Jerusalem Post, Shin Bet Director David Zini requested to interrogate senior Palestinian lawmakers, including Tibi and United Arab List leader Mansour Abbas, over foreign interference claims. Attorney-General Gali Baharav-Miara explicitly denied the request, leading to attacks from Netanyahu’s Likud party.

Abbas dismissed the allegations, calling the investigation reports as attempts to “smear the Arab public” and were unacceptable.

“I and all members of the United Arab List are not afraid of any investigation and are prepared to appear before any law enforcement authority,” Abbas said, provided the process is conducted under the attorney general and judicial system.

Far-right National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir has demanded Baharav-Miara’s removal.

“Slowly but surely, they came to understand that this is a legal advisor who tried to incriminate a minister, tried to incriminate me, and ordered the Shin Bet chief to follow me and act against me. Just for that alone, she should have been sent home,” Ben-Gvir wrote on social media.

Abu Amer said that this was an example of Netanyahu avoiding direct confrontation and instead utilising figures like Ben-Gvir to carry out these tasks. “The attack on the Attorney-General is part of a systematic campaign, not a spontaneous one,” Abu Amer noted.

Ibrahim highlighted that this political incitement occurs against a backdrop of systemic racism.

“The targeting of the national minority with intimidation campaigns has been supported by the head of the government and the head of the state during the past few years,” Ibrahim said, adding that this came in the context of the killing of more than 190 people in Palestinian communities in Israel and in occupied East Jerusalem this year in violence that continues to ravage the community, and is partly blamed on a lack of policing and government funding.

The Palestinian-led parties are now focused on using their increased number of seats in parliament to address that issue, and others facing their community, while facing off against Netanyahu and his allies.

“In the past, they were annoyed by our patriotism only; today, they fear our influence. We hope it will actually backfire,” Tibi said. “The direct practical step is to overthrow them politically, and this comes through several procedures, operations, and a parliamentary step. But we will not stop at overthrowing them, rather we will work to improve the reality for the better.”

## The new Congress may struggle to push a progressive agenda

Major change on key issues, such as Israel-Palestine, will be limited by the president and the Democratic establishment.

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoThe US Capitol building is seen on Capitol Hill in Washington on September 28, 2026 [Kylie Cooper/Reuters]As the dust settles on the 2026 US midterm elections, two facts are clear: First, the Democrats have achieved a historic victory – and second, Donald Trump remains president for the coming two years.

That means Congressional Democrats will have three legislative lanes to pursue: The first is constraining Trump. This includes doing what they can through Congress to bring the Iran war to a conclusion, blocking many of his nominations, particularly to the judiciary, ending ICE’s aggressive immigrant detentions, and pursuing oversight of Trump and his allies’ financial networks and alleged corruption.

The second lane will be about demonstrating a positive policy agenda to inform the 2028 cycle, when Trump will be off the ballot. This is more about “messaging bills” focused on social issues, but could also include economic measures such as tax reform proposals, as well as significant reforms of US global defence cooperation authorities and shifts to major foreign policy questions, such as aid to Israel.

The final lane will be that – perhaps narrow – set of issues where bipartisanship and cooperation with the White House is possible. Putting guardrails around America’s burgeoning artificial intelligence (AI) sector may fit in that box, although some of Trump’s biggest funders are likely to do what they can to derail progress.

Many of the bills that will be put forward in the first and second lanes would be subject to presidential veto. Others will be toned down, as the Democratic establishment seeks to resist the progressive left’s ambitious agenda. These dynamics are likely to make the upcoming Congress less productive.

On foreign policy, in particular, Congress is able to pass just a slim set of acts without presidential endorsement, such as war powers legislation, limiting the president’s ability to wage war without Congressional approval; or measures like the recent Senate resolution advanced by Maryland Democrat Chris Van Hollen to demand reporting on Israel’s human rights record; or committee subpoenas, which can be used to investigate members of the Trump administration for wrongdoing. But they are likely to be ignored or stonewalled by the White House, leading to months, if not years, of fruitless litigation.

That is not to say that introducing “stand-alone” bills on issues, such as arms transfers to Israel, would not be important even if blocked by the president. They remain useful both for framing the conversation and for getting members of Congress on the record and establishing consensus positions.

In the Senate, where several incumbents are likely to throw their hats into the upcoming presidential nomination contest, such bills could be used to highlight the progressive credentials of some candidates – or to force more conservative members into awkward positions. That might ultimately make a difference in terms of the framing and debate for the 2028 election – and a potential Democratic administration thereafter. But it will not change anything in the immediate term.

There are at least three tools available to legislators where a fierce – and perhaps impactful – debate is likely to play out. The first is that enabled by Congress’ constitutional “power of the purse”.  If the current Republican majorities do not manage to pass an annual funding bill by the end of this calendar year, the Democrats will have both the current year and the next two years of US government funding under their control.

This includes, of course, Israel’s annual allocation of $3.3bn, which Trump himself put into doubt in a surprising and as yet unclarified statement on election day. If nothing else, the president’s call for an end to security assistance to Israel may accelerate what would likely have been a steady reduction under Democratic leadership.

The new Congress will also be able to reconsider a range of funding restrictions and requirements, such as the prohibition on funding the United Nations agency for Palestinian refugees (UNRWA), or the limitations on funding the Palestinian Authority.

Passing such bills will not be simply a matter of partisan rancour — we can expect some pretty tough battles to play out within the Democratic caucus itself on some of these issues. Then it is another matter of whether the Trump administration adheres to the law with the funding it ultimately receives.

The second area to watch is that of oversight. With the majority in hand, Democrats in the House and Senate can set the agenda – deciding which actions by the Trump administration are ripe for investigation, identifying witnesses, and using the committee gavels to drive the public debate.

Party leadership will be eager to focus on the issues that are most damaging to the current administration and its likely candidates for succession, such as Vice President JD Vance or Secretary of State Marco Rubio, while being least disruptive to party unity.

And because the system relies on seniority, control of the agenda of Congressional committees that can investigate the government would rest with Democratic establishment members who would be less likely than their newer or younger peers to be critical of the US-Israel relationship.

That said, it is hard to imagine a Democratic Congress that does not at least conduct oversight of Trump’s Board of Peace and its opaque sources of funding and influence. Another likely topic is the question of US involvement in the current war with Iran.

What the Democrats choose not to pursue via oversight will also be important: The weaponisation of anti-Semitism by the current and previous Congress to attack the US education sector and civil society should come to an end under the next Congress. Though certainly Democratic leadership will be keen to find ways to demonstrate that they still care about genuine concerns from the Jewish community, one would hope that these are pursued with a more concrete grounding in reality and shared security than as a tool for repressing pro-Palestinian speech.

But many in the Democratic base would like to see the party do much more – including holding hearings into the US role in enabling Israel’s genocide in Gaza, a topic that would ensnare senior officials from the Biden administration in a very public accounting that the establishment is keen to avoid.

Here is where the details of the outcome of the midterms will truly matter. While there is no doubt that the centre point of the Democratic Congressional caucus has shifted to the left for the upcoming 120th Congress, particularly in the House, the size of the majority matters.

As recent Republican Houses have demonstrated, a small majority is a challenge for the speaker, as it empowers a small group of members to exert significant leverage through the threat of withdrawal of their support for basic procedural steps that are typically decided on a partisan vote basis.

As a result of the midterms “blue tsunami”, however, the Democratic majority is secure enough that leadership will have to make few real concessions to the left of their party, likely teeing up some tense, and almost inevitably public, tussling in the coming two years, particularly when it comes to appropriations bills.

In short, the Democrats’ campaign for legislative power has been a success, but particularly in the face of an imperious White House that has shown little interest in even longstanding legal precedent in its deference to Congress, it remains incomplete.

At the same time, being the party locked out of power has enabled the Democrats up to this point to dodge some degree of the internal wrangling that is already long overdue on key issues, such as foreign policy on Israel.

A majority in Congress may not give them complete control – but it does mean they will now own the legislative branch’s decisions. Whether they demonstrate a meaningful and proactive shift – or let themselves be constrained by establishment conservatism – may define the viability of their path to further electoral success in 2028.

Either way, the American people will be watching what Democrats do and do not do – as will the Republicans who, as Trump enters his “lame duck” years, are likely to undergo an evolution of their own on these key issues.

This makes progress in the coming Congress all the more acute an issue for the Democrats. The challenge for them is simple: take credible leadership on driving change or face the very real possibility of being outflanked by the “America First” right.

## Is Trump really a ‘lame duck’ after losing both houses of US Congress?

Despite losing legislative control, the US president still has strong executive powers to push his agenda forward.

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoUS President Donald Trump throws MAGA hats to the crowd during his midterm campaign rally [File: Kent Nishimura/AFP]By Al Jazeera StaffPublished On 4 Nov 20264 Nov 2026Democrats have flipped some long-held Republican seats and secured a majority in both houses of the US Congress, but a defiant President Donald Trump has given a mixed reaction, expressing his willingness to accept the results and work with the Democrats while also positioning himself to put up a fight.

But critics and commentators are already declaring that, with the Republican midterm losses, the president is “lame duck”.

The ‘lame duck’ period for a president refers to the period at the end of a president’s term, usually after their successor has been elected but before they have left office. An outgoing politician is largely believed to have considerably less influence due to their limited time remaining in office.

The use of the term has expanded to include longer periods when the scheduled or expected departure of a politician results in a depreciation of their ability to influence policymaking.

With Trump leading the Republican Party and the Republicans in control of Congress, the president had a great deal of influence over the US legislative agenda. But now that the Democratic Party will lead both houses of Congress, his influence over that agenda will be greatly diminished, and Democratic worries about the president’s opposition may be reduced by his nearing, and unavoidable, departure from office – assuming Trump does not find a way to seek a third term.

Moreover, with the midterm election losses largely being interpreted as a repudiation of Trump’s policies and a date set for his exit from office – January 2029 – his ability to convince or cajole his fellow Republicans to support his policies could also be curtailed.

This has led pundits to propose that Trump is already entering the lame duck period of his second presidency with two years still remaining in his term.

While “lame duck” presidents are often viewed as weak or ineffective, history shows they are far from powerless in pushing their agenda forward.

The US president is entering a period of divided government but, despite losing his strong legislative influence, he still has executive powers.

Trump has been known to use executive orders to bypass legislative gridlock in the past, centralising authority in the White House even when the Republican Party controlled Congress.

In January 2025, when Congress allocated only a fraction of the funds requested for controlling the southern US border, Trump declared a national emergency in order to divert billions of dollars toward building a physical border barrier by executive order instead.

When a president declares a national emergency, Congress can enact a joint resolution terminating the emergency, but the president can then use his veto power to block that.

Presidential vetoes can only be overturned by a two-thirds majority in both the House of Representatives and the Senate, which the Democratic Party cannot command by itself.

Going forward – as Trump’s new apparel boldly states – the president is likely to make abundant use of his power of veto.

On Tuesday, he told Fox News that he would veto all “insane” legislation from Democrats. “And after a couple of months of vetoes, they’ll go out, and they’ll say, ‘How do we get this stuff done?’ And we’ll work together,” he added.

Yes. Historically, lame duck presidents have been able to achieve major policy goals, but they tend to focus on areas where the executive branch has broad autonomous powers.

“Despite the damaging results for the Republican Party, many issues remain beyond the reach of Congress,” James Boys, research fellow at the Centre on US Politics at UCL, told Al Jazeera.

“The US Constitution grants the president of the United States wide-ranging powers in the area of foreign policy, and it should be anticipated that this is where Trump will spend much of his remaining time in office.”

Several presidents in US history have opted to do just that. After losing control of Congress to the Republicans in the 2014 midterms, for example, former US President Barack Obama used his executive authority to finalise the Iran Nuclear Deal, also known as the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA), and to normalise diplomatic relations with Cuba.

President Barack Obama was able to defend the Affordable Care Act (ACA), his embattled health care law, during his lame duck period.

Former President Bill Clinton spent his final months in office focusing on foreign policy to broker peace between Israelis and Palestinians by hosting the Camp David Summit 2000. The talks eventually collapsed, however.

He also used his executive authority to push through plans to protect 58.5 million acres of forestland, restrict large-scale mining and logging on designated federal grounds and preserve watershed resources.

Former President Joe Biden also entered a lame duck period after announcing he would not run for the presidential race in 2024. In his last days as president, Biden issued a sweeping federal pardon for his son, Hunter Biden, and preventative pardons for other family members and staff.

Reversing prior US policy, Biden granted Ukraine permission to use US-supplied missiles to strike deeper inside Russia. He also directed the Department of Defense to give the remainder of a $61bn aid package to Ukraine to avoid risking it being cancelled by the incoming Trump administration.

## Why are Europe’s governments struggling to respond to growing protests?

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoPeople take part in a demonstration organised by labour unions in Paris, France [File: Reuters]By Virginia PietromarchiPublished On 4 Nov 20264 Nov 2026Protests across Europe show no sign of abating.

They subside on certain days, only to flare up a few days later. Largely led by young people, demonstrations have swept across at least eight European countries, driven by grievances ranging from school conditions and the cost of diesel to a deeper sense of insecurity about the future – and the feeling that opportunities are increasingly out of reach.

“They are plural and uncoordinated, with no single leader or programme,” said Alberto Alemanno, professor of European Union law and policy at HEC Paris Business School. “So it’s less a rejection of one policy and more a signal that people no longer trust the system to deliver. That’s why they are so critical.”

For governments, addressing discontent is proving difficult. Experts say there is no easy fix, and that many have so far struggled to offer answers beyond a show of force. The protests are likely to continue, they warn.

“Where there has been a reaction, the focus is on public order and security,” Alemanno said. “Part of the reason is that, like any popular protest, these movements include or attract small violent minorities. They dominate the headlines and risk undermining legitimate claims.”

Rallies continue because they are increasingly about more than individual grievances, said Donatella della Porta, a political science professor at the Scuola Normale Superiore in Florence. While governments have tried to address some of the protesters’ material demands, they have failed to grasp the broader political concerns driving the demonstrations.

“At this point, protests are no longer just about investing more in the school system. There are also fundamental issues, including military spending, governments’ alliance with [United States President Donald] Trump, the rising cost of gasoline and the war in Iran,” she said. “The issue of Palestine is also very central to these protests.”

This week, protesters have continued to blockade several high schools and universities in France, where students have been pushing for better conditions, more teachers and the replacement of Parcoursup, an admission system that some say is discriminatory.

In Germany, metal and electrical workers have gone on strike while students have demonstrated over fears of the return of conscription. In Belgium, firefighters marched through Brussels, demanding more staff and better equipment. And on Friday in Italy, the USB union brought tens of thousands of people together, calling for a reduction in military spending.

France’s Education Minister Edouard Geffray said on Monday that some measures to meet students’ demands have been adopted, including capping class sizes to 28 for the first year of high school, creating 10,000 places in higher technician programmes, and transforming Parcoursup.

Other measures require in-depth work between now and the end of December, he said on X. But the announcements have failed to quell the protests. On Tuesday, dozens of schools remained blockaded and several universities were disrupted, according to French media.

A nationwide strike over wages, the cost of living and public services is also expected on November 5.

In Spain, massive protests swept through the country for weeks over a housing crisis, triggered by the eviction and subsequent death of an 87-year-old pensioner from her lifelong home. On Tuesday, police dismantled a sprawling tent camp in Madrid’s Puerta del Sol square.

Officials said the eviction took place overnight “without incident or episodes of violence”, reporting that 334 people were removed and two arrested.

In a post on Telegram, protest organisers lamented “a violent police operation” that they said left several people injured.

The government has approved new housing measures, including limits on rent increases and temporary protections against eviction for some vulnerable tenants.

But these promises have done little to dissuade protesters, who are planning a general strike over housing rights and the cost of living on November 11.

In Italy, after violence broke out during protests two weeks ago, the government of far-right leader Giorgia Meloni tightened security measures. “Violent participation” in a demonstration – by joining a group of five or more people and throwing stones, firecrackers or sticks – was made a criminal offence that could result in up to five years in prison and a fine of up to 20,000 euros ($22,500). Italy also made parents liable for damage caused by their children and adopted a policy of preventive detention of children.

The growing emphasis on public order has raised concerns among human rights organisations, which warn that European governments risk treating social discontent as a security threat rather than addressing the grievances behind it.

In a statement in October, UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Turk raised serious concerns about the state responses to protests across Europe and the unnecessary and excessive use of force against protesters, including children.

Following the use of tear gas and stun grenades, among others, by French police against young protesters, Human Rights Watch said the government should “listen to the demands of young people on the streets”.

Della Porta said governments risk further alienating protesters by responding to demonstrations primarily through policing and repression.

“They can start by doing concrete things, such as recognising the right to protest and not sending police in a counterinsurgency-style approach to repress students,” she said.

Several European governments face significant fiscal constraints, while simultaneously increasing defence spending and investing in security. They now find themselves having to respond to demands for better public services, affordable housing and higher living standards while their budgets are increasingly stretched.

The fiscal constraints are particularly visible in some of Europe’s largest economies. Italy’s public debt is projected to reach 138.5 percent of gross domestic product (GDP) in 2026, limiting its room for additional spending. France faces an even more immediate budgetary squeeze, with its deficit forecast at 5.4 percent of GDP, well above the EU’s 3 percent limit. Spain, meanwhile, has struggled to secure parliamentary approval for a new budget, forcing the government to roll over its 2023 spending plans.

These pressures come as governments are trying to increase defence spending, while citizens are demanding more investment in housing, education and public services.

“People will tolerate hard trade-offs if they are explained and if citizens have a say in them. Ignoring them is what fuels anger.”

## Can Democrats stop Iran war, reverse Trump’s tariffs after midterms win?

Democrats can block Trump’s legislative agenda and launch investigations, but they have no two-thirds majority to override a presidential veto.

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoUS President Donald Trump suffered a scathing defeat in the midterm elections on November 3, 2026 [File: AFP]By Federica MarsiPublished On 4 Nov 20264 Nov 2026Democrats have taken control of the United States House of Representatives and Senate in Tuesday’s midterm elections in a scathing defeat for US President Donald Trump’s Republican Party.

The Congress that will convene in early January will be radically different from the first two years of Trump’s second presidential term, which saw Republicans holding majorities in both chambers.

While Democrats will have greater power to scrutinise Trump’s policies and obstruct any legislation they oppose, there is still a lot a president can do without congressional approval.

Trump has acknowledged the role the war on Iran has played in Republican losses in the midterm elections. “I think a part of it was Iran, but at the same time, people understood the importance of what I’ve done with Iran,” Trump said during a telephone interview with Fox News.

Frustration among voters about inflation, and the rising cost of fuel and groceries have been among the main factors behind their rebuke to Republicans. Democrats tapped into this by campaigning heavily on affordability and domestic spending.

Trump embarked on the war on Iran with Israel in February without congressional authorisation. He could still order attacks, but he could also face constraints from the new Congress. Lawmakers could use their power to deny additional funding when the current government-funding bill expires on December 11, presenting a critical budget constraint on Trump’s military decisions.

However, James Boys, research fellow at the Centre on US Politics at University College London, said that while Congress may wish to cut off funding, it may not be straightforward. “With troops in theatre, this would prove to be highly problematic and be seen to be placing American lives at risk,” he said.

Analysts also said the War Powers Resolution is unlikely to succeed in reining Trump in. Previous attempts to use the 1973 federal law have been defeated or rendered ineffective.

The War Powers Resolution states that a president can repel an imminent attack, but military actions must end within 60 days unless Congress declares war. The president can then issue a veto, which can only be overcome by a two-thirds supermajority in both the House and Senate.

The projected breakdown of the newly elected Congress shows that Democrats are well short of those supermajority thresholds.

Scott Lucas, professor of international politics at the UCD Clinton Institute in Dublin, Ireland, said that while Congress is constrained without a two-thirds majority, what it could do is propose legislation by “working with some Republicans who might distance themselves from Trump” after the resounding declaration of dissatisfaction voters have given the president in these midterm elections.

For example, he said, “JD Vance, the vice president, opposed the war and has done so from the start.” Last week, Vance sent a letter to Iran’s security chief in a reported effort to revive talks and avert further attacks.

However, Boys said the fact that Trump no longer faces election himself because the US Constitution requires him to leave office at the end of his second term may make the president instead feel that “he is now unleashed to act with impunity on the world stage”.

“Indeed, given his weakened domestic political circumstances, one can easily imagine Donald Trump escalating policies in the Middle East as he seeks to establish an indisputable presidential legacy,” he added.

Last year, Trump unleashed tariffs of 10 percent to 50 percent on about 90 countries with the aim of shrinking the US trade deficit. The move, which rattled global markets, was struck down by the Supreme Court in February, but the Trump administration has found workarounds to that ruling.

Days after the Supreme Court’s 6-3 vote, Trump doubled down on the tariffs, quickly signing an executive order based on a different statute and raising them from 10 percent to 15 percent. However, those tariffs are limited to 150 days unless they are extended by Congress.

Democrats have blamed Trump’s tariffs for exacerbating the rising cost-of-living crisis as tariffs ultimately fall back on US importers who pass them on to consumers. They accused Trump of “pickpocketing the American people”. A Democratic-controlled Congress could push for exceptions that would give exporters some temporary relief, but any deeper shift in US trade policy will have to wait for the 2028 presidential election and the new occupant of the White House.

Boys said the “situation regarding tariffs remains before the United States courts” and whether the government must give back tariff money it has already collected – and exactly how much of it – remains in dispute.

The one area in which Congress is expected to follow through regardless of the flip in party control is in passing tougher restrictions on China’s access to technology. These include the Remote Access Security Act, which would extend export controls to remote access of US goods, software or technology, and the Chip Security Act, which would require companies to verify that advanced artificial intelligence chips remain in authorised locations to prevent diversion to foreign adversaries.

Lucas said one major outcome of the midterm vote is that Democrats will now assume the chairmanships of all congressional committees, which have the power to launch investigations and subpoena witnesses.

This could result in scrutiny of the Trump family’s vast business empire, the president’s ties with the late sex offender Jeffrey Epstein and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s handling of the war with Iran. They could even try to impeach Trump for a third time over the conflict.

Congress also may – and plans to – launch extensive investigations into the Trump administration’s immigration policies. Democrats have repeatedly advocated for using their new subpoena power to scrutinise collaboration between the US tax agency, the Internal Revenue Service, and the Department of Homeland Security on the Trump administration’s policy of deporting refugees and migrants.

Detaining people at airports and hearings in their immigration cases became part of Trump’s efforts to carry out mass deportations – one of his 2024 campaign promises. In the lead-up to Tuesday’s elections, Fox News released a poll that found 53 percent of registered voters felt the immigration crackdown had “gone too far”.

The Homeland Security Department has argued that about 70 percent of its immigration detainees are criminals, if pending charges are factored in. But critics have questioned that statistic, given that the legal system in the US gives suspects the presumption of innocence until they are proven guilty in a court of law.

Lucas said the midterm vote means “Democrats and responsible Republicans can now reach out to the state and local communities and encourage them to pursue recovery and responsible policies.”
