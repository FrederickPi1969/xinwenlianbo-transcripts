Lawmakers in Paraguay have resumed impeachment proceedings against President Santiago Pena, a day after he resigned amid links to the country’s worst financial crisis in decades.

The Senate convened a rare session on Friday to consider Pena’s political future, opting to move forward with efforts to remove the conservative economist from office.

Politicians formally filed impeachment charges against the president on Thursday, despite his resignation. Pena, who was elected in 2023, has requested a 72-hour extension to prepare his defence before the Senate.

The proceedings follow a vote in Paraguay’s lower house of Congress, where lawmakers overwhelmingly approved impeachment charges against Pena in a 78-0 vote.

The political turmoil surrounding the country’s president follows Paraguay’s central bank’s intervention earlier this week at Ueno Bank, one of the country’s fastest-growing digital lenders, citing breaches of minimum solvency requirements.

Pena previously held a stake in Ueno’s parent company, Grupo Vazquez, until last year, when he claims he sold his shares. Paraguayan lawmakers have accused the president of a conflict of interest and questioned whether authorities failed to act on warnings about the lender’s financial position.

The collapse of Ueno, which held $850m in public funds, has left more than half of Paraguay’s population without access to their accounts, drawing intense scrutiny over the government’s relationship with the bank and financial regulators’ effectiveness.

“My greatest wish is that the return of those funds be guaranteed, that the deposit guarantee fund fulfil its purpose, and that public funds — as well as the resources that belong to all Paraguayans — be protected,” Pena said in a Thursday video address where he apologised for failing to prevent the crisis.

The crisis has also prompted the resignation of Paraguay’s central bank chief, Carlos Carvallo, and arrest warrants for eight Ueno executives, who later turned themselves in.

Paraguay’s attorney general has ordered three criminal investigations into the bank’s collapse, including its regulatory oversight and the placement of public funds in the institution.

If Pena is convicted in an impeachment trial, he would lose the lifetime Senate seat and associated legal protections ordinarily granted to former Paraguayan presidents.

## Why is Denmark separating ‘Western’ and ‘non-Western’ citizens?

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoAn outside view of asylum center Sandholm in Allerod, Denmark [File: Sylvest Andersen/Getty]By Nils Adler and AFPPublished On 6 Nov 20266 Nov 2026Seven families from Helsingor, a coastal city north of Copenhagen, are facing their former landlord in Denmark’s Supreme Court.

In a two-day hearing that began on Friday, they are arguing they were forced from their homes because of where they, or their parents, come from.

Their leases were terminated in 2022 under a 2018 law, once known as the “ghetto law”, which targets social housing estates where more than half of residents are classed as “non-Western”. The government claims the law is aimed at integration. But rights groups have for years fought against the measures, saying they are discriminatory.

“It’s about whether the criteria regarding the tenants’ origin is neutral or not,” Eddie Khawaja, the families’ lawyer, told the news agency AFP.

So what does “non-Western” mean, and could the courts bring the law down? Here’s what you need to know:

In 2020, the UN Human Rights Office said: “Statistics Denmark, under the Danish Ministry for Economic and Interior Affairs, defines ‘non-Western’ as any country outside the EU, with the exceptions of Andorra, Australia, Canada, Iceland, Liechtenstein, Monaco, New Zealand, Norway, San Marino, Switzerland, the USA, and the Vatican State.

“The effect is that ‘non-Western’ disproportionately means Denmark’s non-white, non-European ethnic populations,” it stated.
The label also covers descendants: anyone born in Denmark if neither parent is a Danish citizen born in the country.

These are social housing areas of at least 1,000 people where more than 50 percent are “non-Western” immigrants and descendants.

An area must also meet two of four criteria on unemployment, crime, education, and income.

Areas on the list for five years become “transformation areas”, which the government used to call “hard ghettos”.

They must cut public family housing to 40 percent by 2030 through sales, demolition or conversion.

An estate with the same social problems but fewer “non-Western” residents faces no such obligation.

The law was part of a plan called “One Denmark without Parallel Societies: No Ghettos in 2030”.

In 2021, Kaare Dybvad Bek, then interior minister, said too many non-Western foreigners in one area “increases the risk of an emergence of religious and cultural parallel societies”.

About 11,000 people across Denmark will have to leave their homes by 2030, according to tenants’ associations.

In Helsingor, the landlord Boliggarden announced in early 2020 that it would renovate 96 apartments at the Nojsomhed estate. It then terminated the tenants’ leases. Most of those tenants were of foreign origin.

“They didn’t want the estate to be labelled ‘hard ghetto’ so they wanted to forcibly move us out when we advocated for other solutions,” Riad, a 33-year-old plaintiff who did not want his family name published, told AFP.

The law may be discriminatory, but Danish judges ultimately make the final decision about whether it violated Danish laws prohibiting ethnic discrimination.

The Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) ruled in December 2025, in cases brought by tenants from Mjolnerparken in Copenhagen and Schackenborgvaenge in Slagelse. It said nationality or country of birth alone does not determine ethnic origin, but an allegedly neutral criterion covering many origins could be linked to ethnicity.

That runs contrary to the Danish government’s position and the reasoning of the lower Danish courts in the Helsingor case, which found that the “non-Western” does not describe an ethnic group and therefore did not result in discrimination that violated Denmark’s Equal Treatment Act.

Danish judges must decide whether the “non-Western” criterion really rests on residents’ ethnic origin, the court said, and consequently, if people included in the criterion are afforded less favourable treatment.

Less favourable treatment, it noted, could include “an increased risk of early termination of their leases”.

In January, Sophie Haestorp Andersen, then minister for social affairs and housing, told a parliamentary hearing that authorities must keep applying the law until the Eastern High Court rules, the Danish outlet Danske Kommuner reported.

The four-party coalition in power since June includes the Green Left (SF), which voted for the law in 2018, according to the public broadcaster DR. The party’s Jacob Mark is now minister for children, elderly affairs and housing.

No date has been officially set for the verdict, but reports suggest it could be handed down later this month.

If the families win, they would probably be rehoused in homes similar to the ones they had to leave.

## Veto or deals: Can Trump find a way to work with Democrats?

Trump has three options to affect legislation, say analysts: veto legislation, enforce executive orders or make deals.

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoA hat reading 'VETO' sits on the Resolute Desk as US President Donald Trump speaks in the Oval Office of the White House, Washington, DC, on November 5, 2026 [AFP]By Edna MohamedPublished On 6 Nov 20266 Nov 2026As the United States midterm election results landed late on Tuesday night, United States President Donald Trump took a phone call from the news outlet Politico.

A wave for the Democratic Party had sunk Trump’s Republican Party. For the first time in his two stints as president, Trump’s Republican Party no longer controlled either chamber of Congress.

But in the phone interview, Trump put on a brave face — and offered an olive branch: He said that he would be willing to work with Democrats.

“They’ve already called, and they want to work with me. I have the right to veto everything, but I don’t want to be just sitting there for two years just vetoing everything,” he said.

While a handful of seats remain to be counted, the Democrats have won comfortable majorities in both the House of Representatives, where they have secured at least 253 seats out of 435, and in the Senate, where they have won at least 54 out of 100.

Trump has since posted a picture on his Truth Social account using the Make America Great Again red baseball hat to read “VETO”. Despite the Democrats’ success, as president, Trump holds broad executive authority, with Article 1, Section 7 of the US Constitution stipulating that the president has the power to reject legislation passed by Congress through a veto.

Congress can override the veto, but would need to have a two-thirds majority, something that the Democrats do not have in either chamber.

But Trump’s comments to Politico underscore an alternate possibility — the president may pick and choose when to veto, and when to work with Democrats.

As it stands, Trump has three options: veto legislation, enforce executive orders or make deals.

Since Trump was inaugurated in January 2025, he has passed 292 executive orders: 67 this year and 225 last year, according to the Federal Register.

But now, according to Andrew Proctor, a lecturer in US politics at University College London, Trump has even less leverage than before with Congress.

“I think the biggest thing is we’re probably going to see a decent amount of gridlock. The Democrats are going to try to pass policies, but he’s got the power to veto them, and Democrats don’t have enough seats in either chamber to overcome any vetoes that he has,” Proctor said.

“So that raises questions about, will he try to work with the Democrats at all on passing legislation? I think recent history says that’s unlikely.”

Still, some Democrats have said that they would work with the president to advance programmes central to their campaign promises.

Michigan’s Abdul El-Sayed, the first Muslim elected to the US Senate, has already said he could work with Trump  — even though the US president had used racist and Islamophobic rhetoric to target El-Sayed during the campaign.

El-Sayed told MSNOW on Thursday that if Trump was interested in “getting money out of politics, putting money in pockets and passing Medicare for All, that’s what I ran to do, and I’m looking forward to working with anyone who is willing to do that”.

“He is the president of the United States,” he said. “I’m going to be one United States senator, and if he’s interested in working on issues that I want to work on – getting more people healthcare, ending the Medicare cuts, ending the stupid war, stopping these stupid tariffs with Canada – then let’s talk about doing that and go and deliver for the public.”

Progressive Senator Bernie Sanders has also said, in recent interviews, that he would not rule out Trump signing onto a Medicare for All bill if Democrats pass it in both chambers of Congress. He described Trump as a “politician” who was not “too ideological”.

Proctor argued that Trump could indeed make a deal with the Democrats based on how good it could make him look, but that getting Medicare for All passed in the next two years appeared unlikely.

“I do think that a place where common ground could emerge on healthcare is rolling back, specifically the sort of subsidies that were cut” under Trump’s earlier legislation, he said. “So that would make healthcare more affordable for millions of Americans.”

Proctor pointed out that some Republicans in 2025 had said they were willing to negotiate with Democrats on healthcare reforms.

“That didn’t end up happening, but I could imagine that might be a place for healthcare-specific policy where we could see something emerge,” he said. “I don’t think that a broader healthcare debate on passing Medicare for All, though, is really in the cards.”

Today, that warning carries a devastating weight. Children born under Houthi rule are now being recruited to fight. Schools and summer camps have been used for indoctrination and military training, exposing young people to recruitment when they should be receiving an education and building a future. These practices were documented by the United Nations Panel of Experts on Yemen and the US Department of Labor.

A childhood consumed by fear cannot simply be restored when the guns fall silent. The damage reaches into education, livelihoods, relationships, and the ability to imagine a life beyond war. UNICEF describes the profound effects of disrupted schooling on children’s learning, emotional development, and mental health.

This is why waiting for an uprising against the Houthis has never been a sufficient strategy. People living under coercion face consequences that distant observers do not. Silence under repression cannot be treated as consent, nor can survival be mistaken for allegiance.

But the consequences of allowing Houthi power to grow extend far beyond the people living under its control. The recent attacks across Aden, Hadramout, Shabwa, and Saudi Arabia show how urgently international policy must confront this threat.

In Aden, the October 15 attack on Maashiq Palace brought explosions and renewed fear to the seat of Yemen’s internationally recognised government. A subsequent reported attack on Aden’s airport on October 21 further underscored the vulnerability of a city whose residents need dependable access to transport and essential services.

For the people of Aden, renewed bombardment carries the memory of earlier battles alongside the anxiety of what comes next. A population struggling to rebuild should not repeatedly be forced to organise daily life around the possibility of another missile.

In Hadramout, reports on October 21 described strikes on Al-Rayyan International Airport in Mukalla, with no injuries initially reported. The AFP news agency also reported that a military base at the airport was hit. The presence of military facilities does not diminish the importance of protecting civilians and the infrastructure on which they depend. Airports connect people to medical treatment, employment, relatives, and the outside world.

In Shabwa, the October 19 missile attack hit the Al-Alam training camp for security forces, causing mass casualties. The Reuters news agency reported dozens killed and more than 200 injured, citing the governor. The strike dealt a devastating blow to personnel responsible for maintaining local security, with losses felt far beyond the camp, in the communities mourning their dead and caring for the wounded.

Across the border, attacks on Saudi airports have killed civilians and disrupted international travel. The October 10 strike on Riyadh’s King Khalid International Airport killed 13 people. Travellers of different nationalities have been exposed to a conflict they did not choose. The danger already extends beyond Yemen’s borders.

Together, these attacks reveal the failure of an approach that has not sufficiently constrained Houthi capabilities. Diplomatic activity cannot be measured by meetings held or assurances received while missiles continue to reach cities, airports, and vital infrastructure.

The record also demands clarity about the Houthis’ terrorist designations. Yemen’s National Defence Council designated the group a terrorist organisation in October 2022, and the United States designated it a Foreign Terrorist Organization, effective March 5, 2025. In my judgement, their documented conduct amply warrants those decisions.

Other governments should take those designations and the evidence behind them seriously. Engagement must carry conditions, verification, and consequences. The Houthis signed a UN action plan in April 2022 to end grave violations against children, including recruitment. Subsequent reporting by Human Rights Watch documented continued child recruitment. Commitments must therefore be judged by their implementation, not merely by the signing of documents.

Token penalties or political rewards for terrorism would set a dangerous precedent in international relations: Violence wins concessions, and armed coercion buys political power. Other armed groups could draw the same lesson, potentially including groups closer to home for the countries failing to act.

A credible response must disrupt illicit weapons supplies and financing, strengthen civilian protection, defend maritime navigation, and impose targeted consequences on those directing and enabling abuses. Humanitarian assistance and legitimate commerce must remain protected. Civilians should never pay twice: First for Houthi repression, then for measures intended to confront it.

Protecting the next generation also requires sustained investment in education, independent information, psychosocial care, and the reintegration of children released from armed groups. UNICEF’s child protection work in Yemen includes psychosocial support and reintegration assistance. Preventing recruitment means giving children and their communities a viable future.

North and South Yemen face immediate dangers, and Saudi Arabia and international shipping have already felt the consequences. No government should assume that a threat left to grow will remain geographically contained.

Nearly eight years after my warning, another generation cannot be asked to wait. The threat is established. International policy must finally match it.

Abdesselam is confident that Khelif could win a world title “within three to five fights” and compared his fighter with Muhammad Ali.

Frequently accused of being a transgender athlete, Khelif was born female but naturally carries the SRY gene, located on the Y chromosome, that indicates masculinity.
