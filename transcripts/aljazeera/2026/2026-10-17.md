## Why are Latin American countries building closer ties with Israel?

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoPresident of Argentina Javier Milei (C) with businessman Stan Polovets (L) and Israel's President Isaac Herzog during the Genesis Prize ceremony in Jerusalem, June 12, 2025 [Lior Mizrahi/Genesis Prize Foundation/Getty]By Umar FarooqPublished On 17 Oct 202617 Oct 2026When Israeli Foreign Minister Gideon Saar announced on October 8 that Colombia would be moving its embassy to Jerusalem, he made sure to point out how important the development was for his country’s diplomatic narrative, and that it heralded warming relations with Latin American governments.

“We will work hard because this is the diplomatic battle over the truth,” Saar said. “And the truth is, Jerusalem was always the capital of one nation.”

Brazil, Saar went on to say, might soon join Colombia and the growing list of Latin American countries that had moved their embassies to Jerusalem, the disputed city claimed by Israelis and Palestinians as their capital.

Latin America’s relationship with Israel is shifting, as right-wing parties come into power in the region and pro-Israel politicians use their support for the country to rally voters.

Brazil, the largest country in the region, is in the midst of a presidential election campaign in which the relationship with Israel has become a key talking point.

President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, a left-wing ardent supporter of Palestine, is locked in a close contest with right-wing rival Senator Flavio Bolsonaro. He has sported Israeli flags at campaign rallies and played up his friendship with Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. The pair are scheduled to take part in a run-off election after Bolsonaro performed surprisingly strongly in the first -round, apparently raising hopes for a detente with Israeli officials like the foreign minister.

Last month, for instance, Colombia and Israel began allowing their citizens to travel visa-free between their countries, yet another signal the newly inaugurated government of Abelardo de la Espriella wants to restore ties that were cut in the wake of Israel’s war on Gaza.

Experts say the realignment with Israel is driven, in part, by new leaders trying to signal they have joined a regional alignment with United States President Donald Trump. But it’s also being fuelled by a massive lobbying effort in which Evangelical Christian groups and Israeli-government-backed organisations in the region have pushed Latin American countries to support Israel.

“It’s not one factor or the other, but a convergence,” said Marta Tawil Kuri, Research Professor at the Center for International Studies at the College of Mexico. “Right-wing leaderships seeking validation from Washington, evangelical bases giving them electoral and ideological grounding, and an Israeli diplomatic offensive that finds fertile ground in Latin America precisely as its image deteriorates elsewhere.”

Colombia’s last leader, Gustavo Petro, severed ties with Israel over what he said was the genocidal war on Gaza. He stopped exporting Colombian coal to Israel, cut military and security cooperation and planned to open a Colombian embassy to Palestine. The former left-wing fighter seemed to take every public opportunity to criticise Israel, even using a trip to New York City for a United Nations summit to take part in a protest against Trump and the war on Gaza.

His successor, de la Espriella, has rapidly reversed Bogota’s stance on Israel since coming to power in August, announcing plans to move his country’s embassy from Tel Aviv to Jerusalem, re-appoint ambassadors and even recognising Israeli control over the occupied Syrian territory of the Golan Heights.

Strong presidential systems and weak, unprofessional diplomatic corps allow new heads of state to quickly pivot on issues such as ties with Israel, said Kuri.

“Unlike the US, where support for Israel has stayed relatively stable across administrations thanks to more consolidated institutions and bipartisan consensus, in much of Latin America foreign policy can swing sharply from one election to the next because that institutional buffer doesn’t exist,” Kuri said.

In 2023, Argentina elected right-wing populist Javier Milei as president, who pledged to move the embassy to Jerusalem, refused to recognise a Palestinian state at the UN and said he was “proud to be the most Zionist president in the world.”

In Bolivia, the leftist administration of Evo Morales, who was president from 2006 to 2019, had refused to have full diplomatic ties with Israel. This continued under the presidency of Luis Arce, who joined other regional left-wing leaders in recalling their ambassadors from Israel after October 7, 2023. When Arce was voted out last year, his replacement Rodrigo Paz promptly reversed that policy.

Soon after coming to office this August, Chilean President Jose Antonio Kast undid his leftist predecessor’s actions against Israel as well, appointing an ambassador to Israel who describes himself as a “proud Zionist”.

Over in Honduras, the left-wing government was replaced by a right-wing leader this January. New President Nasry Asfura, the grandson of Palestinian immigrants from Bethlehem, similarly reappointed an ambassador to Israel this year.

In August, Venezuela announced it would restore consular relations with Israel, undoing nearly two decades of an antagonistic stance towards Israel under previous leaders Nicolas Maduro and Hugo Chavez.

Besides deciding where to put their embassies, Latin American governments have also sometimes lent support to legal efforts against Israel over the Gaza war. Mexico, Bolivia, Colombia, Brazil, Chile, Belize, Cuba and Nicaragua are among governments that at one point backed South Africa’s genocide case against Israel at the International Court of Justice.

When Flavio Bolsonaro launched his Brazilian presidential campaign last July, he did so standing beside Argentinian President Milei. Some in the crowd waved Israeli flags and watched as Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu delivered a video message calling Bolsonaro a friend and a “great champion of the friendship between our peoples”.

Giving foreign leaders such a prominent role in an election campaign is “unheard of in Latin America,” said Alexander Main, Director of International Policy at the Washington-DC-based Center for Economic and Policy Research. “This did not happen before, at least not so overtly.”

Bolsonaro’s campaign, experts say, is seeking to make itself part of a larger political camp that is led in the hemisphere by Trump and internationally includes Israel. Some received direct endorsements from Trump, such as Colombia’s de la Espriella and Honduras’s Asfura.

They’ve joined new right-wing umbrella groups led by the US, such as the Shield of the Americas, a counter-narcotics security alliance bringing together Argentina, Bolivia, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, the Dominican Republic, Ecuador, El Salvador, Guyana, Honduras, Panama, Paraguay, Peru and Trinidad and Tobago. The effort is part of Trump’s policy for the Western Hemisphere that seeks to keep out the influence of other global powers like China.

Members of this Trump-led bloc have helped facilitate controversial US policies to deter migration, such as El Salvador, which is housing third-country deportees for the US. They’ve also chosen to frame their war on narcotics as a new kind of war on terrorism, a zero-sum conflict that is inspired by Israel’s war on the Palestinians, according to Main.

“Before, you had left-wing politicians in these regions that have seen what has happened in Gaza and called that war crimes and human rights abuses,” Main said. “Now you’ve seen, on the other hand, sectors seeing this as an existential fight against a deep-rooted enemy and that merits a kind of war on terror framing.”

Having good relations with Israel could translate into access to key military and security resources as well, Main said, including the use of sophisticated spy software such as Pegasus – which was developed by an Israeli software company – and sales of military jets or training of forces.

The rally in August in Brazil that featured Netanyahu’s video message was also an attempt to win support from a large and influential voter bloc there who see protecting Israel as part of their religious worldview.

The Evangelical Parliamentary Front, a caucus of pro-Israel evangelical legislators, includes more than 40% of the Chamber of Deputies and more than a quarter of the Senate. If they formed a party, the loose alliance would become the third-largest in the country.

“Those constituencies have become increasingly politicised in recent years, to the point it is beneficial for politicians to court them and one of the ways they can do that is rallying behind Israel,” Main said.

“Evangelicalism — particularly Christian Zionism — is a real and growing factor in countries like Honduras and Guatemala,” Kuri said.

Three of the new cases were among health workers who treated the country’s first patient, Kenya’s Ministry of Health said in a statement posted on X on Saturday.

Kenya’s cases are linked to the Ebola epidemic in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), which has recorded more than 9,000 cases and more than 4,300 deaths since it was officially detected in May.

Kenya’s index patient died on October 5 after being ill in the DRC for a month and then flying to Nairobi on October 3. The cases are the first known infections ⁠passed on inside Kenya.

Of the first patient’s 130 contacts, the Health Ministry said 128, or 98.5 percent, had been quarantined – above the global benchmark of 85 percent to break the chain of transmission.

Kenya has reacted quickly by strengthening screening and establishing a hotline for anyone who suspects they may have been in contact with an Ebola patient, but some observers have questioned how the index patient managed to bypass health screenings upon returning to Kenya and make contact with so many people.

Ebola is highly contagious and spreads through direct contact with the bodily fluids of an infected person, including blood and vomit, or through contact with contaminated materials and surfaces, such as bedding and clothing.

The dominant strain in this outbreak is the Bundibugyo strain, for which there are currently no vaccines or treatments. This has complicated efforts to control the spread of the disease.

Research is under way to develop one or more vaccines that work against the strain, and some of these vaccines are already in the early phases of testing in humans.

Protesters gathered along Habib Bourguiba Avenue, a major street in central Tunis, as hundreds of police deployed across the capital. Several surrounding roads were closed.

The crowd included lawyers, doctors, journalists and the unemployed, as well as activists and members of parties from across the political spectrum. They demanded the release of detained government critics and an end to restrictions on trade union rights.

Placards reading “No democracy without freedoms” and “No dignity with poverty” reflected the political and economic grievances driving the demonstration.

Protesters also revived a slogan demanding the government’s removal, widely heard during Tunisia’s 2011 uprising.

Addressing the rally on Saturday, UGTT leader Salah Eddine Salmi criticised the concentration of political power in the presidency and said “silence will no longer be acceptable”.

The political demands came alongside mounting frustration over the cost of basic goods and declining purchasing power.

Tunisia has struggled with prolonged economic difficulties, deteriorating public services, recurring water and electricity cuts, as well as shortages of medicines and other essentials.

Demonstrator Yassine Gharseli, who travelled from Sfax, about 250km south of Tunis, said repeated disruptions to basic services had deepened his frustration with the authorities.

On August 20 , protesters in Tunis rallied against rising prices and shortages of basic goods.

The demands recall the uprising that ousted longtime President Zine El Abidine Ben Ali in 2011, which helped ignite protests across the Arab world.

Tunisia subsequently held competitive elections and expanded political freedoms, but unemployment and struggling public services remain persistent sources of anger.
