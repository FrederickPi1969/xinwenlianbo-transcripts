## Tenerife’s Jesus De Miguel honoured in tearful tribute by teammates, fans

A bouquet of flowers marked the spot on the pitch where Jesus De Miguel fell five days earlier. His teammates returned to the stadium dressed in all black. Above them hung a banner that read “Eterno Demi 9” – Demi 9 forever – a reference to the number nine’s nickname.

In the stands, supporters chanted the name they had once roared to celebrate his goals for CD Tenerife. On Saturday, it was a wail to bid him goodbye.

Thousands gathered at the Heliodoro Rodriguez Lopez stadium in Santa Cruz, the capital of Tenerife in the Spanish Canary Islands, where the forward suffered a collision on Monday that resulted in his tragic death. Silence gave way to applause before the club’s centenary anthem was played.

Thundering applause greeted De Miguel’s parents, sister, wife and daughter – a family coping with its loss publicly, but alongside thousands who had gathered to share their grief.

Addressing the bereaved family, Gallego promised that De Miguel’s 18-month-old daughter, Carla, would grow up knowing what her father had meant to those around him.

The solidarity erased the lines of football rivalry across the islands as Ultra Naciente, a supporters’ group from UD Las Palmas in the Canary Islands, placed a wreath on the pitch before the ceremony.

For Jose Diaz Muros, president of Tenerife supporters’ group Pena Iberica, the loss felt personal.

“When a player becomes part of your team, he also becomes part of your life: the conversations at home, the weekends and the memories you share with your family,” he told Al Jazeera.

De Miguel’s death was confirmed on Tuesday, hours after he was taken to hospital following a collision with Andorra goalkeeper Jesus Owono during Tenerife’s second-division match.

The tragedy has not only brought together football clubs all over Spain, but also raised questions about the response by Spanish football authorities to requests by players for a grieving period.

The Spanish players’ union, AFE, said footballers consulted across the categories unanimously favoured postponement because they did not feel emotionally ready to compete.

Las Palmas defender Alex Suarez expressed that distress publicly: “My head is not in football”.

Three Segunda fixtures were postponed, alongside a women’s match involving Costa Adeje Tenerife, but there was no blanket suspension of games.

The federation’s competition judge ruled AFE’s request inadmissible, saying the decision belonged to La Liga and Spanish football’s top division did not postpone its fixtures.

The governing bodies organised commemorative shirts, floral offerings, pre-match silence and pauses from the ninth minute, honouring De Miguel’s shirt number. Their joint statement presented the tributes as a way to remember him with dignity and respect.

“There was recognition of the grief, but the collective request was not met in full,” Albert Ortega, a Spanish football expert, told Al Jazeera.

Ortega believes that players’ emotional wellbeing still has less power to alter the calendar than institutional statements suggest. But he cautioned against assuming commercial interests alone explained the decision.

“We cannot demonstrate that money was the only reason for the decision. We can demand transparency about the criteria and the weight given to their views.”

Tenerife fan Diaz Muros acknowledged the difficulties of suspending fixtures, but said players’ concerns should have been addressed.

In Tenerife, questions were also raised at the resumption of action while an investigation into the collision and emergency response, including treatment and evacuation, were ongoing.

Santa Cruz de Tenerife’s chief prosecutor, Jose Luis Sanchez-Jauregui, told the Spanish news agency EFE that criminal proceedings over the collision seemed unlikely. He cited the distinction between injuries arising during sporting play and deliberate aggression.

Meanwhile, the Spanish football federation’s disciplinary committee has deferred its decision on goalkeeper Owono, but EFE reported a potential suspension of up to 12 matches.

Spanish football expert Ortega argued that prevention of such incidents requires attention that goes beyond individual responsibility.

“Having a written protocol is the starting point,” he said. “Its effectiveness is demonstrated when the people who must implement it know their roles and can act in a coordinated way.”

He proposed independent reviews, regular emergency drills and checks on each stadium’s capacity to respond as possible measures to help prevent such tragedies.

The clash happened during a second division professional football match but questions still remain about procedures in lower division games, where provision of resources and medical response predate De Miguel’s death.

After Colindres goalkeeper Raul Ramirez suffered a fatal injury in the Spanish fifth division in September last year, AFE challenged the provisions in the lower tiers. The ground where he was injured had a defibrillator, but did not have a doctor or ambulance on site.

## US task force to cut off Iran’s transport of advanced weapons: What we know

With it, the US military ⁠⁠⁠⁠is trying to prevent Iran from using ground routes to transport advanced weapons and other materials, the United States Central Command (CENTCOM) announced on Saturday.

The same day, the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) announced ambitious plans for underground complexes capable of sustaining missile and drone production under bombardment. That announcement came amid reports of ongoing construction near Iranian missile bases, based on satellite imagery and previous claims by Tehran that US strikes failed to destroy its underground facilities.

Task Force Iron Gate will aim to find out where “weapons systems are coming in, how they could potentially be cut off” and how “Iran is transporting its energy”, Michael Mulroy, a former US deputy assistant defence secretary for the Middle East, told Al Jazeera.

It expands on the US blockade of Iranian ports to cover Iran’s land borders and the Caspian Sea and will reportedly be run by 20 civilian and military personnel.

Iran shares borders with seven countries – Afghanistan, Armenia, Azerbaijan, Iraq, Pakistan Turkiye and Turkmenistan.

Based in Tampa, Florida, the unit will coordinate with other US government agencies “on preventing the movement of advanced weapons systems, dual-use technologies, sanctioned materials and illegal revenue streams across Iran’s land borders and the Caspian Sea”, CENTCOM said. Task Force Iron Gate will reinforce “the success of our ironclad blockade against Iran at sea”, CENTCOM Commander Admiral Brad Cooper said.

If US President Donald Trump ramps up the war, there will likely be attacks on this “series of targets”, said Mulroy.

“Unlike Operation Epic Fury, which primarily (focused) on military targets, I think these targets will be focused on economic and infrastructure targets, as part of this comprehensive plan to cut the regime off from its ability to fight and ability to fund itself,” Mulroy said. “It’s primarily a coordination body that’s looking for how the US can increase its economic strangulation strategy by going after the ground routes as they’ve already covered a majority of the maritime routes.”

“The Americans cannot achieve their goal through this, which is to apply severe economic pressure to collapse and disintegrate the Iranian economy,” said IRGC spokesman Sardar Mohebi, as reported by the Fars news agency.

Washington previously “claimed that Iran will suffer an economic collapse within two weeks, but we witnessed that this did not happen”, he added.

While CENTCOM’s announcement did not explicitly identify Iran’s weapons trade with Russia, analysts say the unit could be seen as an attempt to disrupt supply networks that include Russian connections.

US media have reported that Russia has sent drone components and explosives to Iran via the Caspian Sea. There are also claims that Russian technology could be helping Iran develop advanced supersonic cruise missiles.

Mahjoob Zweiri, a professor of contemporary history and politics at Qatar University, told Al Jazeera that reports of military equipment imported via Russia are a “real concern” for the United States.

Rami Khouri, a public policy fellow at the American University of Beirut and a non-resident senior fellow at the Arab Center in Washington, said of the new unit: “The US really doesn’t quite know what to do about Iran as we’ve seen, and it’s doing things off the cuff” – a strategy that has led to little success.

Khouri pointed to Iran’s history as a trading nation for thousands of years as evidence that the US mission is near impossible.

“There’s an enormous web of contacts that Iran has had for thousands of years with traders and partners and allies all over the region, especially in Asia –  it’s virtually impossible for the US to seal off every one,” he said.

While the US can impose a blockade or sanctions on Iran, it cannot control the behaviour of everyone from the Russians, to the Chinese, to the Emiratis and beyond, said the analyst.

“There are people all over the region who have links with Iran, some very close, some just commercial,” he said.

A lot of the materials that go into Iran’s weapons systems are bought from some other countries, – some even from the US or Europeans – and then they are channelled through companies in Asia, Khouri added.

Former Trump aide and top counterterrorism official Joe Kent, who resigned in March due to the US’s war on Iran, said the new task force is an attempt by CENTCOM to subvert ongoing diplomatic efforts.

“This is CENTCOM inviting Iran to escalate while the vice president is attempting to negotiate with the Iranians,” Kent posted on X. “This is, as CENTCOM calls it, ‘policy by CONOP’, when the military finds a way to subvert the White House without blatantly disobeying the President, by taking actions that will ensure a war escalates or perpetuates.”

According to The Wall Street Journal, CENTCOM chief Cooper is among the many officials, alongside Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, pressing Trump to renew attacks on Iran.

The task force may also just be symbolic because the US has no military presence on the Caspian Sea, said Zweiri.

“America has no access to the sea, unless some of the neighbouring countries like Azerbaijan or Armenia or others collaborate [with them] … And they’re not collaborating on this because they don’t want to upset Russia,” he said. “It will require … troops on the ground, and this is unlikely to happen.”

Zweiri also said that the Caspian Sea’s contribution to Iran’s economy is just 11 percent, so the task force’s actions won’t have much negative impact on Tehran.

Al Jazeera could not independently verify this figure but the Caspian Sea accounts for only a fraction of Iran’s overall maritime trade, according to the Soufan Center based in New York City.

## Egypt’s reopening of its embassy in Tripoli signals regional shift on Libya

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoLibyan forces stand guard at the gate of the Egyptian embassy in Tripoli, which reopened after eight years, on November 7, 2026 [Mahmud Turkia/AFP]By Mohammad MansourPublished On 8 Nov 20268 Nov 2026The reopening of the Egyptian embassy in Tripoli marks a fundamental political shift, both domestically and regionally, for Libya, analysts say.

Egyptian Foreign Minister Badr Abdelatty inaugurated the historic embassy building on Saturday, which had been closed for 12 years due to security concerns.

Libya has been politically divided since the fall of Muammar Gaddafi in 2011, with rival administrations formed in Tripoli in the west and Tobruk and Benghazi in the east.

Egypt was widely considered to be backing the eastern authority, effectively led by strongman and renegade military commander Khalifa Haftar, and his self-styled Libyan National Army (LNA), until recent regional developments led Cairo to rethink its approach.

Libyan political analyst Hazem al-Rayes said the reopening of the embassy in Tripoli – which was the seat of the internationally recognised government that Haftar was fighting – is a sign of this.

“I can consider that what Egypt is doing in its recent moves towards the capital, Tripoli, is an attempt to readjust its behaviour in the Libyan file … [for] five years, no Egyptian foreign minister visited the capital,” he told Al Jazeera.

Elias al-Barouni, a political science professor at Libya’s Nalut University, also believes the move goes beyond the resumption of regular consular services in Tripoli.

“We are facing an indicator of a shift in Cairo’s method of managing its relations with the Libyan parties, and a rearrangement of its priorities in dealing with the Libyan crisis,” al-Barouni told Al Jazeera.

There have been several significant political and regional shifts that have somewhat stabilised the volatile situation in Libya, where thousands have been killed in fighting between militias attached to rival political authorities.

In July 2026, Ankara and the LNA reached unprecedented coordination, spearheading agreements to develop military facilities, advanced surveillance systems, and training programmes in Turkiye for eastern Libyan officers.

The deputy commander of eastern Libyan forces and Haftar’s son, Saddam Haftar, has also paid visits to regional capitals, including Turkiye and Qatar, which have supported the United Nations-recognised Government of National Accord (GNA) in Tripoli.

In Libya, rivals recently signed the so-called “4+4 agreement” that sets out a roadmap for possible presidential and legislative elections over the next two years.

Badr Hassan al-Shafei, a professor of African studies and conflict resolution at Cairo University, told Al Jazeera that Egypt came to revisit its policy towards Libya after realising that its reliance on eastern forces, led by Haftar, was not conducive to its overall strategic interests.

“It is clear that Cairo … found that relying on one party or leaning towards one party in Libya, which is Haftar’s party, may not be the right bet perhaps at the present time or even for a while, since the Turkish intervention several years ago,” al-Shafei told Al Jazeera.

Egypt has also become more concerned about developments to the south of its border, particularly the expansion of the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) in Sudan, which had allegedly received support from Haftar.

“Today, Egypt is no longer the Egypt of five years ago when its policies and relations were limited only to the [eastern] authorities in Benghazi,” al-Rayes told Al Jazeera.

It is “convinced today that its ally [LNA], which it supported throughout the past years at the expense of the legitimate government in Tripoli … has distanced itself from it today, and it needs to readjust its political behaviour with Tripoli and with the legitimate government”, he said.

During Abdelatty’s visit to Tripoli, he discussed critical economic files with Libyan officials, including the 2019 maritime border memorandum of understanding signed between Tripoli and Ankara.

According to the Facebook page of the Egyptian Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Abdelatty reaffirmed the importance of addressing Egyptian concerns regarding maritime demarcation between Libya and Turkiye in light of international law. The eastern parliament recently ratified the maritime agreement, which it initially opposed.

“Consequently, perhaps Cairo is trying to prevent the ratification of this agreement from causing damage to its interests in gas and oil exploration in the Eastern Mediterranean, and from that it wanted to extend bridges to Tripoli as well,” al-Shafei said.

“Instead of limiting itself to political and diplomatic objection from outside Tripoli, Cairo has become negotiating directly with the Libyan government that signed the agreement.”

Egypt might also be attracted to opportunities that reconstruction and the revival of Libya’s oil industry might bring.

“Perhaps with the reconstruction process, the incoming Egyptian labour will have priority, or even the currently present one will have its living conditions improved, not to mention the idea of the possibility of Egyptian investments and companies entering the reconstruction process,” al-Shafei said.

After years of backing rival sides, a new regional consensus means there is a greater likelihood of rivals in Libya reaching a comprehensive agreement to end the war, al-Rayes told Al Jazeera.

But al-Barouni cautioned that there is still a plethora of issues facing Libya, and only a comprehensive solution can end the divisions within Libya.

“The Libyan problem is not merely a dispute over the name of the head of government, but rather it is a dispute over the nature of power, the distribution of influence, control over institutions and resources, the future of the military institution, and the conditions for reaching elections,” al-Barouni said.

“If Cairo, Ankara, Washington, and other capitals agree on the necessity of ending the division, that is a development that can be built upon, but the success of any settlement remains linked to the ability of the Libyans to formulate an independent national project. Libya needs a settlement that ends the division, not a deal that redistributes the division.”

## Could Iraq’s Kurdish region return to two separate administrations?

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoJalal Talabani welcomes Masoud Barzani (R), head of the Kurdistan Democratic Party (KDP), as he arrives at a meeting for the Iraqi political leaders on February 2, 2006 in Baghdad [Wathiq Khuzaie/Getty Images]By Sherko AbdullaPublished On 8 Nov 20268 Nov 2026Disagreements between the Kurdistan Democratic Party (KDP) and the Patriotic Union of Kurdistan (PUK) over the formation of a new government have led to the threat of two rival administrations emerging in the Kurdish region of northern Iraq, analysts say.

The dispute between the two dominant political forces in the semiautonomous Kurdistan Regional Government, which traditionally has operated under a power-sharing agreement, escalated after a parliamentary session was held last week in Erbil without the PUK’s participation.

PUK leader Bafel Talabani responded by withdrawing his party from the government, and a threat is growing that a rival administration could be formed in the party’s Sulaimaniyah base.

Independent Kurdish politician Yasin Aziz said the latest developments have deepened the historical divisions between the KDP and PUK.

“I believe the spectre of two administrations in Kurdistan is now very close, and the outlines of such a scenario are becoming clear. … The events have been painful and rapid. The differences between the two parties were significant and have now led to a breakdown in relations,” Aziz told Al Jazeera.

“There had been hopes that the two sides would reach an agreement, but activating parliament without the PUK deepened the divisions between the KDP and PUK.”

The KDP, which is led by Massoud Barzani, is the biggest party in the region, and its strongholds are in Erbil and Dohuk.

Since the first Gulf War in 1990 and 1991 and the establishment of a US-led “no-fly zone” in northern Iraq, Iraq’s Kurds have largely governed themselves, separate from the national government in Baghdad.

The Iraqi Constitution enacted after the 2003 US-led invasion of Iraq recognised the northern Kurdish region as a federal entity with its own legislative, executive and judicial powers.

There is a historical precedent for the possible separation of powers in this region between two rival authorities. When a conflict erupted between elements of the KDP and PUK in the 1990s, the two parties formed separate local governments in Erbil and Sulaimaniyah, respectively.

While there have been no recent violent flare-ups between the two parties, political distrust remains deep.

During the sixth parliamentary elections in the region on October 20, 2024, the KDP won 39 seats and the PUK 23. This left a 16-seat gap between the two parties.

But the KDP’s ability to form a new government was still dependent on reaching an agreement with the PUK and other parties, such as the New Generation Movement, which secured 15 seats in the last election.

The PUK has so far refused to collaborate, so after a two-year wait, the KDP reactivated the regional parliament on Wednesday without its rivals.

Although the rivalry between the two parties is longstanding, Aziz argued that the current crisis is more serious and deeply rooted than previous political disputes.

“Given the political stubbornness of both parties, I do not expect an agreement or understanding to be reached soon,” he said. “Both sides must make concessions to each other. The party willing to make greater concessions will demonstrate greater concern for the Kurdistan region.”

“The responsibility for deepening this conflict lies with the leaderships of Kurdistan’s two main parties,” he said. “Their failure to recognise the risks posed by continuing these disputes is one of the reasons we have reached this point.”

Subhi Mandalawi, a member of the KDP, blamed the current impasse on the refusal of the PUK to form a new government with the KDP.

“Following the Kurdistan parliamentary elections, the PUK delayed the formation of the new government. They were counting on the KDP being unable to secure the legal quorum needed to convene a parliamentary session,” Mandalawi told Al Jazeera. “But we held a parliamentary session without the PUK in 2018 as well, and they subsequently joined the government and parliament.”

Mandalawi insisted that the door remains open for the PUK and other political parties but warned that prolonged divisions would lead to deeper problems for the Kurdish region.

“Continuing to obstruct legislative institutions two years after the elections is unacceptable and harms the Kurdistan region domestically, regionally and internationally,” he said.

“Threatening to divide the Kurdistan region is a hasty, ill-considered and unrealistic decision. No party leader can take such a step, and the PUK would be the biggest loser if it went ahead with it.”

Despite the differences, there is little risk of a return to conflict between the two parties. “We are brothers, and there can be no fighting among us,” Mandalawi said.

Mahmood Khoshnaw, a senior PUK official, said the KDP’s decision to convene parliament without his party has worsened political tensions in the region.

“Activating parliament without the PUK represents a departure from the principle of partnership and from political realities, and there will be consequences. If a prime minister is appointed in Erbil, we will take the same step in Sulaimaniyah. Whatever the KDP does, we will do the same in Sulaimaniyah,” Khoshnaw warned.

“The situation in the Kurdistan region is heading towards chaos unless the crisis is addressed rationally and realistically. Activating parliament without reaching an understanding with the PUK has had a negative impact on relations between the two parties.”

Despite this, there are questions about whether Sulaimaniyah could legally separate from the Kurdish regional government.

Ramadan Albadran, an independent political analyst and constitutional affairs expert, said the dispute is not a constitutional issue about the KDP’s convening of parliament but a deep political crisis with the potential to create security repercussions for the region.

The Iraqi constitution also provides no mechanism for dividing the region into zones of influence between the KDP and PUK.

“If the PUK moves to separate Sulaimaniyah from the Kurdistan region, that would not be a legal or constitutional act,” Albadran said.

## ‘Don’t bring your camera’: Filmmakers face extortion in Lagos

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoA woman sells pepper and sweet potatoes at a Lagos market, where filming in public can expose content creators and journalists to demands for payment [Sunday Alamba/AP]By Pelumi SalakoPublished On 8 Nov 20268 Nov 2026Lagos, Nigeria – Half an hour after Nurudeen Sanni and his crew began shooting a documentary in Ajah, several men surrounded them and demanded 100,000 naira (about $75) before they could continue filming.

As the crew negotiated, one of the men slapped a crew member who tried to protect the cameras. A scuffle broke out, and the men produced knives and cutlasses. The crew fled, but one member was stabbed in the neck and seriously injured, Sanni said.

“The painful part was that we saw a member of our crew being beaten and we could not do anything,” said Sanni, a 40-year-old sound recordist.

His experience illustrates the risks that filmmakers, content creators, photographers and journalists say they face while working on the streets of Lagos, Nigeria’s largest city and commercial hub.

Men locally known as agberos, a term commonly used for street touts, including some who collect levies from commercial transport operators, have been accused of demanding money from people filming in public and threatening those who refuse to pay.

In August, music video director ThankGod Omori, known professionally as TG Omori, described a night shoot in Ikoyi that was disrupted at about 3am by a crowd demanding money. He said he left the set in the hands of his assistant directors and security personnel, with one scene still unfinished. The following morning, he learned that the men had broken through a gate and injured a security guard who had refused them entry, according to Nigerian newspaper PUNCH.

Actress and content creator KieKie has also described being confronted by men after filming a short video in Obalende. She said they initially demanded one million naira (about $750), but she paid approximately 700,000 naira (about $525) after negotiations. For a separate shoot around Kara, near Berger, she said she spent close to 500,000 naira (about $375) on security.

Stanley Ikechukwu, an analyst at Lagos-based risk advisory firm SBM Intelligence, said creators regularly reported harassment and extortion. He described the situation as a “war on camera”.

Nabila Okino, an analyst at global risk advisory firm Africa Practice, said filmmakers were particularly vulnerable because abandoning a location could mean losing time and money.

“Because filmmakers often need to shoot in specific locations, those demanding money know that there is a cost to abandoning a location and starting again, which gives them leverage to extract cash,” she told Al Jazeera.

For journalists, the consequences extend beyond the cost of filming. Threats and harassment can prevent reporters from covering events and leave some communities and incidents underreported.

When Karina Adobaba-Harry covered the demolition of Makoko in January for News Central, the Nigerian television channel where she works as a camera operator, men in the area approached her crew and demanded money before they could film.

“That was funny to me because we are here to tell your story, you want the government to hear your voice … and you want us to still pay you. That sounded ridiculous to me,” she told Al Jazeera.

Adobaba-Harry said she regularly encountered men demanding payments while reporting. Some left when she explained that she had no money; others threatened to seize or damage her camera. She said reporters had sometimes paid out of their own pockets or used miscellaneous funds provided by their employer.

Ikechukwu said such incidents could affect what the public learns about events in the city.

“A reporter who is harassed or whose camera is seized while covering a flood, a fire, or a protest is not just being robbed, the public is being denied information. It creates self-censorship. Reporters will start avoiding certain areas, and the news will only come from ‘safe’ places,” he told Al Jazeera.

For small production teams, unexpected payments add to the cost of camera rental, location fees, crew wages and transport. Some also budget for security or money they may be pressured to hand over after arriving at a location.

Adobaba-Harry said she had learned to change her demeanour when confronted by men demanding money.

“Sometimes, you have to take up the persona of the agbero so that when they see that you are not as calm, they will leave you,” she said.

She said many victims did not report incidents because they doubted that complaints would lead to action.

“Even if you report, chances that you will find the guys there [are] low. It is seen as a normal thing in Lagos, except if your life is in danger. But if it is just a case of wanting to take money from you, nobody will care,” she said.

Ikechukwu said the risk of being targeted had become another consideration for filmmakers planning a shoot.

“A filmmaker has to budget for ‘settlement’ on top of camera rental, location, crew, and food. It increases cost. It kills spontaneity; you cannot just see a beautiful Lagos moment and capture it. You have to think, ‘Will I be harassed?’” he said.

Okino said authorities should work with community leaders, market and park associations and other local groups to establish clear rules for filming in public spaces and ensure harassment and extortion are not tolerated.

Foreign content creators documenting Lagos have also reported harassment. In September, Chinese travel content creator Xiao Jony shared a video of men confronting him while he was filming at a market in the city.

He said they demanded money and that, over a stretch of about 10 kilometres (6.2 miles), he encountered more than 20 checkpoints where people asked him for payment. It remains unclear who operated the checkpoints or whether they were linked to any government agency.

Okino said such experiences could discourage tourists and production companies from filming in Nigeria.

“They may decide that the cost and risk of producing content in Nigeria outweigh the benefits. At the margin, that could mean choosing neighbouring countries with more predictable and straightforward filming environments instead,” she said.

Ikechukwu said videos showing tourists being harassed could undermine efforts to promote Nigeria abroad.

“We tell the world, ‘Come to Lagos,’ but our own behaviour says, ‘Don’t bring your camera,’” he said.

If this fails, then new elections would be called.

But as things stand, Eisenkot is unlikely to be able to form a majority government of the kind Netanyahu has relied upon to force through his right-wing policy agenda.

He may have to rely upon the support of at least one of the two parties representing the ultra-Orthodox community, which have often been allies of Netanyahu. It will also be critical for him to persuade some of the more right-wing elements of his opposition bloc to get the support of Arab parties outside the government.

How far he will be able to go in this is uncertain for a man whom observers described as less comfortable with the subterfuge and political guile often attributed to Netanyahu.

Eisenkot emerged as the leader of his volatile opposition bloc just days before the October 27 elections, and even then, messaging from within the coalition appeared confused, contradictory and seemingly absent of any central coordinating figure.

So far, any support from the ultra-Orthodox United Torah Judaism Party, which has broken with its traditional ally Shas in declining to endorse Netanyahu, is likely to come in return for the continuation of the de facto shield afforded to young ultra-Orthodox men who refuse military service.

Support from parties representing Palestinians with Israeli citizenship, even if offered from outside the government, is likely to come in return for at least some meaningful effort to tackle the violent crime wave that has been allowed to engulf Palestinian communities inside Israel.

They will also want the epidemic of violent settler pogroms, which have swept through the occupied West Bank with the seemingly active encouragement of Netanyahu’s government partners – particularly far-right Ministers Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich – to stop.

But Aida Touma-Sliman, a Knesset member from the left-wing Hadash party and a member of the Palestinian-led Joint List, said developments in recent days showed that Eisenkot doesn’t want to rely on Palestinian voters.

“He appears much more willing to compromise with the ultra-Orthodox religious parties, particularly over the law governing military conscription, than risk being seen to depend on Arab support.”

One of the primary issues that whoever leads the country next will have to deal with is Israel’s standing in the United States. Israel’s reputation has fallen to an all-time low among the American public due to the genocidal war on Gaza and the US-Israel war on Iran, which Netanyahu is accused of convincing US President Donald Trump to start.

On the eve of what turned out to be disastrous midterm elections for Trump, he announced an end to the $3.8bn military aid package extended to Israel annually.

Irrespective of the last-minute announcement, Trump’s Republican Party suffered historic losses, and senior officials, including Vice President JD Vance, blamed the war on Iran.

Carnegie Endowment senior fellow Aaron David Miller acknowledged Israel’s reputation in the US was at a low ebb, one that Eisenkot – with his discomfort over the public stage and reluctance to speak in English – would need to address.

“Perceptions of Israel are fairly entrenched. Essentially, the public’s been watching the region in split screen since 2023. On one screen, there’s the carnage inflicted by Hamas on October 7 [2023], and on the other, there’s the death and destruction unleashed by Israel in response.”

“In the eyes of many, Israel is no longer David but Goliath,” he told Al Jazeera. “Ahead of Eisenkot may be the toughest US landscape any Israeli prime minister has to navigate.”

For now, at least, Eisenkot has the backing of the majority of Israelis as much for what he isn’t as for what he is.

Unlike Netanyahu, the well-polished son of a privileged Ashkenazi Jewish family who has nevertheless sought to leverage the grievances of Mizrahi Jews, Eisenkot represents a more genuine package.

Born in Tiberias to parents from Marrakesh and Casablanca, he grew up in the equally remote southern port city of Eilat before joining the army at 18, later rising to the rank of chief of staff. He then followed the well-worn path of senior Israeli military officers into politics, eventually serving in the national unity government assembled by Netanyahu after the Hamas- led attacks on southern Israel on October 7, 2023.

Described by both allies and opponents as both “menschy” (decent) and “schlumpy” (unkempt), Eisenkot’s careworn appearance reflects, at least in the eyes of some voters, his personal connections with the October 7 attacks and subsequent genocidal war on Gaza.

His son Gal was killed in Jabalia in northern Gaza in December 2023 while taking part in the war, followed a day later by his nephew Maor.

In November 2024, another nephew, Yogev Pazy, was killed in Gaza. This has created a persona around Eisenkot at odds with Netanyahu’s family, whose relatively glamorous lifestyle appears far removed from the Israeli public’s current situation.

“He seems very military in his bearing, very steady and a man you don’t always know what he’s thinking,” Yossi Mekelberg, a senior fellow at Chatham House, said. “I think he’s offering what Israel needs above all else right now: a sense of normalcy and the understanding that there are rules that apply equally and to everyone, including the government.”

But this will make little difference for Palestinians. Eisenkot is the architect of the Dahiyeh Doctrine, which advocates the use of disproportionate force and widespread destruction against civilian communities resisting Israeli force. It is far from clear if Eisenkot’s appointment as premier will make any difference to the yawning gulf in rights between Jewish and Palestinian citizens of Israel.

Eisenkot was reported to have expressed resentment at having to sit alongside such far-right figures as Ben-Gvir and Smotrich during his eight-month tenure in the national unity government. But he has nevertheless overseen punishing policies upon both Palestinians and Arabs in neighbouring states.

As chief of staff, Eisenkot oversaw brutal operations against Palestinians in Gaza and the West Bank as well as overseeing extensive air strikes against Iranian targets and arming rebels in Syria.

From 2015 to 2018, years overlapping with his tenure, United Nations figures record about 650 Palestinians killed by Israeli forces or in conflict-related incidents. The toll included 295 deaths in 2018 alone. Among them were 189 Palestinians killed and thousands wounded during the Great March of Return protests in Gaza, including 35 children.

A UN commission later found reasonable grounds to believe that Israeli forces under his command had committed serious violations of international law, some potentially amounting to war crimes or crimes against humanity.

During the 2006 war in Lebanon when Eisenkot is credited with having devised the Dahiyeh Doctrine, Israel killed 1,109 people, about 860 of whom were civilians. There are no numbers for the tens of thousands of people killed under Eisenkot’s tactic in the years since.
