## ‘Her hands are as cold as ice’: Gaza families struggle to keep warm

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoAmira Saad holds her four-month-old baby, for whom has had to borrow winter clothes to keep the infant warm [Abdelhakim Abu Riash/Al Jazeera]By Maram HumaidPublished On 12 Nov 202612 Nov 2026Gaza City, Gaza Strip – At night, as temperatures drop inside their makeshift shelter in Gaza City, Amira Saad struggles to keep her four-month-old baby, Mohammed, warm.

The baby mostly only has thin summer clothes. Unable to afford winter clothing, 27-year-old Amira has borrowed a warm garment from a neighbour, but remains worried about the cold during the night.

She lives with 18 family members, including nine children, under a tarp offering little protection from the elements. Finding enough warm clothes and blankets for everyone has become increasingly difficult.

The family was displaced from Gaza City’s Shujayea neighbourhood earlier in Israel’s genocidal war on Gaza, and now lives in a makeshift camp west of the city.

“We were flooded while we were sleeping. The water came inside and soaked our blankets, mattresses and pillows. Everything was wet,” Amira says.

The children mostly have old, torn summer clothes. Amira’s mother-in-law, 60-year-old Basima Saad, is also worried about her own 23-year-old daughter, who has a disability, and needs warm clothes and medication.

“Her hands become as cold as ice. I keep her beside me in bed to warm her, just as I would a small child,” Basima says. “I dress her before I dress myself. I have to make sure she is warm first.”

With no regular income, the family relies on community kitchens for food. Spare money for winter clothes and blankets simply is not available.

“My husband does not have a job. Two days ago, he went out to work breaking up rubble, but he broke his hand. The situation is very bad, and the cold is extremely severe,” Amira says.

The danger to the Palestinians living in these conditions, and particularly the very young, is not hypothetical.

On Thursday, a nine-month-old baby, Kinan Ahmed Raqiq, died of hypothermia. His father told AFP that he woke up in his tent to find that his son’s skin had turned blue.

She’s raising her children alone – her husband Hamza was killed by an Israeli quadcopter in January 2025, and her eldest son Haitham was killed by a sniper in November of the same year, age 25 at the time.

She is originally from Gaza City’s Zeitoun neighbourhood and has been displaced six times during the war, fleeing Israeli attacks and losing the family’s home, land, and car.

Now living in a tent, Amal struggles to provide for her children. Her younger daughters, Rimas and Malak, are still wearing summer clothes despite the arrival of winter.

Amal tries to make do with what is available, but it is not enough to meet her daughters’ needs.

Rain also seeps through the damaged tarpaulins covering the family’s tent. With no heating equipment, they rely on blankets to keep warm.

“We have no means of heating ourselves; we just cover the children with blankets,” she says.

Amal fears another storm will bring more rain and cold. “We just pray that God makes this winter easier for the people living in tents,” she says.

The United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) says 94 percent of Gaza’s population urgently needs shelter assistance. Some 65 percent of households live in tents or makeshift shelters, while 650,000 children need winter aid, including warm clothing and blankets.

By September, aid agencies had secured enough winter clothing for just 9,000 households and bedding for 50,000 – far short of what was needed. OCHA said funding shortages were further hampering preparations for winter.

The UN Children’s Fund (UNICEF) has warned that winter conditions put children in Gaza at increased risk of hypothermia and respiratory infections – at least 11 children died from hypothermia last winter, according to the Gaza Ministry of Health. The agency is seeking $97.5m for its 2026-2027 winter response, which aims to support 540,000 children and their families across Gaza and the occupied West Bank.

Recent rains have worsened the crisis. UNICEF said on November 9 that flooding had affected more than 106,000 people, while some 950,000 displaced Palestinians were living in flood-prone areas.

More than three years into Israel’s genocidal war, which has killed more than 74,600 Palestinians and wounded over 176,000, homes and livelihoods across Gaza have been devastated. Nearly two million people have been displaced, many repeatedly, and hundreds of thousands remain in tents and damaged shelters.

As the war enters its fourth year, unemployment and rising prices have made basic necessities increasingly unaffordable, leaving families struggling to protect their children from the cold.

In Gaza City’s Rimal market, 25-year-old shop owner Mohammed Abu Shanar sees the same struggle among his customers. Many ask about winter clothing prices, but leave without buying anything.

“The prices are very high. They are not within everyone’s reach. People come in, ask how much something costs, and then leave because they cannot afford it,” he says.

Mohammed sells around four to six winter tracksuits a day. He says many children from nearby displacement camps still wear short-sleeved or lightweight clothes despite the cold.

He attributes the high prices to shortages of goods entering Gaza – the result of continued Israeli restrictions – and the cost of buying stock from wholesalers.

“First, there is a shortage of goods. The wholesalers hold on to their prices, and the merchandise is expensive for us, too. If these items were selling for 20 shekels ($6.25) or 25 shekels ($7.80) instead of 40 shekels ($12.60) or 50 shekels ($15.70), perhaps people would be able to buy them,” he says.

Mohammed says he has invested more than 20,000 shekels (about $6,265) in winter stock, but after three weeks of sales he has recovered less than 5,000 shekels (about $1,570).

Mostafa Sepahvand, 41, and Farhad Javadi Manesh, 46, were convicted on Thursday following a six-week trial at Woolwich Crown Court in south London for engaging in conduct likely to assist Iran’s intelligence service in a bid to inflict violence on “subjects” in the UK.

These included journalists for Iran International, Mojtaba Pourmohsen and Kambiz Hosseini, and a founding member of UK Lawyers for Israel, Jonathan Turner. Iran International is banned by the government in Tehran.

Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) was outlawed by the UK in July under new powers to counteract state threats, with penalties of up to 14 years in prison for anyone convicted of supporting or assisting the organisation.

“This was a calculated operation to spy on those critical of the Iranian regime,” Frank Ferguson, chief prosecutor for the Crown Prosecution Service’s special crime and counterterrorism division, told reporters.

He said the pair were promised rewards to send intelligence back to Iran, while Sepahvand was willing to go beyond surveillance to commit a “violent attack” against at least one of the targets.

London’s Metropolitan Police said this was the fourth case in recent years involving direct threats to Iranian journalists in the UK and that it is “working hard to keep them safe”.

“The threat from hostile states to the UK is increasing and Iran continues to adapt its approach to using ‘proxies’,” said Vicki Evans, deputy assistant commissioner at the Met.

“We have seen examples where people who fly into the country to carry out criminal activity and then leave shortly afterwards, others where they use British nationals or, in this case, Iranian nationals with settled immigration status,” she added.

Most recently, Iran has been linked with a plot against a United States-run military airbase in England.

Sepahvand and Manesh were accused of visiting addresses linked to their targets, taking photos and videos and using platforms such as ChatGPT to research them online.

The pair will be sentenced at London’s Central Criminal Court, better known as the Old Bailey, on December 17.

Another measure attempted to reduce real estate speculation by stopping any company involved in the real estate market from buying properties for less than 70 percent of their value.

## ‘No war, no peace’: Can Iran break the deadlock with the US?

Iran continues to assert control over which vessels may pass through the Strait of Hormuz, where attacks on tankers taking “unauthorised” routes have disrupted shipping.

Ghalibaf says Tehran remains open to a deal with the US to agree a ceasefire and the reopening of the strait, and has been waiting for Washington to respond to its terms for this to happen. “The ball is now in the American court,” he said.

Faced with the prospect of restarting an unpopular war or making concessions to Iran in order to secure a deal, experts say US President Donald Trump has for now chosen to ratchet up economic pressure. To this end, Washington has tightened sanctions, maintained its blockade on Iranian ports and announced an operation that Trump says will cut off overland routes Iran uses to transport advanced weapons and technology.

For now, full-scale fighting remains on hold, but a formal peace deal is still out of reach, with a fragile stalemate that may not hold for long, experts say.

“The current equilibrium is inherently unstable,” Ali Vaez, deputy programme director for the Middle East and North Africa at the International Crisis Group, told Al Jazeera. “Because what Washington considers a sustainable stalemate, Tehran sees as a slow-motion strangulation. Both sides are banking on the other’s pain threshold being lower than their own.”

Tehran has laid down seven conditions before it will agree to a ceasefire and the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, then restart peace talks through a Qatari mediator, and has been waiting for an official US response to its demands. They include the lifting of the US naval blockade on Iranian ports, sanctions relief, releasing frozen Iranian funds, ending hostilities on all fronts and formally declaring the war over.

Ghalibaf said these conditions had already formed part of the memorandum of understanding (MoU) reached with the US in Islamabad in June and must be implemented before Iran will re-enter negotiations with the US.

But Tehran’s push for a deal reflects the pressure the blockade and sanctions are placing on its economy, analysts say. The longer that pressure lasts, the harder it may become for Iran’s leaders to agree to terms they may struggle to defend at home.

“Iran wants relief from the siege, but after paying such a heavy price … its leaders cannot afford a settlement that looks like surrender,” Vaez said. “The more Washington demands capitulation, the more elusive a deal becomes.”

But there are still signs of movement. Qatar said on Tuesday it was “working tirelessly” to resolve the crisis and that both sides had made positive statements about diplomacy. A day earlier, after hosting Qatari Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman bin Jassim Al Thani at the White House, Trump said there was “momentum towards the end of this conflict”. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio, however, has described Iran’s demands as “unacceptable”.

Gerd Nonneman, a professor of international relations and Gulf studies at Georgetown University in Qatar, told Al Jazeera that the cost of keeping up this pressure could narrow Washington’s room for manoeuvre.

“The US side may very temporarily think the status quo is preferable to making significant concessions now, but that will never be a sustainable strategy as it is prohibitively expensive, both in terms of overall economic effects and of the cost of the military operation,” he said.

Iran’s main source of leverage in the standoff is the Strait of Hormuz, analysts say. By seeking to dictate which ships can pass and which routes they can take, Tehran can raise the cost of the stalemate for Washington and its Gulf partners. Ghalibaf told Al Jazeera the waterway would “never return” to its pre-war status.

That leverage was on display last weekend, when shipping slowed sharply following attacks on tankers. Just five commodity vessels crossed on Saturday, November 7, while trade intelligence firm Kpler recorded none on Sunday, November 8. That compares with 31 crossings over the previous weekend, the Reuters news agency reported, citing the firm’s ship-tracking data.

In a statement carried by the Tasnim news agency, Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) said a “massive explosion” on Wednesday night brought two supertankers to a standstill as they travelled along a route it said was unauthorised. The IRGC said the vessels were carrying more than four million barrels of oil between them.

Analysts say intermittent attacks on tankers allow Iran to destabilise shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, preserving its main source of leverage over Washington.

“Washington has the luxury of waiting, but it does not have the luxury of deciding how Tehran behaves while it waits,” Vaez said. “The paradox is that the more effective America’s pressure campaign becomes, the greater Iran’s incentive to break the impasse by force.”

Trump’s advisers are reportedly divided over what to do next.
