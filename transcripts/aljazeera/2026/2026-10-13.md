## UK spy chief says Iran is stepping up targeting of military bases

The head of the United Kingdom’s domestic spy agency has said Iran is ramping up attempts to strike sensitive military targets after a series of incidents at airbases in Britain, even as Tehran has denied any involvement.

In his annual speech, M15 Director-General Ken McCallum on Tuesday said there had been a major shift in Tehran’s calculus this year and that “Iranian ambitions to come at harder targets, like military sites”.

McCallum’s warning came after UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham said Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) was “almost certainly” behind a suspected plot against Royal Air Force (RAF) Fairford, an airbase used by US bombers to carry out strikes on Iran. The incident prompted the United States to withdraw its military jets from the facility over security concerns earlier this month.

British police declared a major incident after three “suspicious” vans drove close to the RAF Fairford airbase in Gloucestershire on September 27. Five British men in their 20s were arrested “on suspicion of committing offences under the Explosives Act”, but all were released on bail.

Police said they found a supply of petrol in three vans linked to the suspects, but no explosives. The men were later released without charge on police bail “under stringent conditions”.

Two others were arrested later, including a British-Iranian national, but were later released. None of the men have been charged.

Two Latvian men were arrested for trespassing at RAF Molesworth, which is used by the US military and NATO for intelligence operations, on Friday. The men were also released on bail.

UK Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband said on Monday that the government was sanctioning two Iranians for their “role in facilitating or engaging in hostile activity on behalf of the Iranian regime”.

One is Ruhollah Bazghandi, the head of IRGC counterespionage operations, and the other is Shahram Ali Reza Tamarzadeh Jakki, whom British officials described as a facilitator for Iran’s Zindashti criminal network.

McCallum said he could not speak about the recent incident at RAF Fairford because it was an ongoing investigation, but added, “The RAF Fairford incident … is ⁠not unique. There have been other examples, not just in the UK but around the world.”

The spy chief said UK authorities had carried out 50 arrests over the past year to disrupt Iran-linked operations.

However, he said he doubted Iran’s ability to launch complex attacks, perhaps using explosive drones, due to the limited sophistication of operatives Tehran used in the UK.

“Given what we’ve actually seen in practice in the UK ⁠in recent years, that has not been anywhere near [that] level of capability,” McCallum said.

“That said, clearly it is the case that in countries closer to its own homeland, the IRGC has quite ⁠deep experience of using more conventional and hybrid technology in a more aggressive and ⁠sophisticated way,” the MI5 boss added.

Tehran has rejected accusations ‌of involvement in the Fairford incident or any other accusations of harmful activity in Britain.

Speaking about NATO’s so-called “theory of victory” at the Warsaw Security Forum on Monday, General Alexus Grynkewich, who has served as head of United States European Command and the Supreme Allied Commander Europe since 2025, said the plan rests on three strategies designed to stop an invasion in its tracks: “block, blunt and break”.

The announcement comes amid mounting warnings from European intelligence agencies of rising threats from Russia, including both the prospect of conventional military action and “hybrid warfare”, which can include tactics from cyberattacks to tampering with undersea cables.

In a bid to ease the alliance’s growing concerns, Grynkewich said: “What I want to do is assure the one billion citizens of our alliance that we’ve got this. We know what we need to do. We know how we would do it.”

But the new plan from NATO comes at a time when the alliance itself appears divided on how to deal with Russia, following a series of recent overtures from US President Donald Trump to Russian President Vladimir Putin, including his latest controversial plan for Russia to release stocks of diesel to global markets despite sanctions on Russian oil.

So, what’s in the new NATO plan, and what are European agencies warning about? How has Russia reacted? And is the alliance even on the same page on all of this?

General Grynkewich told the Warsaw Security Forum that NATO does not view a military confrontation with Russia as “imminent or inevitable”, but that it will nevertheless be ready for one.

“Implementing the theory of victory I’ve described today would require me to issue orders that I hope to never give,” he said. “But let there be no doubt: should war come, we will block, blunt any attack, and then if it does not cease, we will break the forces conducting aggression against us.”

The “block, blunt and break” strategy, he explained, is aimed at stopping an attack rather than taking enemy territory.

Drawing directly on lessons learned on the front lines in Ukraine and from conflict in the Middle East, Grynkewich said the plan relies on the use of precision weapons with “affordable mass”, like drones, and other autonomous systems.

In the plan, he said, the alliance’s forces would “block” an attack at the border to halt enemy advances on the battlefield, then “blunt” the attack by hitting incoming troops from the air, sea, and land. Then, at last, they would aim to “break” the enemy by targeting the supply chain and command networks.

“If required, we will break the enemy’s forces and the operational warfighting system that is arrayed against us,” he said. “Enemy formations will be starved of replenishment. Enemy commanders will be unable to control their forces. Enemy forces and support capabilities will be reduced and destroyed.”

Germany’s foreign intelligence chief, Martin Jaeger, warned last week that the country risked being drawn into a “violent conflict” with Russia, saying Moscow increasingly viewed Berlin as a principal adversary.

He cited an attempted drone attack on a Ukrainian cargo plane at Leipzig airport in August as an example of the escalating threat. Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt has also warned of a Russian-linked “shadow war” involving sabotage, cyberattacks and other covert operations.

In France, President Emmanuel Macron warned last month that the Russian hybrid threat facing France and other European countries had intensified in recent weeks, and ordered the preparation of plans to protect critical infrastructure.

The United Kingdom’s Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said that he had received confirmation upon entering office that Russia interfered in the 2016 Brexit referendum, although he did not publicly detail the evidence behind that assessment. Last week, he travelled to Berlin to meet with German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, in part to discuss plans to combat Russian hybrid warfare.

Russia issued a nuclear warning to NATO late last month, saying it was prepared to use its arsenal, including nuclear weapons, if the alliance attempted to isolate Kaliningrad, a Russian exclave bordering Poland and Lithuania.

In July, Romania’s Defence Ministry said it had recorded 33 instances of Russian drones entering its airspace since Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine began in February 2022. Eighteen of those incursions had occurred this year, and Romanian F-16 fighter jets had shot down three drones during the year.

Poland has also accused Russian intelligence services of orchestrating an arson attack on a Starlink satellite-internet station in Wola Krobowska last month. Warsaw described the incident as part of Russia’s hybrid warfare campaign against countries supporting Ukraine.

Norway, too, has issued similar warnings. Beate Gangas, head of the country’s Police Security Service, said this week that Europe is facing its most serious security situation since World War II, echoing concerns over suspected Russian sabotage and covert operations across the continent.

In August, Russia warned the UK that it could face “consequences” for supplying drones to Ukraine that have been used in long-range attacks inside Russian territory.

“London’s actions will inevitably carry consequences for which ⁠it will have to answer,” the Russian embassy in London said in a statement on August 17. “The deeper its involvement in the conflict and the greater its support for Kyiv’s terrorist machinery, the higher the price it will pay.”

Despite this, Russia is not preparing for “any kind” of conflict with Europe, President Vladimir Putin said last month. He added that Russia had “no real motive” to escalate tensions and criticised European countries that have suggested otherwise.

“Many in Europe talk about expecting an attack from Russia and therefore believe they must themselves prepare for hostilities, for a war with Russia,” Putin told reporters in St Petersburg.

“I want to emphasise once again: we are not preparing for any kind of hostilities with Europe,” he noted.

Putin added that Russia was considering its stance on whether to resume peace talks with Ukraine following recent Ukrainian attacks on Russia, particularly on energy infrastructure.

“On proposals to resume negotiations, yes, they are all on the table, and it is all possible. But after everything they’ve done, we will give careful thought to what steps we need to take in response,” Putin said.

Since taking presidential office in the US, Trump has repeatedly attacked other NATO members for not shouldering enough of the financial burden for defence. He has also been angered by NATO members’ general refusal to join in the US-Israel war on Iran.

As a result, there are fears that Trump cannot be relied on to provide support if it is needed within the NATO alliance, analysts say.

This West Bank camp he can see in the distance once housed a two-storey building comprising four apartments, each measuring 300 square metres (3,229sq feet), in which he and his extended family lived.

After he was displaced from the camp, he, his wife, his four children, and his mother were confined to a small, rented apartment no bigger than 100sq metres (1,076sq feet) in Thinnabeh. His home in Tulkarem was partially destroyed when he left the camp.

Everything changed for Fuad and his family on January 21, 2025, when Israel launched a military assault in the occupied West Bank dubbed “Iron Wall”, initially targeting the Jenin refugee camp. A week later, the operation expanded to include the Tulkarem and Nur Shams camps in the city of Tulkarem.

Israeli forces forcibly displaced the entire populations of the three camps during January and February 2025 and continue to prevent their return, the United Nations has said.

Data from UNRWA cited in a report titled, Destruction of Palestinian Refugee Camps in the Northern West Bank, stated that 33,362 Palestinians remain forcibly displaced from the three camps. By October 2025, 52 percent of structures in the Jenin camp, 48 percent in the Nur Shams camp, and 36 percent in the Tulkarem camp had been destroyed or damaged.

The Israeli operation has affected hundreds of homes, roads, and infrastructure, including water and electricity networks, while humanitarian aid to the camps has been cut off.

Israeli forces killed 102 Palestinians, including 21 children, during the period covered, according to the report. This represents 46 percent of the total number of Palestinians killed by Israeli forces in the West Bank during this period.

On Sunday, the Israeli Supreme Court ordered the government to provide a timeline for the return of displaced people to the three camps, setting November 23 as a deadline for a response.

Israel’s far-right Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich rejected the ruling, warning that the return of residents could allow for the reconstruction of what he termed “terror infrastructure”. He called on the government not to implement the decision.

Meanwhile, Israeli Defence Minister Israel Katz opposed the court’s intervention, asserting that the army’s continued presence in the camps was a security decision and stating his intention to maintain a military presence there.

Fuad, 42, a greenhouse installer, found himself forced to flee with all the camp’s other residents and more than 40 family members. But he and his neighbours carried with them the hope of returning home.

They moved between the homes of relatives, enduring harsh living conditions, until he finally settled in the Thinnabeh suburb of Tulkarem.

The neighbourhood, which overlooks the camp, has “restored his spirit” because he can see his home even if it’s from afar. Although he is unable to reach the building, he can gaze at it and imagine a time when he can return.

“If a decision were issued right now allowing us to return, I would go back immediately without a moment’s hesitation,” he said.

## African Union holds Kigali talks as fighting continues in eastern DRC

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoAn M23 police officer stands guard as protesters march against insecurity and violence against civilians in Goma, eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, on March 12, 2026 [File: Reuters] (Reuters)By Layth MushtaqPublished On 13 Oct 202613 Oct 2026Kigali, Rwanda – The African Union (AU) has held consultations in Kigali, Rwanda, as part of efforts to advance the peace process in the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), where fighting continues despite diplomatic initiatives to end the conflict.

The consultations, held on October 6, focused on implementing the Washington Peace Agreement, security arrangements, the disarmament, demobilisation and reintegration of fighters, refugee protection and regional economic cooperation, the AU said in a statement.

The delegation comprised former Ethiopian President Sahle-Work Zewde, former Kenyan President Uhuru Kenyatta and former Botswana President Mokgweetsi Masisi. Zewde is the deputy chairperson of the AU Panel of Facilitators for the eastern DRC peace process.

The Kigali consultations followed meetings in Goma, eastern DRC, on October 5. According to the AU, the delegation met representatives of the United Nations Organization Stabilization Mission in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (MONUSCO), the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA), the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), the Expanded Joint Verification Mechanism Plus (EJVM+) and the Alliance Fleuve Congo/March 23 Movement (AFC/M23).

Following the Goma meetings, the AU panel called for respect for ceasefire commitments, cooperation with verification mechanisms and restraint from actions that could undermine the peace process. It also stressed the need to protect civilians, ensure safe humanitarian access and prevent sexual and gender-based violence.

In Kigali, the facilitators held separate consultations with Rwandan officials and representatives of regional institutions. The AU said the discussions covered security commitments, the protection of refugees and returnees, and regional economic cooperation, including mineral traceability, cross-border trade, energy and infrastructure.

The facilitators said they would continue their engagements in Kinshasa as part of efforts to support dialogue and advance the peace process.

The consultations come amid parallel diplomatic efforts to address the conflict. The Washington Peace Agreement, signed by the DRC and Rwanda on June 27, 2025, includes security commitments intended to address concerns over armed groups and the presence of forces in eastern DRC.

Implementation remains a central issue. At a meeting of the agreement’s Joint Oversight Committee in June 2026, the parties expressed concern over escalating fighting and called for the agreement to be fully implemented, including commitments concerning the Democratic Forces for the Liberation of Rwanda (FDLR) and the disengagement of forces.

The Washington agreement is separate from the Doha Framework for a Comprehensive Peace Agreement, signed on November 15, 2025, by representatives of the Congolese government and AFC/M23 under Qatar’s mediation.

The Doha framework provides a basis for negotiations between the two sides on outstanding issues, including security arrangements and ceasefire monitoring. Subsequent discussions have focused on implementing existing commitments and negotiating remaining protocols.

The two processes address different aspects of the conflict: the Washington agreement deals with relations and security commitments between the DRC and Rwanda, while the Doha process addresses negotiations between the Congolese government and AFC/M23.

Kinshasa accuses Rwanda of supporting AFC/M23, an allegation Kigali denies. Rwanda, in turn, accuses the Congolese government of supporting and arming the FDLR, which it considers a threat to its national security.

The Washington agreement includes commitments by the DRC to address the FDLR and by Rwanda concerning the disengagement of its forces and the lifting of defensive measures. The parties have continued to discuss how those commitments will be implemented and verified.

The continued diplomatic engagement reflects the challenges facing efforts to resolve the conflict, with security commitments and negotiations involving different parties proceeding on separate tracks.

The AU’s consultations in Goma and Kigali form part of its wider mediation efforts. Whether those efforts contribute to improved security will depend on the implementation of commitments by the parties and continued engagement in the respective peace processes.

## Yemeni families’ search for missing loved ones hindered by new fighting

After six months, all of his comrades went back to their families, but Munif never returned home.

More than six years have passed, and Munif’s family, who asked to use just his first name for security reasons, are still searching for answers about his fate.

He is one of hundreds of people who have disappeared in Yemen since war broke out in September 2014, when the Houthis – an armed group also known as Ansar Allah (Arabic for Supporters of God) – captured the capital and forced the internationally-recognised government to flee south.

“Ten other men from our village joined the front line on the Saudi-Yemen border. All of them came back. We asked them about my brother’s whereabouts, but none could tell us where he had gone,” said Mohammed, Munif’s brother. “A definite answer about his status would be a relief for the entire family.”

There were hopes that a truce in 2022 between the Saudi-backed Yemeni government and Iran-aligned Houthis could help families begin their search for their missing loved ones.

Since July this year, the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) has received more than 1,800 tracing requests from families looking for missing relatives in Yemen.

But the resumption of fighting in July has halted this search for many families, who are trying to find out if loved ones have been detained, injured, killed, or simply lost in records, due to displacement or desertion.

After careful consideration, Mohammed believes there are two possible outcomes for his brother, Munif.

“He might have been hit by an air strike, leaving no trace of his body, or perhaps he was captured, and he remains in captivity,” Mohammed said.

If Munif was killed in an explosion, finding a body part that could be identified would bring the family some relief. But the family is still hoping for the latter scenario, and that Munif is being held captive by government forces.

“We would have accepted his death, adjusted to the loss, and forgotten my brother’s possible return. We just need to know what happened to him,” he said.

“We have heard about some fighters who were believed to be dead, and their families accepted the news of their deaths and mourned them. However, they emerged alive after years of captivity. So, Munif may still be alive.”

Both sides have held many prisoners of war over the decade-long conflict, but the number of captives remains uncertain.

In May, Yemen’s internationally-recognised government and the Houthis signed a United Nations-backed agreement in Jordan to exchange more than 1,600 detainees. However, the implementation of the deal was postponed after both sides accused each other of obstructing the agreement.

Abdulaziz, 24, has been on the front lines in Marib, an energy-rich region in central Yemen, fighting for the Houthis. Before the recent surge in fighting, he used to call his family once every 10 days. But for the past two months, his family has not heard from him.

“We are waiting for a phone call from Abdulaziz, and we do not know if he will ever call us,” said Ahmed, Abdulaziz’s brother.

Fighting has intensified on the front lines in Marib and Al-Jawf since the Houthis seized part of the Yemeni coast in September, and Rashid al-Alimi, the head of the Presidential Leadership Council (PLC), announced a counteroffensive by government forces.

The use of artillery, drones and warplanes to target Houthi positions likely means that many more people will be killed or missing.

Ahmed, 26, assumes that his brother may have been killed in attacks by government forces in recent weeks.

“We have not received any information from his comrades and field leaders about his injury or death. But my parents state that Abdulaziz’s voice or appearance is the only believable proof that he is alive,” he said.

“Abdulaziz is in the battlefield. And people there kill or get killed. So, his [possible] death is not a shock for me. But continued ambiguity is more painful than confirmed news of his death.”

With no word on his whereabouts, Abdulaziz’s mother will not know whether to wait for his safe return or begin to mourn his loss. “Whether he continues to be lost or his dead body returns, my mother’s eyes will never stop filling with tears,” Ahmed added.

Christine Cipolla, the head of the ICRC’s Yemen delegation, said families of the missing had the right to know about the fate of their loved ones.

“Behind every missing person is a family living in anguish. Their pain cannot be ignored, and their wait must not be prolonged,” she said in a statement.

Two months ago, Ali, 23, joined the Houthis’ campaign against government forces on Yemen’s western coast.

His parents objected to Ali leaving home to fight. But because he was unemployed, he insisted on joining the Houthis.

“We were told that Ali was killed in the fighting in Mocha. So far, we have not received his body, and we do not know where it is,” Yasser told Al Jazeera. “Recovering his corpse would ease our grief, end our uncertainty, and prove that he is really dead.”

Yazeed Al-Jeddawy, a research manager at the Sanaa Center for Strategic Studies, told Al Jazeera that the ICRC and other qualified humanitarian teams must be allowed safe access to all parts of Yemen to recover bodies, document the missing and preserve identification evidence.

“This is not only about meeting legal obligations. Families may carry the pain and uncertainty of not knowing for decades, and their experiences will become part of how this war is remembered,” Al-Jeddawy said.

Some missing people may have been detained or forcibly disappeared, while others may have been killed in fighting or separated from their families.
