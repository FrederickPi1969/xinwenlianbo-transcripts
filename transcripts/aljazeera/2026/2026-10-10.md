## With or without Hamas

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoHamas fighters stand guard as International Red Cross vehicles arrive in Nuseirat camp in the central Gaza Strip to receive three Israeli captives on February 22, 2025 [File: Bashar Taleb/AFP]As we begin the fourth year of a war that has brought the near-total destruction of Gaza, calls for Hamas to surrender administrative authority and evacuate the Strip are intensifying. For Gaza to have any viable future, it is argued, the group’s political and military wings must be dismantled.

While Hamas’s fate is a critical question, charting a meaningful path towards recovery is impossible without addressing the realities that gave rise to it in the first place.

What is happening today in Gaza is directly related to the origins of the Palestinian-Israeli question, which has little to do with any particular armed group.

Hamas did not exist in 1948, when hundreds of Palestinian villages, including Naj and Huj, were depopulated and destroyed during the Nakba – the Palestinian catastrophe that accompanied the creation of the State of Israel – to make way for the establishment of Sderot, Or HaNer and numerous other Israeli cities and villages built upon their ruins.

The group had not yet been established in 1967, when the Arab-Israeli War took place and Israel initiated its military occupation of the Gaza Strip and the West Bank, while simultaneously seizing and unilaterally annexing East Jerusalem—actions that actively set the stage for the broader Israeli settlement project.

In the following decades, the occupation profoundly affected Palestinian society. That, combined with growing religious influence—as the number of local mosques tripled from 200 to 600 between 1967 and 1987  — and a vacuum left behind by a fading secular nationalist leadership, prepared the ground for the rise of faith-based nationalist factions. The outbreak of the First Intifada in 1987 was a political turning point that directly triggered the creation of Hamas.

The group was established by a group of Palestinian men, including three prominent figures. Ibrahim al-Yazouri, was born in Bait Daras, near today’s Ashdod, and was just eight years old when soldiers of the Haganah killed members of his family and expelled the survivors to Khan Younis, south of Gaza City.

Another founder, Abdulaziz al-Rantisi, was born in Yibna—a village near Jaffa whose ruins now sit beneath the Israeli city of Yavne. He was only a year old when Israeli forces expelled his family to Khan Younis.

Lastly, Ahmed Yassin, was born in al-Jura, a village near Ashkelon that was razed to the ground by the Israeli military. He was 12 years old, when he and his family had to flee their home and settle in al-Shati refugee camp on the Gaza coast.

Highlighting this history should not be misunderstood as an attempt to downplay Hamas’s responsibilities. However, the group’s actions cannot be separated from the historical context and the role the State of Israel has played throughout this process.

Unlike the self-proclaimed ISIL (ISIS) and other groups that have emerged in recent years—which lack deep roots in local societies and rely on obsolete ideologies—Palestinian factions are deeply intertwined with local society and national identity.

While Hamas’s trajectory is marked by several disastrous decisions—most notably its fatal decision to launch the assault on Israel on October 7, 2023—the group remains, above all, a symptom of a century of suffering, oppression and a relentless struggle for self-determination.

Any proposed solution – with or without Hamas – to the war in Gaza that fails to address these underlying realities is bound to fail. It must be emphasised here that the widespread tendency in the West to frame the Palestinian people’s right to self-determination as a “reward for Hamas” is deeply flawed. It stems from a systemic prejudice that must not be normalised.

The right was enshrined in international law and supported by an overwhelming majority of nations long before Hamas’s attack.

It is also a mistake to equate Hamas’s actions with the will of the Palestinian people. Today, nearly 50 percent of the population of the Gaza Strip is under the age of 18; when Hamas won the elections in 2006, more than half of Gaza’s current residents had not even been born.

Furthermore, in an Arab Barometer survey conducted in the West Bank and Gaza between late September and early October 2023, only 29 percent of Gaza respondents said they trusted the Hamas-led government. The majority blamed poverty on internal factors; 31 percent directly pointed to the Hamas authorities as being responsible for the situation.

Crucially, a majority (54 percent) supported a two-state solution to the conflict; 73 percent favoured a peaceful resolution. Just 20 percent favoured military action.

The presence of Hamas in Gaza cannot justify the Strip’s wholesale destruction and the crimes committed against civilians that have resulted in Israeli officials facing accusations of war crimes and crimes against humanity. Hamas was not present at al-Nasr Hospital in November 2023, when the Israeli army left newborns to die in incubators after forcing their caregivers to evacuate and prevented their return. Hamas was not in the car with Hind Rajab, the young Palestinian girl, when Israeli forces fired 335 bullets into it in January 2024.

Ironically and unsurprisingly, Hamas has long been viewed as useful by Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu to maintain the argument that there are no moderate Palestinian partners with whom to negotiate.

This strategy aligns closely with the 2017 Subjugation Plan—officially titled “Israel’s Decisive Plan”—a political and ideological framework presented by Israeli far-right politician Bezalel Smotrich in 2017, which explicitly advocated for disregarding Palestinian national aspirations.

This refusal to address the structural roots has frozen the contemporary diplomatic landscape. The Israeli unwillingness to engage in dialogue with Palestinian interlocutors contrasts sharply with the pragmatic realism historically articulated by Israeli General Moshe Dayan, who famously observed that peacemaking requires negotiating with enemies rather than friends.

Today’s diplomatic landscape is defined by a deep imbalance in expectations. For many years, Palestinian factions have faced strict, non-negotiable prerequisites to attain international political legitimacy—such as the explicit recognition of Israel, the renunciation of violence and adherence to past accords.

Conversely, no reciprocal demands have been placed on Israeli authorities, who face no requirement to recognise a sovereign Palestinian state, honour previous treaties, or renounce violence.

In fact, Israel’s own political evolution is intertwined with factions that utilised political violence. Paramilitary organisations such as the Irgun, led by Menachem Begin, and the Lehi, led by Yitzhak Shamir, conducted operations that targeted civilians, yet both leaders eventually transitioned into mainstream politics and became prime ministers.

This power imbalance directly manifests as a diplomatic asymmetry also in recent ceasefire negotiations, which remain deadlocked by Israel’s demand for the complete disarmament of Hamas. By establishing the eradication of the group’s military capabilities as a precondition for a permanent truce, Israel’s leadership sets a political threshold that effectively forecloses a negotiated settlement.

This allows the Israeli government to perpetuate a status quo that yields significant strategic, territorial and political dividends.

Ultimately, doing away with Hamas will not do away with the Palestinian demand for freedom, nor will it satisfy Israel’s and Palestine’s legitimate right to security. The conflict did not begin with the rise of a militant faction and it will never end until all involved parties finally realise that, as Peruvian Indigenous jurist Dionisio Inca Yupanqui warned two centuries ago, “a nation that oppresses another ultimately forges its own chains”.

The defendants, who had been in custody for more than two years, were also found guilty of armed robbery and armed carjacking.

## Why is the US sanctioning the International Criminal Court?

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoA view of the International Criminal Court (ICC) in The Hague, Netherlands on September 2, 2026 [File: Mouneb Taim/Anadolu via Getty Images]By Christine MaguirePublished On 10 Oct 202610 Oct 2026The United States has sanctioned the International Criminal Court (ICC), hours after one of its former judges was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in what the Nobel committee said was, in part, a show of support for institutions under attack.

South African judge Navanethem “Navi” Pillay, who is currently a judge at the International Court of Justice (ICJ), also in The Hague, was announced winner of the prize on Friday.

Nobel Committee Chairman Jorgen Watne Frydnes said the system of international law was “under tremendous pressure” and its institutions were “under attack”.

“In awarding this year’s Nobel Peace Prize, the Norwegian Nobel Committee wants to remind the world that the rule of law – national and international – underpins the system we have set up to ensure peace and resolve conflicts non-violently. Peace requires justice,” the committee said.

The US move follows sanctions it has already imposed this year on judges and other personnel at the ICC.

On Friday, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio announced the latest set of US sanctions targeting the ICC, this time focusing on the court itself, which was established in 2002 to prosecute war crimes, genocide and crimes against humanity.

“We will ban transactions with this rogue court, cutting off their resources and crippling their ability to operate against us because our message is clear: the United States and the American people are not subject to the jurisdiction of this fake ICC,” he said in a video shared on social media.

The Trump administration claims that the ICC has gone beyond its remit by pursuing non-member countries. In 2024, the Trump administration condemned the court when it issued arrest warrants for Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and former Defence Minister Yoav Gallant for suspected war crimes in Gaza. It also previously opened an investigation into the actions of US soldiers in Afghanistan.

The US and Israel are not parties to the Rome Statute, the treaty that establishes the court’s jurisdiction. However, abuses committed by citizens of non-statute states in ICC territories or countries can still be prosecuted.

“Either the ICC will end its threats, or we will end the ICC,” Rubio said in a statement, adding that if the US allies failed to “rein in the court”, the US would “continue its campaign to dismantle the ICC, brick by brick”.

These sanctions are just the latest in the US’s campaign against the ICC. Previous measures have targeted dozens of ICC judges and prosecutors, the UN special rapporteur for Palestine, Francesca Albanese, and Palestinian rights groups which have provided evidence of war crimes committed by Israel to the court.

In August, ICC President Tomoko Akane and senior lawyer Abdoulaye Sey were also sanctioned by the US, in what Rubio said was the US’s unwavering mission to protect Americans from this sham of a court”.

Any assets they hold in the US are frozen, and US companies are limited in conducting transactions with them.

The sanctions targeting the court could see companies that provide services to it penalised. They will also freeze any ICC assets held in US territories.

However, they have given US companies a six-month grace period to wrap up any existing business with the court. There are also some other exemptions allowing for certain transactions.

William Schabas, a professor of international law at Middlesex University in London, told Al Jazeera that the court has been “developing a plan B” to get around potential sanctions for more than a year.

This includes migrating to European alternatives to US software, and ending its relationship with insurance company Axa, which does business with the US.

“How significant this will be remains to be seen,” he said. “Obviously for the institution, it’s going to complicate things considerably.”

“What Trump is trying to do is to make the world safe for American and Israeli war criminals,” Kenneth Roth, a senior fellow at Yale University and former executive director of Human Rights Watch, told Al Jazeera.

The ICC itself released a statement condemning the US move, which it said was an attempt to “obstruct the course of justice” and intimidate those who work or cooperate with the court.

“It is an assault on the rule of law and on the very foundations of the international legal order which strikes at the simple principle that no one stands above the law,” it said, calling on its 125 states parties to take action to ensure it can continue its work.

“History will judge this moment. Future generations, and above all the victims of mass atrocities, will ask how we responded when an institution created to protect them came under attack,” it added.

UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres said he was “deeply alarmed” by the decision, adding that it was “a serious blow to our collective pursuit of international criminal justice”.

The foreign ministers of the UK, Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Canada and Japan all said they “strongly disagree” with the sanctions, and warned of the significant impact they would have on the court.

Netanyahu thanked Trump for his leadership on social media, and accused the ICC of being a “political body that poses a mortal threat to law-abiding democracies worldwide”.

The US targeting of the ICC threatens the rule of law, as international courts and tribunals must be able to operate without the threat of sanctions.

The US itself has repeatedly ignored international law under President Trump. Its joint attack on Iran with Israel in February was criticised by observers as being in violation of both US law and Article 2(4) of the UN Charter, while the US military abduction of Venezuelan President Nicolas Maduro from Caracas in January was also condemned as illegal.

The Trump administration has also launched strikes against alleged drug smuggling boats in the Caribbean Sea and eastern Pacific, killing more than 200 people in what UN human rights experts warn are “extrajudicial and unlawful“.

Under Trump, the US has also pulled out of international organisations and agreements, from the Iran nuclear deal (the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action) and the Paris Climate Agreement during Trump’s first term, to the World Health Organization and the UN Human Rights Council, more recently.

“What Trump is doing is obstructing justice,” Roth said of the ICC sanctions. “If you look at Article 70 of the Rome Statute, it makes it a crime to intimidate or threaten court officials because of their official duties.”

According to the decree, legislative elections planned for next month will be combined with a presidential vote and set for September 11, 2027, Wafa, the official Palestinian news agency, said on Saturday.

The long-awaited parliamentary vote was originally set for November 28, with a presidential election expected in early 2027.

The decree came after a committee of top Palestinian officials met on Saturday morning to discuss postponing the vote.

His tools are still there, but the customers are not. Amid soaring silver prices over the past year, demand for his work has plummeted.

The small workshop sits beside Chowk Bazaar, one of Old Lucknow’s oldest and busiest markets, a famed hub for wedding shopping full of silver jewellery and handcrafts.

For three decades, Hussain has been one of the bazaar’s busiest craftsmen, making a distinctive form of silver slip-on shoes, known locally as nagras.

He starts by cutting a thin silver sheet into the outline of a nagra, then sketches floral and leaf motifs onto the metal. Hussain then rests the sheet on heated lac, a soft natural resin that cushions the metal while he uses a hammer and engraving tool to tap intricate designs into place.

Hussain then bends the silver around a foot-shaped furma, or mould, before fitting it over a base of cardboard, rubber and velvet, soldering the pieces together and polishing the pair. Craftsmen have used this process to make silver nagras since the 1600s.

Originally, the decorative shoes were made for daily use by the Nawabi, Muslim rulers of the former kingdom of Awadh.

“Silver nagras used to be the most important part of a nawab’s attire,” said 68-year-old Nawab Masud Abdullah, an eighth-generation descendant of Nawab Muhammad Ali Shah, the ninth Nawab of Awadh, who ruled from 1837 until he died in 1842.

Once reserved exclusively for Nawabs, silver nagras have become a cherished wedding tradition over the last century, embraced by Lucknow’s middle and upper classes alike.

“Our forefathers wore silver nagras every day. Later, they became reserved for celebrations and eventually only for special occasions,” Abdullah explained. “The good thing was that the tradition spread beyond the Nawabs. People, particularly brides across Lucknow, embrace it as their own and now a bride is considered incomplete without a nagra.”

“In the past, only Nawabs could afford these shoes because they symbolised wealth, power and status,” he said. “But over the last seven or eight decades, they became popular among middle-class and wealthy families too, especially for weddings and special occasions.”

There are two types of silver nagras — open-toed chappals and closed-toed jutis, and customers can choose different patterns, ornamentation and designs.

Hussain’s family has been making nagras since the early 1900s and the trade has provided a comfortable living. Before each wedding season, he has typically made about 10 pairs a month, he says, with each priced between $105 and $230 (10,000 and 22,000 rupees).

“We lived with dignity,” he said quietly. “We earned enough to feed our family, educate our children and save a little every month. We never imagined that one day we would struggle even to run our household.”

Over the past year, however, the orders have vanished with the rise in silver prices. The metal was already costing a bit more before the U.S.-Iran war launched in February, owing to tight supplies and growing investor demand. But the conflict added another jolt, as uncertainty in global markets drove investors towards silver and put the metal — and the nagras — out of reach for many Lucknow shoppers.

Prices for precious metals actually began rising with Russia’s invasion of Ukraine back in early 2022, but economists say prices surged significantly with the US and Israeli attacks on Iran.

In 2023, silver prices in India largely fluctuated between $725 (70,000 rupees) and $830 (80,000 rupees) per kilogramme, rising to around $985 (98,000 rupees) in 2024. But this year, amid the conflict with Iran, silver prices briefly soared as high as $4,670 (450,000 rupees) per kg before easing after the ceasefire. Even now, after some decline, prices are hovering around $2,600 (250,000 rupees) per kg, more than three times their levels just a few years ago.

Dr. Salahuddin Ayyub, an economist and international trade expert with India’s Ministry of Commerce and Industry, said geopolitical conflicts have triggered unprecedented volatility in global commodity markets.

“When stock markets become volatile, investors move their money into safer assets, such as gold and silver,” Ayyub explained. “This sudden surge in demand pushes the prices of precious metals sharply upwards.”

The consequences have been particularly severe in India, he said, because gold and silver remain deeply intertwined with weddings, other cultural traditions and family savings. As prices continue to rise, many households are increasingly opting for lower-cost alternatives, such as artificial jewellery.

He warned that while consumers may find substitutes, those whose livelihoods depend on the precious metals industry, including artisans, silversmiths, jewellery shopkeepers and traditional craftsmen, have far fewer options.

“The impact extends well beyond consumers,” Ayyub said. “Entire communities whose incomes depend on these metals are now facing shrinking demand and growing economic uncertainty.”

The surge in silver prices has had another consequence that is often overlooked by the government and policymakers. According to Ayyub, when a country witnesses a sharp rise in demand for imported precious metals, governments often intervene to curb imports and protect their foreign exchange reserves.

“India depends heavily on imported silver. A sudden spike in imports puts pressure on the country’s foreign exchange reserves, so the government tries to discourage excessive imports through tariffs and other restrictions,” Ayyub said.

These import duties are eventually passed on to consumers. “As tariffs increase, the landed cost of silver rises. That is one of the reasons why silver prices in India are currently trading above global prices,” he said.

Globally, silver currently costs $2,100 (201,000 rupees) per kg, but in India, it’s more than 20 percent higher because of heavy government import taxes and strict licensing that chokes off supply. India depends on imports to meet more than 80% of its domestic silver demand and its purchases account for over 20 percent of the global trade in refined silver.

India more than doubled its effective import taxes on gold and silver in May. That saw silver imports plunge by 87 percent to just $75.6 million that month, down from $566.2 million the previous year, the country’s lowest level in over three years.

High silver prices are now forcing Lucknow’s brides to make tough, sometimes devastating, decisions.

For 28-year-old Lucknow resident Uzma Siddique, who is set to marry in December, owning and wearing a pair of silver nagras on her big day has always topped her wedding wishlist.

“My mother wore silver nagras at her wedding in 1992. So did my grandmother and so did my great-grandmother,” Siddique said. “I always imagined myself wearing them and taking my wedding photographs in them and posting them on Instagram. It was never just footwear; it was an emotion and a family tradition.”

“A pair that cost around $156 to $208 (15,000 to 20,000 rupees) just a few years ago is now selling for nearly $673 (65,000 rupees),” she said.

The BoP’s mission was laid out in Trump’s 20-point Gaza peace plan, as was the “ceasefire” between Israel and Hamas. The ceasefire began on October 10, 2025.

The plan envisioned steps for each side to take that could eventually create a “credible pathway” to Palestinian statehood.

It required Israeli forces to withdraw to about 50 percent of the Gaza Strip and cease aerial and artillery bombardments. The peace plan required Hamas to release all Israeli captives. It also proposed a later phase in which the group would disarm and its members would be granted amnesty or a safe exit from Gaza.

The peace plan established the BoP as a transitional governing body until the Palestinian Authority, which governs parts of the occupied West Bank, could take over.
