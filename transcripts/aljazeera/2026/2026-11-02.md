## As Islamophobia ramps up in US midterms, Texas Muslims eye local stakes

xwhatsapp-strokecopylinkgoogleAdd Al Jazeera on GoogleinfoOrganisers talk to Muslim voters in Houston, Texas [Joseph Stepansky/Al Jazeera]By Joseph StepanskyPublished On 2 Nov 20262 Nov 2026Houston, Texas – The final stretch of the United States midterm elections has been shrouded in growing Islamophobia, centred most prominently on Muslim candidates, such as Michigan Democratic Senate hopeful Abdul El-Sayed.

This climate of prejudice may be presenting as a national issue, but it has been years in the making in Texas, where state politicians have pushed rhetoric and policies that Muslim voters say stigmatise their very existence.

Even without a Muslim candidate on a statewide ballot, the election’s stakes, Muslim voters said, are extremely local.

“Honestly, I feel it most through my kids,” said Samir Nadjam, a lawyer from Houston, who attended a “Fall Fest” celebration at a local Islamic centre with his family just days before the November 3 vote.

“As adults we can pick and choose who we associate with,” the 45-year-old said. “But kids, they all go to school together and you’ll have certain families who watch certain news. And kids will say things, just off-putting things.”

Muslims account for less than two percent of Texas’s population, but community organisers hope a major increase in voter turnout could flex electoral muscle. Regardless of party, many Muslim voters told Al Jazeera they wanted to send a message that they are not to be treated as anyone’s political boogeyman.

“Muslims are pushed out. They are discriminated [against] everywhere,” said Emran Gazi, the president of the Islamic Society of Greater Houston, which oversees more than 30 mosques in the sprawling metropolis.

The society vastly expanded voter registration drives this year, with religious leaders pushing the message during Friday prayers. The ISGH does not endorse specific candidates, Gazi said, but instead urges members to vote their conscience.

“We want to make sure the politicians and officials understand that Muslims need to be factored before they make any decision against Muslims,” he said.

Several voters said it was not hard to understand the grim rationale of “othering” another group for political gain. But the verve with which the rhetoric and policy have escalated in the Republican-dominated state has been staggering.

“Honestly, it’s been hell,” said Mariam Ahmed, a 42-year-old physician. “It’s got to a point where, within the last two years, I contemplated leaving the US in general.”

“I have a child. I don’t want him to be viewed as a terrorist,” added Ahmed. “I’ve already had to deal with that. I don’t want that to happen to my kid.”

The messaging from Republican Governor Greg Abbott and the state Republican party has coalesced around the concept that ‘Sharia’ – an amorphous term for Islamic customs and practices, which some countries have codified into their governments – poses an imminent threat to the people of Texas.

“We are not waiting on Washington, DC,” Abbott said at the Republican Midterm Convention in Dallas last month. “We will fully ban Sharia Law in Texas.”

Senator Ted Cruz, speaking at the same event, echoed the sentiment, saying “Sharia law is fundamentally inconsistent with the Constitution and we need to stand together and stop it now.”

In June, the state Republican party adopted an official platform dubbed “Don’t Sharia my Texas”, vowing “criminal punishment; disqualification for public, military, and law enforcement service; denaturalisation; and deportment” for any of its proponents.

Texas state legislators launched the “Sharia-free Texas Caucus” in March, which has since gained 38 members in the 150-seat chamber. That came just months after two US Representatives from Texas, Chip Roy and Keith Self, launched the “Sharia-Free America Caucus” in Congress, which has grown to 68 members.

Rights advocates and residents said the emphasis on Islamic law has been as baffling as it has been disconcerting. No jurisdiction in Texas, or the US for that matter, has enacted any form of Islamic law.

“In real life, nobody’s hating on everyone as much as they’re making it seem to be,” said Sarah Dabbas, a 33-year-old physician who recently moved to Houston. “And never in the history of the United States has anyone imposed anything like Muslim law on the government.”

Monitors say state officials have distorted a handful of unique cases, including investigating a company that sought to develop a Muslim-centred housing development in North Texas and groups that oversee voluntary Islamic arbitration, similar to practices in Christianity and Judaism.

The Bridge Initiative, a Georgetown University-based Islamophobia tracker, has said state politicians have increasingly pointed to “ordinary expressions of Muslim civic life” in arguing that Islamic law is encroaching.

That includes “public participation, demographic growth … and involvement in community development,” the group wrote in a report published in February. “In effect, they frame Muslims’ engagement as active citizens, and at times their mere existence, as evidence of a broader threat.”

The political climate has not spared prominent Republican Muslims in Texas, with the situation coming to a head in June during the Republican Party of Texas Convention.

In one instance, outgoing Republican Party chair Abraham George told two Muslim delegates they should leave the party, the Texas Tribune reported. In another instance, a state senator told a panel that Muslims were “required by Sharia to lie” to fly below the radar.

Those targeted with harassment included the son of party delegate Tarek Hussein, who founded the Council on American–Islamic Relations (CAIR) Houston office in the early 2000s.

Governor Abbott, who faces an upstart challenge from Democrat Gina Hinojosa, has sought to designate CAIR Texas as a “foreign terrorist organisation”, a label typically applied by the federal government.

Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton, who is running in a tight Senate race against Democrat James Talarico, has since sued CAIR, seeking to force it to stop operating in the state. CAIR Texas has, in turn, sued Abbott on constitutional grounds of freedom of speech and expression.

Speaking to Al Jazeera, a Muslim Republican who attended the event described the experience as “very hurtful”.

“I have been involved with Republicans for a long time,” said the attendee, who asked that his name not be used for fear of further harassment. “I identify with their business values, family values.”

While still involved in local Republican politics, he said he will leave some Republican candidates off his ballot.
