## Kerry Kennedy remembers her mother's life and advocacy in new book

Two years ago this month, Ethel Kennedy passed away. The matriarch of the prominent political dynasty, she was a steadfast supporter of her husband Robert F. Kennedy's career and a lifelong advocate for human rights.

Really, everybody can do it. There's always something just around the corner that, if you see something wrong, if you speak out, you can change it.

She's now being remembered in a new book by her daughter Kerry Kennedy called "Ethel Kennedy: The Extraordinary Life and Bold Legacy."

Welcome to the "News Hour." Thanks for being here.

Kerry Kennedy, Author, "Ethel Kennedy: The Extraordinary Life and Bold Legacy": Great to be here. Thanks.

So you described your mother, who you call mom, as fun and funny and fully alive. How hard was it to try to capture the span of her life in a book?

Well, you're right. There's so many more stories I could have put in this book.

But I really want to capture her, her story, rags to riches. She started in an Irish slum in Chicago, grew up in this huge mansion in Greenwich, Connecticut, Republican, then married my father, and it changed her life, of course.

And they had this amazing romance. And then she lost him in such a violent way. And then she became one of the most empowered women in our country, full of determination, and one of our great leaders on civil rights.

I mean, after your father's very violent, very public death, to go on and raise 11 kids ultimately in the national spotlight, how did you do it? And what new did you -- what new things did you learn about her in writing this book?

You know, you have to think about that day, because Mommy was -- she had 10 children. She was pregnant with my little sister Rory, and she was holding my dad's hand. And she was walking through the back room after -- the back kitchen, after winning the California primary, this great joy.

And this madman came out and shot and killed my father, and shot five other people that night. So it was the shock of my father's death, but also it's a miracle my mother wasn't killed. And, after that, holding her husband in her arms as he was saying his last words and bringing them to the hospital, she didn't go under the covers and pull them up.

She went to work. She had five kids in California at the time. She organized for us all to get back to Virginia. She organized for three other children to be -- to come in from the East Coast and say goodbye to their father. She organized that funeral in New York.

She, who was living for the next, for what was present and for the next generation, she was not going to go into a hole.

I mean, Kerry, despite all the loss, your father's assassination, she lost her own parents in a plane crash, two sons she later buried, two of your brothers as well, she always managed to forge on.

You talk about this hope and this resilience. Where did that come from in her?

You know, well, I think there were a couple of things.

I think she had this deep, deep faith, and that kept her going. She -- first thing in the morning, she said her prayers on her knees. Before and after breakfast, lunch, and dinner, she said her prayers. At dinner, we read the Bible out loud. And, after dinner, we -- I mean, before we went to bed, we said the rosary around our bed.

So, faith. And she was at mass every single day. And then she never thought of herself as a victim. And the difference between a victim and a hero is activism with a loving heart. So, she was an activist, either on behalf of her kids or on behalf of social justice. And she was always looking for a way.

While I have you, I have to ask you about a couple of other controversies around your family name, one of which is, of course, your late uncle's name, John F. Kennedy, on the Center for Performing Arts here in Washington, D.C. It's a name that President Trump has been trying to change to add his name to the center.

Well, it's a mystery to me as to why it's so important to President Trump.

I think that this is a memorial that Congress set up to a slain president. It would be like Trump putting his name on the Trump Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, the Trump-Washington Monument, or the Trump-Lincoln Memorial. It makes no sense.

What I think is really important is that the American people are suffering. The price of a tank of fuel oil in Maine has gone up 80 percent since the start of the Iran war. People can't have to choose between feeding their children and buying medication for their elderly.

President Trump, do your job. Focus on the suffering of the American people. Focus on stopping this ridiculous war in Iran. Do not worry about the Kennedy Center. Leave it alone, and it will be fine.

At the same time, so many in your family have run for office, have served in so many different ways, but you and others publicly spoke out against your brother's candidacy, Robert F. Kennedy Jr., when he ran for president. He, of course, endorsed Donald Trump, is now serving in his Cabinet as HHS secretary.

When he was running, you said his candidacy was dangerous. Do you still hold that same view?

I mean, I think tens of thousands have died or will die in the United States and millions around the world because of his policies. We can see that. I mean, in Philly -- in Pennsylvania a couple of weeks ago, two adults died because of vaccine skepticism, which he spreads, and two infants died.

Now, those two -- you could say the adults made their own decision, but those infants, the only reason that they passed is because people around them didn't get vaccinated. Get your vaccines. Go get your COVID vaccine, get your RSV vaccine, get your flu vaccine. And, for God's sake, get your measles vaccine.

Are you in contact with him about this? Do you share these views with him privately?

Oh, yes, he knows exactly how I feel about them.

And what is his response when you have expressed these concerns?

He says what he says publicly about them. He goes on about these quasi-scientific studies that have been debunked by the -- by real scientists.

You know, as we mentioned, your mother just passed away two years ago. She was 96 when she passed, but she was witnessing this new modern political era that we are now in.

What did she make of the way that politics are unfolding in America today?

You know, Mommy, she was raised as a Republican. She married a Democrat, and she was a fierce Democrat. But she had great, deep loving friendships with George Bush and George Bush Sr. and Don Rumsfeld, who she played tennis with every morning, and then argued with all day long.

So she was able to hold in her hands that she could have a great relationship, loving relationship with somebody, and disagree with them completely. I think that's how she approached Bobby. That's certainly how I approach Bobby.

And I think this is an issue that so many American families are facing right now. We don't have to hate someone who we would disagree with. You got to love them. Find that bond. Find the dignity in the other, and move ahead with your lives.

The book is "Ethel Kennedy: The Extraordinary Life and Bold Legacy." The author is Kerry Kennedy.

Kerry, thank you so much. It's been great to speak with you.

To fact-check some of the president's claims, we're joined now by our White House correspondent, Liz Landers.

Liz, let's just start off with something the president talks about frequently at these rallies, which is immigration. Here's what he had to say about Somali immigrants during a rally in Ohio earlier this week.

All they do is kill each other in the streets. Then they come into the United States of America and they tell us how to run our country. We're not going to stand for it. They drive up taxes. They drive up health care costs, insurance costs, childcare costs, and they cheat on everything.

It's a disaster. And we're not going to stand for it.

Setting aside the comments about violence there, what do we know about what the president had to say about immigration driving up costs?

Yes, the Congressional Budget Office has analyzed this and looked at the immigration surge here in the United States starting in 2024 and projecting for 10 years and says that the immigration surge will actually add $1.2 trillion to federal revenue.

And, by 2034, the annual increase in revenue will be up to $164 billion. So this surge is projected to boost economic activity and, in turn, tax revenues. And when it comes to what he was saying about insurance there, undocumented immigrants are not eligible for Medicaid coverage.

There are certain circumstances where lawfully present immigrants may have some kind of qualified status, but they have to wait five years before they are eligible for it. And KFF has found that immigrants are not more likely than U.S.-born citizens to report using government assistance for food, housing, and health care, Amna.

## What makes the Oregon school shooting different – and what can be learned

There are many more questions to be answered in the days ahead.

To explore some of them, we're joined now by James Densley. He's professor and chair of criminology and criminal justice at Metro State University in Minnesota. He's best known for co-creating The Violence Prevention Project. That is the largest, most comprehensive database of mass shootings in the United States.

So, some limited information about the shooter and the way this attack unfolded so far. But, from what we do know, what stands out to you about this shooting?

We have 26 years of school homicides in our database, nearly 400 cases. And this is the first time a civilian shot and stopped the attacker. Usually, when school shootings are stopped before the police arrive, it's by a teacher, a coach, a custodian, or another student who tackles the shooter before they reload. So that's a very unique thing.

You know, we have a father here with two daughters at the school. He ran toward the gunfire. It's incredible bravery. And I'm sure it'll be held up as proof of one argument to another, but we must not forget that this also began with a 16-year-old who got hold of a gun that they should not have had.

And that's the best part of prevention that we have here for the next time. Even with a hero on the scene, in three minutes, four people were already dead. So, stopping a shooter is not the same as preventing a shooting.

And so the real goal is to never need these heroes at all.

We should also point out the teacher who was involved in the altercation with the shooter apparently threw her cell phone at him and was fighting with him when that father entered the building and ultimately shot him.

But I should also point out what officials shared about the fact that the shooter entered the building by forcing his way, shooting his way through locked doors. The superintendent today mentioned how much they have invested in security. How should we look at that, the front-end security that many schools have now put in place?

Yes, it's a great question. The doors were locked and he shot through them. The locks buy time and time saves lives usually.

But a locked door is also the last line of defense. It's not the first. In our data, shooters force their way into schools in only a handful of cases. Most of those, though, do tend to be former students, because they know the building and they're prepared to break in.

And by the time someone is shooting at your front door, prevention's already failed in many ways. The real work, I think, has to happen weeks and months earlier, when someone notices warning signs and can step in.

Officials also said today they don't yet know how the shooter got the weapon or the ammunition. It's been reported, however, that the shotgun belonged to his mother.

Is that kind of situation consistent with what we have seen in the past where a minor gets access to a weapon from a parent or relative?

Yes, sadly, that's part of the story that's painfully familiar.

In school mass shootings in our data, whenever a shooter is a teenager, and we know where the gun comes from, it usually comes from home every single time, actually. It's typically parents and grandparents.

Children are too young to buy these guns, and so that's why they take them. But this is also the most preventable part of the problem. A lock safe at home costs a few hundred dollars. It's the cheapest school safety measure in America, and it's the one that's right in your house, not even at the school.

So I would say, for every parent watching this tonight, ask yourself this question. Could my child access my gun today if he or she wanted to? And if the answer is yes, then you need to do something about this.

Now, we have seen cases in Oxford, Michigan, and also the Apalachee High School in Georgia where parents have actually been charged and found negligent and guilty for these cases. So the law is starting to treat these unsecured guns as part of the crime.

Got a minute or so here left, but I have to ask you about what we heard from the school superintendent when he said how deeply impacted everyone in the community is and how this will stay with them for years to come.

From the shootings you have looked at in the past, how does it stay with a community?

I mean, it's just -- it's every parent and every community's worst nightmare. I mean, the youngest child killed was just 5 years old in this case. In our data, no younger child has died in an attack on a school. And the shooter was 16. This is children killing children.

It's a tragedy on so many levels, and it will have ripple effects throughout that community for generations. There's going to need to be a lot of care and compassion and aftercare and support for these families and for this community.

And my heart goes out to them, and I'm sure everybody else who's watching this tonight as well. It's just devastating.

That is Professor James Densley from Metro State University joining us tonight.
