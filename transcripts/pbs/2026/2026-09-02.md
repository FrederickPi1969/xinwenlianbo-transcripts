## Painter Alex Katz reflects on his career and artistry ahead of 100th birthday

Artist Alex Katz is an American great who just celebrated his 99th birthday. And what better way to celebrate than an exhibition in Nice, France this fall, entitled Henri Matisse-Alex Katz?

Senior arts correspondent Jeffrey Brown recently visited Katz at his home in Maine for our arts and culture series, Canvas.

Alex Katz is best known as a painter of people, often his friends, very often his wife, Ada, and of nature, especially the woods and water around him in his summer home near the coast of Maine. Painting for him is about capturing what he's called quick things passing.

What does that mean, quick things passing?

It's a way of being in the present tense.

At age 99, as we saw in his studio recently as he showed us his latest work, the movement is certainly slowed, but the art, mind and magic continue as he looks out at Coleman Pond to scenes he's painted for decades, a group of trees, a swimming raft on the pond., further afield, the dramatic coast as seen from above and in this painting.

You're trying to capture time and light. You just feel it. You feel what you don't like, you feel what you do like, and you feel what a painting should be like.

He's also looking back at the young man who set out so long ago to conquer the world from his childhood in Queens and early years of art making in the downtown Manhattan scene.

I had a real connection with myself and painting. And I said, well, we're going to go -- we're going to go for it flat out. And I did.

As a young man in the 1940s, he started drawing, a lifelong practice now being celebrated in an exhibition at the nearby Colby College Museum of Art, subway riders, dancers for his work with the choreographer Paul Taylor, flowers, and much more.

He first came to Maine in 1949 to attend the renowned Skowhegan School of Painting and Sculpture. And five years later, he and Ada bought this house. On the day of our visit, Katz was joined by his son, Vincent, twin grandsons and friends, the kind of scene that shows up in his work. And he still sometimes works here in the original studio attached to the house.

He draws and paints what he sees, but then refines the image in repeated renderings, as seen in this 1978 documentary, from small sketches building up the figure or scene into larger, sometimes very large works.

I paint with the idea that there's an audience out there, and I'm going to tell them what things look like, and I'm going to tell them what a painting should be.

He'd come of artistic age when abstract expressionism ruled. Think Jackson Pollock. Katz was determined to go his own way.

Something deep inside tells me not to go with the other guys.

I think Jackson Pollock might be the best American painter ever. And I ain't like Jackson Pollock at all, you know? And so I will try something else. Don't be afraid. You should never be afraid of your instincts.

It took a while for others to come along, the figures, the flatness of the paint on the canvas, the clear, crisp lines, everything the art world at the time was not, all of it now on display in a permanent wing at Colby dedicated to Katz's art.

He chose in a very sort of defiant way to depict people and landscape. And I think it was very much because he has this contrarian streak. He likes to do what other people are not doing because people tell him he's not supposed to do it.

And the seeming simplicity of the technique, it's actually highly refined, worked and reworked, paring to essentials.

It's very hard to look easy, like, when you look at the world, and it doesn't look like flat planes. So to be able to create a composition from flat planes, I think, is really a challenge, but it also is a sort of distillation of the world in this really evocative way.

He's able to reduce a whole landscape sometimes to two colors. And you can read. It's legible. You know what he's depicting, and that's magic.

Gallery shows, museum exhibitions, and high honors would all come, including a National Medal of Arts from President Biden in 2023.

On a "News Hour" visit in 2015, at age 88, Katz was still climbing up ladders and painting every day.

I like doing it. Never know what's going to happen when you start on the Canvas. I love the adventure. And I love sticking it to people who didn't think I was anything for so many years.

These days, the images are dramatically pared down. He showed us sketches and paintings based on a beach scene he recalls from a job he had as a teenager.

They stayed in the back of my head for 50, 60, 70, 80 years, you know?

These are not what most people would think of as an Alex Katz painting.

One touchstone for Katz has been the artist Henri Matisse. It pops up in works as early as this 1956 watercolor. And now he will be paired with the French master at the Matisse Museum in Nice. Katz grew emotional speaking of it.

I'm going to have a two-man show with Matisse, right?

Well, Matisse is like, in 400 years of European-American painting, he's the best technical painter, period.

I will put my paintings up there and see if they're any good.

Meanwhile, art and nature continue to interact on the coast of Maine.

## Pediatrician says RFK Jr. sowing doubt about measles-associated deaths

Schools are back in session, fall is just around the corner, and that means viruses, colds, and flu are on their way back too. Today, a coalition of leading medical groups issued their own vaccine guidance in response in part to changing and confusing messaging from federal officials.

This all comes on the heels of rising measles cases in multiple states this year, and two recent deaths that have become the center of a hot dispute.

Last week, Pennsylvania's governor, Democrat Josh Shapiro, publicly announced that two people had died, both of whom had measles. One was a newborn who got the virus from his mother. The other person was also unvaccinated.

State officials called these measles-associated deaths. But the CDC took the unusual move of contradicting state officials on that classification.

So, for more on this moment that we are in, we are joined again by pediatrician Dr. Paul Offit, where he also directs the Vaccine Education Center at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia.

So there are these two deaths in your state. Both people had measles. State public health officials say that these were measles-associated deaths. But the CDC later argues, well, there's not enough information and we're not going to categorize them that way.

This, as you well know, has now blown up into this big fight between your governor, Shapiro, and HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, who alleged that Shapiro was giddy in announcing these deaths and had possibly fabricated the cause of these deaths.

What do you make of what has gone down here?

Dr. Paul Offit, Children's Hospital of Philadelphia: Well, I think the Pennsylvania Department of Health could have helped us out more here.

I mean, when those two little girls died in West Texas last year, the 6-year-old and 8-year-old unvaccinated girls died, you knew the cause of death, measles pneumonia, which is typically the cause of death. Here, they didn't really announce specifically what the ages were of these two people died, what their vaccination status was or what their cause of death was. So that could have helped us out.

But I think Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has diverted the conversation away from what we should be having. What we should be having is we know that, over the last year-and-a-half, we have had 5,000 reported cases of measles in this country. And when you talk to people on the ground both in Texas or in South Carolina or now in Pennsylvania, they think that is a vast underestimate.

They think there could be three to four times that number of cases. Now you're heading into the winter months, and children are going back to school. Measles is a winter-spring disease.

And I think the conversation should be, let's identify these high-risk communities like the Lancaster County community in Pennsylvania, get clinics out there, vaccinate as many kids as we can so that, when we head into winter and spring, we don't suffer even more children suffering, more children being hospitalized and more children dying of this disease. That should be the conversation.

But, unfortunately, Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who is, frankly, an anti-vaccine activist acting as secretary of health and human services, doesn't talk about any of that.

Can I ask you about this distinction and help us and our audience understand this, the distinction of between dying from measles and a measles-associated death? What's the distinction?

I think what physicians will often say, for example, is measles-associated pneumonia, measles-associated dehydration, measles-associated encephalitis. And what they mean is that measles caused that. We know the details of one of these deaths. So it was an infant who died in the first day of life. We know he had measles in his lungs. We know he had a splenic rupture.

We know that the spleen can enlarge and become rupturable because of measles. So we know all that. So I think it is fair to say that that child died from measles, but we just keep going back and forth on this sort of semantic morass, instead of focusing on what we should be focusing on, which is, it's September. This is going to get much worse as we head into the winter months.

Let's make sure kids get vaccinated. But you never hear that from our secretary of health and human services, because he wants to divert us away from one of the really important issues here.

I mean, the CDC also fell into this semantic mess and says they're not going to categorize this on their federal tally of measles fatalities. This has been seen as a bit of a test of the new CDC director, Dr. Erica Schwartz.

What do you make of the agency's response here?

I think it's been disappointing. I think the CDC initially reported those two deaths in 2026 from measles in our state, Pennsylvania, and then they took it down.

And you get the sense there's a politicization of this, and it's too bad. I mean, while, I mean, public health will always to some extent be political because it requires resources, it shouldn't be partisan, and it's become partisan, and it's hard to watch because our children are suffering this fight.

Separately, just today, the American Medical Association, the American Academy of Pediatrics felt that the national environment was such that they needed to come out and reiterate that vaccines are safe and life-protecting.

What is your sense as to why they felt the need to step out in this moment?

Well, you had President Trump issuing an executive order where he recommended that we don't have 17 routinely recommended vaccines, but we go down to 10 or 11, recommended separating the measles, mumps, rubella into its three component vaccines, recommended changing adjuvants in vaccines.

And I think they wanted us to make -- they wanted people to know that those statements made by President Trump with Robert F. Kennedy Jr. standing next to him were not science-based. There's no new science here. They wanted to reiterate what is the science-based routinely recommended vaccines for children to make sure that people understood that.

And I think people do. I think, despite President Trump's press conference, the next day, the sun came up, parents took their children to pediatricians, they were still getting vaccines according to the routinely recommended science-based schedule. So I think that will pass.

Dr. Paul Offit, Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, always good to hear from you. Thank you.

## Inside Afghanistan's secret schools where women defy the Taliban to educate girls

It's been nearly five years since the Taliban banned education for all girls older than 12 in Afghanistan. And they didn't stop there. Now every day seems to bring a new law limiting women. But Afghan women are pushing back.

In this exclusive report, special correspondent Leila Molana-Allen and videographer Eric O'Connor visit a secret underground school in the capital city of Kabul, and they were the first international news crew to sit down with the women risking their lives to protest the oppressive rules.

The identities of all the women have been changed for their protection.

Each morning, in the early dawn light, dozens of teenage girls pass quietly through an unmarked door that stands open on an unremarkable side street. They climb down a darkened stairway, descending to a stiflingly hot basement hidden behind thick curtains.

They're not allowed to be here. But, in today's Afghanistan, the list of what women and girls are allowed to do is growing ever shorter.

Girls in Afghanistan have been banned from secondary and a higher education for nearly five years now. But across the country, women are pushing back, finding hidden and creative ways to educate the next generation.

Farimah, Founder and Teacher of Secret Girls School (through interpreter): From the very beginning, we unexpectedly had hundreds of students. Everyone was so happy because it was the first door of hope for them after a long time.

When the Taliban suddenly announced a ban on education for girls over 12, Farimah feared both her career and her purpose were gone.

I suddenly felt very lost, so I decided to make this class to bring the girls together and teach them whatever I know.

Founding a school inside her home was the one way she and her family felt they could make the girls feel safe. But they never expected so many to come. They're now teaching hundreds of teenagers. They don't advertise. These girls all found this place by word of mouth. The whole community keeps their secret to protect them.

This strong commitment that our students show, it motivates us to work harder for them. And they are very, very intelligent girls. They're very willing to learn, and the most positive thing is that their families are also supporting them.

Thirteen-year-old Niloufar spent her 12th year, the last of her permitted education, terrified of what would come next. Suddenly, friends told her parents she could continue learning here in secret.

She's so inspired by her teachers, she now wants to follow in their footsteps.

Now that we are studying here and are supported, I feel very happy and hopeful. When I finish my studies, I want to teach other girls so they can also have the opportunity to receive an education.

It's a rare opportunity for these young girls to get outside and make new friends. Noosha and Maryam, both 18, had been out of school for nearly five years before they found out about these classes a few months ago.

I was extremely hopeless. I felt like I could not breathe, as if I was trapped in a very small prison.

Noosha couldn't sleep for months, dreading what her life would become. Like many girls her age after the Taliban took power, she suffered a severe mental health crisis.

Those were very difficult days. I cried every night. But when I came to the school and realized such a place existed, deep inside, I made a decision and told myself, I must study, I must succeed, and I must become someone who can build a better future for myself and for the next generation.

Maryam knows the new Afghanistan she faces as a woman will not be kind to her. Trapped inside for years with her dreams in ruins and few friends, she'd lost all belief in herself.

In Afghanistan, being a girl or a woman feels like a crime. Even though women already endure many hardships, the Taliban makes life even harder for us. Some young girls in Afghanistan are forced into marriage because all education has been completely closed for us.

Whenever I went somewhere, someone came to our house, people would ask whether I had had a marriage proposal. I was afraid of this.

But now knowing her fellow Afghan women are willing to fight for her future has given her the strength to fight for herself.

Since I came here, I have realized that even if I learn just one word in a day, that one word means the whole world to me. Sometimes, you only understand the value of something after it's taken away from you.

If Farimah and her colleagues are found out, they face prison or much worse.

Yes, it's a really, really big risk. But we want to take a stand because Afghanistan needs women who should stand up, who should resist. We want to show the world that this is not the end for us.

Some girls haven't been so lucky. Maya was in her next to last year of high school when the Taliban returned to power. Her life changed in a moment. Gone were her dreams of university, a career. And she was married off to a man she's never met. We're speaking on the phone, so her in-laws don't find out and punish her.

Maya, Teenage Bride (through interpreter):

When you realize that you can't reach your dreams and are forced to stay at home and have to focus on your husband, it feels like you have no control over your choices.

She soon fell pregnant, but too small to carry a child, and too young to know how to take care of herself during pregnancy, at three months, she lost her baby.

When they told me I had lost my baby, I came home and cried so much. People said I was too young and that I might not be able to get pregnant again. That was the worst day of my life.

Young marriage has always been a concern in more rural, traditional areas of Afghanistan. But in the past five years, activists say rates have surged even in the cities. Girls as young as 14 are now regularly forced into marriage, often to much older men.

Maya fears, without school to keep them safe and occupied into adulthood, many Afghan girls will now face the same fate she did.

Girls don't even have the power to stand up and say, we don't want to get married. She becomes the wife of someone without knowing how he will treat her, what difficulties she will face, or what painful days may be waiting for her. Even breathing as a woman here feels difficult. Honestly, I don't have much hope for the future.

In the Western province of Herat, women aren't just losing their right to learn, but their voices too. I have come here to meet a group of girls risking everything to stand up to one of the harshest Taliban crackdowns in the country.

Herat has traditionally been Afghanistan's most diverse and liberal province. But now even this traditional music is banned.

Living in Herat was great. Everyone could dress the way they liked, and every woman had freedom and enjoyed her rights.

All that changed when the Taliban took power. The authorities decided to use Herat as a test case, reasoning that, if they could enforce their most severe vision of women's dress here, hands, feet and face covered, with some even arguing only one eye should be visible, they could enforce it across the country.

Ms. Mubariz, Protester and Secret Online School Founder: Every day when we are waking up, we are reading the news, we are seeing a new rule, and, mostly, like, about 90 percent of the rules are against women. They are just restricting women every day.

The dreaded morality police, enforcers from the Taliban's Ministry of the Propagation of Virtue and Prevention of Vice, began patrolling Herat's streets.

Those of us who did not normally wear a headscarf had to carry one with us in a plastic bag, so if we got caught, we could quickly put it on.

Now women are not going out, because they're afraid that, if we go out, they will arrest us.

So you think it's just a campaign of fear to stop girls from going outside?

Yes. Even one time in the city in Herat, they beat one woman in front of her husband.

They were checking home by home to find those people. Even they checked the hospitals. The people who were injured, they went to the hospital to arrest them.

And since then, they have been arresting people almost every day.

Arrested girls released from prison are so traumatized they won't breathe a word about what happened to them.

When they returned home, all their body was shaking, their hands were shaking, and they couldn't talk about that.

Dima still feels unsafe every time she leaves home. But she'd do it again. She says they have no other choice.

When I look at these years, I see that the youth of our people being wasted. This is our life that is being wasted. I know that I might die or they shoot me. But if my actions could help a generation live freely, have education and work rights, I would attend a protest again.

We have to, because we tell ourselves it's either death or victory. I believe one generation has to make sacrifices so the other can live.

And these women continue to resist in their daily lives. They're teachers in a secret online school started by Ms. Mubariz that now illegally educates 12,000 teenage girls across the country.

So this book is about depart of women. When I'm starting every day with fear, with so many problems, with new challenge every day, I'm just telling myself that I should have courage.

Every day waiting for a knock on the door to be taken away for the most serious crimes a woman here can now commit, teaching, learning, having a mind of your own.

For the "PBS News Hour," I'm Leila Molana-Allen in Herat, Afghanistan.

## Democratic strategist on the new anti-partisan swing voters

Democratic voters in Massachusetts bucked a national trend last night, voting to back incumbent Democrats in Congress. Incumbent Senator Ed Markey held onto his spot on the ballot, staving off a primary challenge from Congressman Seth Moulton in a race that frequently focused on age and the party's direction.

Terrance, thank you so much. Good to speak with you.

So, what has the war with Iran meant for the global economy so far?
