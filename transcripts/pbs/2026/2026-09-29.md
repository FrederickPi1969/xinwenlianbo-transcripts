## Nikole Hannah-Jones reflects on her daughter's school experience

For decades, Pulitzer Prize-winning journalist Nikole Hannah-Jones has reported on race, inequality, and the vast divisions in American education.

But in a deeply personal new essay, she turns the lens on a choice she made for her own family, sending her daughter to a high-poverty, low-performing school in Brooklyn. It was a decision rooted in her belief that families with choices have a role to play in making sure that all children receive a quality education. But, years later, her daughter says that choice came at a cost.

I spoke with Nikole Hannah-Jones about the tension between principle and parenthood. Here's part of that conversation for our video podcast "Settle In."

In this piece, you write that there were warning signs about your daughter's schooling fairly early. There were the chaotic lunchroom, the absent teachers, the unanswered e-mails. And then, in fourth grade, you discovered that much of her math work was wrong and in many cases hadn't even been graded.

That for a lot of people would have been a rip cord moment. But I hear you when you say that you believe in the collective good and that, for -- to leave that school would have been giving up on all of those kids. Is that why you stayed?

Nikole Hannah-Jones, "The New York Times Magazine": Yes, so let me again -- it's a single essay, so it can't tell every nuance of the story for 10 years.

If you read it paired with the first essay, which was even longer than this one, you will know that, when we picked that school -- so what some folks have said is, oh, she went and picked the worst school she could find so she could make a point. That's not true.

When we picked that school, it was led by a dynamic principal named Roberta Davenport, who had grown up in the housing projects across the street. She had turned that school into a magnet school to try to draw in middle-class parents to socioeconomically integrate the school.

When I started that school in pre-K, it's -- that school had Mandarin. My daughter was taking Mandarin in pre-K. There was a cohort of middle-class parents who came in with us. They were there through pre-K and kindergarten. She had excellent teachers really the first three years.

Even the lunchroom scene, again, I have spent more time in schools than most people have. So I know it is the least unusual thing to see kids in first, second, and third grade acting rambunctious in a lunchroom. What was the problem was the adults who were not doing any correction, right?

But my daughter's classroom teacher, she was still getting an amazing education at that point. So, no, I wasn't going to leave because I saw something wrong in the school. What I did was, I addressed and made the principal address what was wrong in the school.

But as we went further along in the school, by first grade, all the white parents had left the school except for one. And by third grade, almost all the Black middle-class parents had left the school. But, by then, we have now been at the school for five years.

We know the families. We know the children. My husband was a Title I -- on the Title I task force. He was on the school leadership team. He was on the PTA. He was a parent organizer. We were invested. We had relationships and a sense of ownership over this school.

So, no, we didn't leave. And I get people saying they would have taken their child out. If you read the essay, we struggled with it. We had many conversations, emotional conversations, and all we kept thinking is, we can rescue our child from this, but what about the other kids? There were 300 other children in that school.

And I get that the instinct, and it was my instinct as well, is to say, you know what, I feel for them, but I got to save my own child. But, in the end, we just couldn't live with that decision. And it just -- it didn't feel right. We had to try to fight for the other kids in our community.

Say more about that, because you're right. I think most people would say, when an act of solidarity with other people's children becomes an unacceptable gamble for my own child, my own child will always win out.

I think it's really hard for people to understand that perspective.

I mean, look, I know that it is. I don't expect people to. I -- one thing I realize is that, and I have always said this, it's easy to have values when you're not confronted with actually having to live them. And that's for all of us on a whole bunch of things.

There are things that I'm absolutely hypocritical on, and there's things that I really, really believe in that I try really hard to live out those values. If it went smooth the entire time, the values would be easy. It's when you're really confronted with the challenges of a system that you have documented as doing exactly this for 20 years that you have to decide, who am I? What are the values?

Now, again, when my daughter got older, I had to grapple with her saying, that hurt me. And I have to live with that as a parent. And I have had to publicly try to grapple with that in the last week.

But I don't know what else to say, except that I just don't believe -- I just believe strongly that we have to try to do better for all of our kids. And I know, in the abstract -- I have heard this 1,000 times -- well, there were other ways to support these schools. You could have -- could have done other things.

And then I always point to, then why haven't we done it? In New York, in Brooklyn, there's a majority-Black middle school. It is where most of the Black middle-class parents in my area send their kids to. This school can raise hundreds of thousands of dollars every year for its PTA.

I haven't seen an effort to say, well, let's take 20 percent of that money and give it to this low-income school, like my daughter's school, that raised $2,000 if we were lucky. I'm not saying they should do that.

But what I am saying is, if we don't have skin in the game, it's all just -- it's all theoretical. We all like to believe that we're going to support our needier neighbors, but we just don't. So, yes, were there -- were there many times where my husband and I were like, can we stick this out, are we doing the right thing, can we mitigate this enough for our child? Yes.

Was it worth trying? Yes. Are we the product of people who made personal sacrifice? Absolutely, right? The fact that you and I can even have a discussion about choice, about where to send our child, whether we can put our child in these majority-white schools is because other people were willing to make a personal sacrifice of their own children sometimes, right, their own children's education, for that to happen.

I just, maybe more than most, feel a deep connection to that struggle. I mean, I spent my life studying it. I have spent my life talking to the people who made that sacrifice. And it's always been a few of us. It's never been most. And that's OK.

So I get that people won't understand that. It is the most unnatural thing in the world, right, to not put your child ahead of every other child in every area. It is unnatural. It was unnatural for us. That's why we cried about it many nights. It's why we had long discussions.

But I'm also looking at the other kids in her classroom who are just as deserving. And it just never felt right to just leave them behind because we could. And that's all I can say. The answer won't change. It's like I told my daughter: "You can ask me a million times, but the answer is the answer."

## Ayad Akhtar blurs fiction and reality in new novel, 'The Radiance'

A Pulitzer Prize-winning playwright and author has a new novel that explores big questions about states of being and the state of America today.

Senior arts correspondent Jeffrey Brown talks with Ayad Akhtar for our arts and culture series, Canvas.

A writer named Ayad Akhtar who rides his bike on a rural road in Upstate New York and writes a story about a writer named Ayad Akhtar who rides his bike on a rural road and one day is hit by a pickup truck. What's true? What's fiction? What is a story anyway?

Ayad Akhtar, Author, "The Radiance": What I'm really after is trying to find the reader where they are today. There is something about reality, the question of reality, that excites people in a way that fiction, the imagined, seems to be a little bit less adhesive.

So it's an interrogation in the process of how you use your life to write stories.

The new novel is called "The Radiance," a term describing the change in the way the imagined writer, victim of a hit-and-run, perceives the world in his own life and interacts with those around him, somewhere between an awakening and madness.

So the book really tracks that eight-month period where he is in this altered state.

He sees the world differently, feels the world differently. He sees his own inner world differently.

Totally. Everything is more vivid. Everything is lit up by a kind of radiance, if you will. I was looking for something that could begin to get at maybe what the experience of what we used to call the soul might be.

That sense of being inside your own experience and lit up in some way, but mysteriously.

I know they're looking at me, so I figure, why not make it easier for everyone involved?

Akhtar is a novelist, screenwriter, and playwright always ready to take on big ideas,but always grounded in thorny realities of our day. In his Pulitzer Prize-winning 2012 play, "Disgraced," it was Muslim identity and religion in post-9/11 America.

And other plays and books have taken on large subjects in both subtle and overt ways, often picking up on aspects of Akhtar's own life as a Pakistani American raised in Milwaukee, making his way as a writer who is both insider and in some important ways outsider.

In "The Radiance," the duality is direct, as the character Ayad Akhtar teaches a course, Investigations in Autofiction, while the writer writes it.

The narrator is teaching a creative writing class where he is also talking with his students about story and about using their own lives to write stories.

So we have a character, Ayad Akhtar, and we have you, the writer, Ayad Akhtar.

And you're playing with this idea of what?

Well, it seems increasingly that folks tend to read fiction and wonder what part of the fiction is based on the writer's life. I think, especially when you're writing an experience that feels like it might mirror the writer's life, say, a Pakistani American narrator, and the writer is a Pakistani American writer, what aspect of this is real?

So I thought, let's dispense with the with the charade and let's just give him my name and many of the facts of my life, and then I can make stuff up, and then we will figure out, have a new relationship to this question of what's real and not real.

It's a novel of ideas, but it's grounded in time, place, the now, contemporary America.

I mean, it has to be. If you're going to talk about things that maybe are more permanent to our existence, they have to be nested in a concrete here and now. People want things. People are living lives that we recognize as lives today.

Indeed, the characters like Akhtar live in a rural area rife with political and class divisions.

He's living in the country. He's surrounded by, on one side of the state road. Trump-supporting anti-vaxxers and whatnot and, on the other side of the road, sort of folks who've moved in from the city who have coffee shops where they sell kale and whatnot.

These divisions that are very clear in our society.

Exactly. And so all of those political fault lines are part of the daily experience of those characters.

And the novel is set on a college campus with heightened tensions over what can be taught and by whom that mirror actual headlines today.

Why explore these kind of ideas you're talking about, including the soul...

Higher education. Higher education is the education in the higher things, at least in theory. It's the place to ask the big questions, the biggest questions about God, about sex, about love, about meaning, about life, questions that may not have answers.

But the asking, the active pursuit in asking is enough of a meaning. And I think that maybe, maybe higher education has become a place where it's a little bit less friendly to larger questions like that, just as it is less friendly in the culture at large to be asking larger questions like that.

In "McNeal," his most recent play, Akhtar presented a renowned writer, played by Robert Downey Jr. on Broadway, turning to AI, another way into the questions of who is writing and what is real, clearly obsessions for the very real writer himself.

I think increasingly we are confronted by an informational environment where we don't know what is concocted by AI or what is propaganda, what is point of view, what is advertising, what is discourse.

I think it's an interesting opportunity for a novelist or for an artist, because I think that there is a higher standard of truth than just, is it objectively or documentarily true? Is it metaphorically true? Does it ring true? Does it feel true? And I think that's ultimately a poetic truth that we're after as artists.

And I think it's an interesting -- I'd like to think it's an interesting way to try to get there.

## Affordability and accountability top priorities if Democrats take House, Jeffries says

The latest national polling shows Democrats with a solid shot of regaining control of the House of Representatives in the midterm elections.

If they do, Congressman Hakeem Jeffries could go from minority leader to speaker of the House, with debates over the economy, government funding, and President Trump's agenda all unfolding.

House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries joins us now.

I want to start with AI, what with President Trump meeting with those tech leaders at the White House today.

You have said that Congress needs to lean in on AI safety. What does that mean in practice? What guardrails should Congress put in place?

Well, our fundamental responsibility as members of Congress is to protect the general welfare, the health, the safety, and the well-being of the American people.

And you have industry stakeholders raising alarms that the pace of AI development is going so fast that the ability to make sure we ensure the safety of the American people is in question. And that, of course, in our view, means that congressional action that is decisive, working with all of the relevant parties involved, is important.

Unfortunately, Donald Trump has come to the conclusion that self-policing is appropriate. It is not. This is a nation of laws. And Congress is here for a reason, to ensure, yes, of course, that science and innovation and technology can continue to develop at a pace that exceeds what's going on in China with the Chinese Communist Party.

At the same period of time, we also want to make sure we protect the American people from bad actors and harms that could befall them.

Let's shift our focus to the economy, because Democrats are making affordability central to the midterm argument. What would Democrats actually do with control of the House that would make life less expensive for the American public?

Well, life is far too expensive for the American people, and everyday Americans need us to decisively act in order to make life more affordable.

There are too many people, they're working hard, they're playing by the rules, they can't thrive and can barely survive. People are drowning in this failed Trump economy and have had enough.

There are a variety of different things that need to happen. One, we need to end the Trump tariffs, which have cost everyday Americans thousands of dollars per year in additional expenses, particularly as it relates to groceries and goods.

We need to stop this reckless war of choice in Iran, which has skyrocketed gas prices on the American people and also had an impact on increasing food prices because of the increase in fertilizer expenses, as well as the increase in diesel prices.

And, certainly, we need to reverse the Medicaid cuts, which have ripped health care away from more than 10 million Americans or is threatening to do so. And at the same period of time, we certainly need to restore the Affordable Care Act tax credits, so we can reverse the increases in health insurance premiums that tens of millions of Americans are experiencing.

Another fight playing out in Washington right now is over Congress' power of the purse. And I raise this because, for the second year in a row, President Trump is trying to cancel money that Congress has already approved, this time more than $800 million.

If this money expires before Congress or the courts can act, hasn't the White House found a way around Congress' power to appropriate funds?

Well, it has been incredibly unfortunate that my Republican colleagues have become nothing more than a reckless rubber stamp for Donald Trump's extreme agenda.

And as opposed to behaving like separate and coequal parts of a branch of the federal government that is not subservient to Donald Trump, but is coequal to the executive branch, they continue to allow the president to try to get away with anything he wants to do, including changing the allocation of funds that Congress previously appropriated in a bipartisan way.

And so, in connection with the upcoming spending debate and agreement that we will need to reach by December 11, certainly, I think it's important for the American people that Congress reasserts its authority.

If the White House can simply refuse to spend parts of a bipartisan funding agreement, I'm sure people will ask the question, why should Democrats ever make another deal, another spending deal with Republicans?

Well, it certainly is the case that we cannot proceed in entering into an agreement when it's clear that the other side of the aisle is not going to, in good faith, adhere to that agreement.

It's going to be one of the things that certainly will be part of the discussion as we move forward, because when decisions are made to allocate taxpayer dollars in ways that are designed to uplift the health and the safety and the economic well-being of the American people, that money should be spent in a manner consistent with what the law says should happen.

And when that doesn't occur, of course, Congress is going to have to respond, in our view, and certainly the courts are going to have to respond, as they have previously done in other instances.

If Democrats retake control of the House come November, what's the first bill a Democratic House under your leadership would pass?

Well, we will have that discussion at the appropriate time.

Right now, we are running through the finish line, but we're going to make sure that dealing with the affordability crisis is a top priority. And we have established working groups in five different areas where we believe action needs to be taken on affordability. That's housing, that's health care, that's gas and utilities and energy affordability, it's groceries and goods, and it's caregiving.

And we believe that decisive action needs to be taken, at minimum, in all five of these areas, and that certainly will be our intention.

On accountability, there are some Democrats who are already talking about impeachment. Of course, as you well know, you were a former House impeachment manager. The president was impeached twice, acquitted twice in the Senate. How are you thinking about an impeachment or more broadly accountability if Democrats take control of the House?

Well, we certainly haven't ruled anything in, and we haven't ruled anything out, because our focus is going to be delivering accountability in areas where you have individuals who have stolen from the American people, are shortchanging the American people, or have been screwing over the American people.

And when you have people in high office who are so focused on enriching themselves, their family members, their friends, or their donors, then what it means is that they're not actually doing the job to make life more affordable, more comfortable, and more livable for everyday Americans.

And so we have promised on the accountability front, beginning on day one, to make sure that we're holding the crooks accountable. We will follow the facts, apply the law, be guided by the Constitution, and then, of course, let the chips fall where they may, in the best interests of the American people.

House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries, thanks again for joining us this evening.

## Self-regulation 'not enough' for AI safety, Gary Marcus says

Top executives from the leading tech companies in the U.S. met with President Donald Trump today to discuss the future of artificial intelligence amidst growing concerns over AI models losing control.

The meeting came after the president unveiled a new A.I.-powered Web site designed to help Americans navigate government services. The president is also pushing a new name for artificial intelligence, superintelligence, as he argues the technology is transforming the economy and that the U.S. cannot afford to lose its lead over China.

This is one more example of why I will never stifle the growth of technology that will be bigger than the Industrial Revolution. President Xi knows that. We're leading by a lot. And we don't want to mix it up too much either.

Somebody said, oh, let's do a joint venture. Let's work together with China. But if we do that, we're going to be giving a lot of secrets that -- that we have that they just would want to have or anybody else. And, frankly, there is nobody in third place. I mean, it's all between us and China.

That push for innovation comes amid growing calls from researchers and lawmakers for stronger safeguards and growing concern about whether those protections can keep pace with the technology.

For more on the future of AI, we're joined now by Gary Marcus, professor emeritus at New York University. He's also the author of "Taming Silicon Valley" and the Substack Marcus on AI.

So we have heard on this program from AI researchers and experts who have warned about the potential for catastrophic, even existential risks from AI. You have pushed back on some of those warnings. What do you see differently?

So I don't think extinction is a real likely risk for humanity. All of the scenarios, the so-called doom scenarios where AI just wipes us out like in "The Terminator," all assume that we won't fight back, and we will.

They all kind of miss some details about how we are geographically diverse, genetically diverse. They're just fantasies and not realistic. So extinction is not likely, but catastrophe might be likely. So we already had an incident where an AI reported something wrong and almost led to a war.

And we will see more of those, because these systems make errors and hallucinations. We're seeing lots of cyber hacking. And that might lead -- excuse me -- to, for example, the temporary takedown of an electric grid that leads to the temporary takedown of a hospital. We saw something like that with the WannaCry virus in the U.K. a few years ago.

So, there are lots of vulnerabilities. There may be serious damage. I just don't -- I don't think that extinction is actually one of the things that should keep us up at night.

And it raises the question of what to do about it. The president, as he met with those tech leaders at the White House today, he seems focused on self-regulation. In fact, he says the industry is capable of what he calls tremendous self-regulation, and they signed a voluntary agreement focused on AI safety reviews.

And trusting these companies is insane. The New York Times just broke a story saying that OpenAI was aware of the problems that gave rise to the Hugging Face incident that everybody is talking about months before it even happened, and they didn't do anything because they wanted to rush their products to the market.

That's exactly why we can't rely on self-regulation and why we need outside regulation, just like we have in aviation and food and drugs and everything else. It's absurd to exempt the AI companies, when they have shown time and again that they are not responsible.

How do you regulate these companies without simply giving China an advantage?

I think the race against China is a little bit overblown.

China actually does have regulation, has licensing regime around its own software products. And we race in industries all the time, but still have some kind of regulation around them. We don't make terrible cars just because we want to beat China on cars.

Right now, the U.S. AI industry is building deeply problematic software, and we need to make sure that the companies make sure that it is safe. And this kind of race against China is not a reason not to do that. Moreover, the race against China is really going to end in a tie. It's going to be like Coke and Pepsi.

Both nations are going to have their own so-called sovereign AI, but neither is going to completely wipe out the other. And so the whole race aspect makes it zero sum, and that's really not what we should be doing.

You have been calling for a dedicated federal AI agency for years. What power should the government have that it doesn't currently have?

What we really need most of all is independent evaluation of new software products, the same way we have for new drugs. You should have to, if you're the maker, show that the benefits outweigh the risks.

This should be like 101 and obvious. Right now, the White House is actually doing a lot of that, but without independent oversight with a secret framework that they haven't revealed to the public. So they did actually preclude one system, which was Mythos by Anthropic. They delayed it.

They didn't delay OpenAI's latest system, which actually has, I think, more problems in some way. And so this is happening, but in an idiosyncratic way, which means customers can't trust U.S. AI right now. They don't really know what's going to be released, what's not. It's not transparent.

We need a public, clear set of criteria like we have for drugs and so forth. That's the number one thing that we need. The other thing we need is auditing once systems are released. We need full disclosure of incidents and things like that.

Do you think Washington is capable of imposing meaningful rules on this industry before there's a major crisis?

I mean, that's the, you know, what is it, $3 trillion question is whether this is going to happen before or after there's a major incident.

The Hugging Face hack -- and it turns out there were tens of thousands of similar incidents -- is really a wake-up call. And one of those incidents -- most of them won't be harmful, but one of them could be very harmful. And we really are racing the clock here to see whether the government can do anything about it before something really bad happens.

Gary Marcus, great to speak with you. Thanks for joining us.
