## High cost of GLP-1s drives some to seek cheaper, unregulated versions

We return now to our series exploring GLP-1 medications, tonight, a look at the challenges around access.

Despite some recent improvements, millions of Americans still face significant barriers to affording these powerful drugs. So, many patients have found alternative, cheaper ways to get their hands on unregulated versions.

Stephanie Sy has the story, part of our GLP-1s: Reshaping America series.

Robin Washington does her best these days to keep up with her 2-year-old grandson.

He keeps me on my toes, but by the time it's time to go to bed, I'm done.

And when he takes a nap, sometimes, I take a nap.

For the 67-year-old, that exhaustion is a reminder of a nearly lifelong struggle with her weight.

I had my second kid, and then that's when I could not get the weight off. I weigh like, what, 255? If I could just get past the 200.

In the past few years, Washington started hearing a lot about weight loss medications like Ozempic and Wegovy.

I know it's a big fad, kind of, but it's not a fad. It's really a revolution.

She wondered whether the drugs could jump-start her weight loss and help ward off future health problems like diabetes. So she asked her doctor about them.

She said, yes, that's an option. She gave me some names, brand names, and I looked them up on the app for my insurance. And it came up not covered, not covered, not covered. And then she -- so then I asked her, how much would it be a month? And she was like $1,000, $1,500. I was like, whoa.

Now, amid a sea of celebrity endorsements and before-and-after photos online, Washington thinks GLP-1s have become another example of America's haves and have-nots.

Despite a high prevalence of obesity, Black Americans were almost 50 percent less likely to be on obesity medications like GLP-1s than white Americans. But issues with access and affordability are widespread. Out of an estimated 30 million American adults currently on a GLP-1, more than half said paying for the medication is difficult.

I was at the time unemployed and on -- literally on having to make a decision between taking care of my family or getting the medication.

I am going through bankruptcy. I haven't filed yet, but I do have a lawyer. For the quality of life change that I have had by being on this drug, to me, it's really worth going through any financial challenges.

It makes total sense that you get to the point where you're willing to do anything just to be a little more comfortable in your own skin.

Most insurance plans still cover GLP-1s for conditions like type 2 diabetes. But as more and more people get on the drugs, companies are dropping coverage for weight loss, citing soaring costs. By one estimate, the number of employers covering GLP-1s for obesity fell 17 percent over just the last year.

The carve-outs are going to remove the ones who are on the edge. But the problem at hand is that there are lots of people with prediabetes and other clues who should have access to this medication.

Dr. Betty Wang is one of the co-founders of BW Primary Care, a practice outside Baltimore where Robin Washington is a patient. Right now, about half of the calls coming into the clinic are from people curious about GLP-1s.

Discussions about access and cost can be all-consuming and frustrating.

Often, a person will come in with a sound bite or a coupon in hand thinking it's going to be a very quick conversation and that it's going to be something like $25 a month. We have seen people be irate, almost to the point of being very upset with us, as if we have more control in the process than we do.

At the same time, many Americans are finding easy access to GLP-1s, especially to compounded versions of the drug they can get through telemedicine. These less expensive options, which can have the same active ingredient as the brand names, but are often mixed with other substances, are not approved by the Food and Drug Administration.

It was very easy. Just went online, answered maybe 10 questions about your health. As far as I know, nobody checked to see if I was telling the truth or not.

It was very surprising. I was expecting a consultation with a lot of medical history being discussed. That was all absent. It felt almost as though they were trying to get me through the process as quickly as possible.

But another avenue has emerged for getting cheaper GLP-1s, the so-called gray market.

This is the Tirzepatide. This is a 10 milligram vial.

Elizabeth Zibilich is among the thousands of people who currently mix their own GLP-1s at home.

I go slow because it tends to dissolve a little bit easier that way.

Zibilich buys peptide powder from China, labeled for research purposes only, not human consumption, and mixes it with sterile water before injecting herself once a week.

You really don't feel it because it's such a small needle.

In 2015, Zibilich was hit by a truck while crossing the street. She suffered a serious back injury, which led to weight gain. At one point, she reached nearly 430 pounds.

She underwent bariatric surgery, which helped her lose weight and keep it off for years, but the weight came back during the pandemic.

So, at this point, you talk to your doctor?

I did. We decided to try Ozempic. I lost 20 or so pounds, finally feeling like I'm getting on track. And then, because I am on Medicaid, it was ripped away from me. Medicaid at that time in Louisiana was only covering GLP-1 for type 2 diabetes.

So, at that point, you're starting to game out like, what, how am I going to get this? Like, are you feeling like this is -- this drug is so important in your life that you have got to find a way to keep...

At that point, I was starting to give up.

After about nine months off Ozempic, Zibilich, a self-employed artist, discovered the gray market. She's now paying about $100 a month, far less than the up to $500 she'd pay for a brand name GLP-1.

I go through a friend who orders it for several people. They are not doing it to make money. They're not doing it for their own benefit. They are doing it to help others. And the batches that they get, they will have it tested so that you can see, is it truly this thing?

And we have not had any trouble any time.

The FDA has warned that products on the gray market are illegally sold and of unknown quality and may be harmful to consumers' health. But Zibilich has been on this version for over two years and has kept the weight off.

Do you wish that you did not have to go through the gray market?

I do. But this was for my health. If I had continued again on that path after I gained another 100 pounds back during COVID, I was going to die.

More people with access to this means healthier bodies.

Back in Maryland, Robin Washington found out she's eligible for a national pilot program that allows certain Medicare recipients to access GLP-1s for $50 a month. We followed Washington to her appointment at BW Primary Care, when she finally received her first injection of Zepbound.

How does it feel to all of a sudden be there at the point?

It was crazy. I'm glad that I was able to have that chance, because, like I said, I thought I wasn't going to be able to do it.

In just three weeks, she lost about five pounds, saw her blood pressure drop, and had less inflammation in her knees.

But Washington is aware that the Medicare pilot program is set to end next year. And since most people lose the benefits of these drugs when they stop taking them, she knows she may be right back where she started.

For the "PBS News Hour," I'm Stephanie Sy.

Tomorrow night, our series continues with a look at the promise GLP-1s have shown in treating addiction.

## Brett McGurk chronicles negotiations to free Oct. 7 hostages in 'Brink'

Brett McGurk served under four presidential administrations, ultimately working as President Biden's lead on foreign policy in the Middle East.

During that time, the terrorist group Hamas attacked Israel, killing over 1,000 people and taking hundreds hostage, including several Americans. McGurk chronicles the high-stakes negotiations he led to secure the hostages' release in his new book, "Brink: Inside the Race to Free the October 7th Hostages."

Brett McGurk, welcome to the "News Hour." Thanks for being here.

Brett McGurk, Author, "Brink: Inside the Race to Free the October 7th Hostages": Thanks so much for having me.

Most of your book focuses on efforts to secure the hostages after that October 7 Hamas attack. That day in 2023, as you lay out, more than 1,000 Israelis killed, nearly 250 taken hostage, the largest mass hostage event in modern history, and you're in the room with President Biden and when others as he's speaking to those hostage families.

Many of them are frustrated and upset. What do you remember about that? What stands out to you?

Well, it was a few days after the attack. He had a call with the families of American missing, and -- because we didn't know who was alive, who was dead. It was very -- I describe it as this all comes together. We see little jagged pieces as the whole picture comes together.

And parents, loved ones, like, wailing into the screen at the president to do something, to bring their loved ones home, and, in the middle of that call, because it's about three days into the crisis, one of the -- I remember one of the family members left the screen, came back, and she had learned that her loved one was killed.

And that call was scheduled on the president's calendar for half-hour or so. It went on for I think almost an hour-and-a-half. And it was after that he said to me, "Do whatever you can to get these guys home."

You write specifically in the book, "Inside the Biden administration, this caught us completely by surprise," when you're talking about the October 7 attack. You said: "We had no clear warnings, nor did the Israelis, from what they told us. There'd been no advance warning of Hamas' attacks."

And I'm sure you have seen the reporting in recent weeks, right...

... both from Haaretz and from The New York Times, that UAE's leader had gone to Netanyahu and said Hamas was planning a major attack. Vivian Salama at "The Atlantic" reported that Egypt's intelligence chief days before October 7 went and told Netanyahu that Hamas appeared to be mobilizing for a major attack.

Were those reports the first time you learned about those meetings and what Netanyahu knew?

Yes, well, I know the Egyptian intelligence chief quite well. He's a character in the book, Mohammed Bin Zayed, the president of UAE, I also know quite well.

So I was -- we were not aware of those meetings. And we did not -- and we looked through all of our intelligence information. We did not have a -- what you call a warning or indicator.

You describe a near breaking point between President Biden and Prime Minister Netanyahu, which is the spring of 2024, and on a call with Netanyahu, Biden says: "Bibi, man, what the eff are you doing?"

Well, that was on April 4. The president is just frustrated. He's running for president now. He's in his reelection. The hostage talks have broken down, and I describe in the book why.

And we had a horrific Israeli drone strike, killed a number of aid workers with World Central Kitchen, Jose Andres' organization. And Biden was just fed up. We all want this to somehow stop.

But also on that call -- that's why it's so important -- and we had intelligence information that Iran was preparing to launch the largest ballistic missile attack almost in history. At the same time, the frustration is boiling over because we can't find a way to get the hostages out.

And Biden is taking criticism from the right that we're not supporting Israel enough and putting enough pressure on Hamas and from the left that we're not restraining Israel enough. And what he did in that call, which is very important, was, yes, he said that. He also said to Netanyahu: "I'm going to help defend Israel from this Iranian attack. I'm going to deploy military assets. And after I help defend you, we're going to get a hostage deal on the table, bring this war to a close."

And as it played out, I think that was the right message at that time.

But also on that call, you mention, at that time, that Biden threatened to cut off military aid...

... that he said he would go so far. And he did not. You were among those arguing against it. Why? What do you think would have happened if he'd actually cut off military aid to Israel?

Well, I think the private -- the private messaging was very effective. And President Biden used some more colorful language than that, that you just mentioned. He did very selectively curtail munitions after this period.

If there was a real threat to cut off military aid, could that have changed Israel's war conduct?

Could it have saved thousands of Palestinian lives? At this point in the war, tens of thousands of Palestinians had been killed, over 30,000, by some estimates.

So how do you stop it? That's right. So how do you stop it? This is what was the consuming question. There's a complete disconnect between the view that we could have made a decision like that at that period at that time, and somehow the war would have stopped, lives would have been saved, hostages would have come home.

You don't believe it would have had an impact?

No, and I think the record reflects that.

But I reflect in the book, if there was a shortcut, Biden would have taken it. If there was a shortcut, Biden would have taken it. We all would have taken it.

You do get to a cease-fire and hostage release deal, one that you reach alongside president-elect Trump and his adviser Steve Witkoff.

I'm just curious because you have seen Steve Witkoff in his negotiating mode.

He's now negotiating, trying to negotiate the end to two other conflicts. What do you think he brought to the table as a negotiator, given his background and skill, and will he succeed in Iran and Russia's war in Ukraine?

At that moment, so many things in the region had changed. So the table was set. I thought, being so close to this, now's the time to push for a deal.

And I knew we could not push for a deal without having the incoming Trump administration working hand in glove with us, because the nature of a transition, as time goes by, the incumbent president who's leaving, the power just wanes. I have felt this before.

So I called Jared Kushner after the election. I eventually met Steve Witkoff. And I just -- we described the situation, that we think we can stop this war and get the hostages home. And Steve, I can't give the direct quote here because there's some colorful language, but he says: "I'm here to save lives. Let's save lives."

And Steve and I worked hand in glove in those final weeks to close that deal.

You document in the book how hard you worked to avoid a wider regional war, in addition to trying to get the hostages released and a cease-fire in Gaza.

Has this new war, U.S. and Israeli war in Iran, has that undone that ability to stop a regional war?

I think, if you go through the book, and there's a number of moments in which we're debating the use of American military power in the middle of this broader regional crisis.

And President Biden was always very clear, the American people understand the use of American military power to protect American personnel, protect American interests. They would not understand being dragged into a broader Middle East war.

And I think, in President Trump's term here, the campaign that began February of this year, I think, suffered from the same fatal flaws that I have seen in the past, with an infinite, unclear objective, which looked to be a regime change objective early on, and without articulating to the American people exactly what we're trying to do.

However, once you're engaged in this, there's always the bit of a trap. How do you actually find your way out of it without getting drawn into another major military confrontation, tit for tat, which I think plays into Iran's hands? We will see.

The book is "Brink: Inside the Race to Free the October 7th Hostages." The author is Brett McGurk.

Brett, thank you so much. Good to see you.

Thank you so much for having me. Thank you.

## As some Afghans escape the Taliban, other refugees are forced to return

Afghanistan is now in its sixth year of Taliban rule. Their return has brought economic collapse and deep uncertainty, especially for the country's young people. Many Afghans are still trying to flee in search of safety in another country.

At the same time, more than six million people who had escaped are now returning, some of them deported from neighboring countries.

Special correspondent Leila Molana-Allen and videographer Eric O'Connor traveled across Afghanistan, from its western border with Iran to its Eastern frontier with Pakistan, at the famed Khyber Pass, speaking with Afghans struggling each day to build a future or simply survive.

It's one of the largest forced migrations of human beings in the world. But here in Afghanistan, millions of people are being pushed back into the country they risked everything to leave.

Amir has just been forcibly deported from Iran with his sister, nephew and niece, who he looks after as his own kids.

Amir, Deported From Iran (through interpreter):

I didn't come back to Afghanistan willingly. I have nothing here, no job, no life, no home. In Iran, at least I had some income.

He lived there for 20 years, eking out a living doing casual labor. But after the war with the U.S. and Israel began, with jobs in short supply and food prices rocketing, Afghans were no longer welcome.

They often said, go back to your own country. We'd go to and from the bakery in fear. Even those who had documents were afraid.

Eventually, Amir was arrested at work. Many men picked up and deported suddenly like this are separated from their families. Amir begged police to at least let his know and take them with him.

My biggest concern right now is that I have no money. I keep thinking, how am I going to live?

The vast majority of Afghans going to and being pushed back from Iran are unemployed young men desperately seeking work to escape Afghanistan's foundering economy.

Wakil is queuing to register to return to a country he tried to flee just days ago. He and his friends walked for eight hours through the burning desert, led by smugglers demanding a steep fee, cash they had to borrow in the first place to make the journey. Before they could make it to the nearest city, they were caught.

Wakil, Deported From Iran (through interpreter):

They caught the six of us and beat us severely from 8:00 in the morning until noon. They kept us under the sun and tortured us.

My ears hurt a lot. My brother was also beaten and his eardrum was torn.

He says the Iranian border police stole whatever they had left before dumping them back at the border. It's an accusation we heard from many here.

They search us and take whatever money we have. They even search inside our clothes. They leave us without any money to return home.

Now he's back, injured, in debt, and with even less than he started with.

International aid cuts have had a devastating impact here, just as they face greater need than ever. The U.N. refugee program's budget in 2022 was $240 million. This year, they will be lucky to get a third of that. Those funding cuts have decimated cash support for refugees. Returnees used to get $350 per person. Now they get a mere $170 for an entire family.

And Iran's authorities have made clear to the Taliban if it can't or won't stop Afghans crossing into Iran, they will.

We have just been told that we can't go and film at the border because Iranian border guards have just opened fire at some Afghans trying to cross into the country.

A young woman who's been shot is rushed to the clinic by Taliban border guards. They won't let us follow her inside.

Twenty-year-old Ahmad faced that wall of bullets trying to sneak across the border into Iran last week. He made it across, but was arrested almost immediately.

Ahmad, Deported From Iran (through interpreter):

They fired a burst of gunfire at the ground beneath our feet. One of the fragments hit here. They beat us with their fists and feet, rifle butts, sticks, and stones. They put a gun to my head.

After spending a week in a detainment camp in horrendous conditions, he's developed a nasty case of scabies too.

They brought us to the camp. They kicked us and punched us. I fell to the ground and my hand became like this. It keeps getting infected. And then it starts bleeding.

This isn't the first time he's tried to get to Iran. I asked him why he'd take such a risk, knowing he likely won't make it and could die in the attempt.

I had no choice. There was no other way. And I have no choice but to go again. When there isn't even food for us to eat, what are we supposed to do? Aren't I only young? Aren't these other people so young? We're all forced to do this. This is life, misery.

The destitution here is so inescapable that this boy, severely injured and barely able to walk, will endure beatings and bullets again and again with a chance to make a few dollars to send back to his family.

Wakil says it's the same story for every young man he knows.

Millions of young people have gone to the borders because of poverty. We never know whether these young people will return safely or not. They risk their lives for a piece of bread.

Wakil doesn't have any money left to get home, and the budget cuts are so severe the NGOs here can no longer even provide transport to the city. There's nothing more they can do for him.

With evening closing in, Wakil walks out onto the endless highway to try and hitch a ride. If he can't find one, along with many others, he will have to walk 75 miles through the boiling sandstorm of the day and the freezing desert night to get there.

On the other side of the country, a different border, an equally dire crisis. More than six million Afghan refugees have crossed back into Afghanistan over the last three years, 600,000 of them just this year across this border with Pakistan. They have been pushed back by a crackdown by the Pakistani authorities and the reigniting of the conflict between the two countries.

Many of these people haven't lived in Afghanistan for years. They arrive exhausted and penniless with no idea how they will rebuild their lives here. They enter through this cage, heading towards the reception center just a half-mile away, Omari Camp.

Charlie Goodlake is the U.N. High Commissioner on Refugees' head of fund-raising in Afghanistan.

Charlie Goodlake, Senior Executive Assistant to U.N. High Commissioner for Refugees: Things couldn't be more difficult here. It's nearly 40 degrees. The weather's dry,. It's arid, and people are living in tents with no air conditioning, with no electricity, sometimes for weeks at a time.

## Haitian student's suicide reveals human toll of immigration crackdown

Over the weekend, the family of Pierre Damas Bel laid him to rest. The 20-year-old Haitian student lost his legal immigration status in July after the Supreme Court cleared the way for the Trump administration to end temporary protections for hundreds of thousands of Haitians. Bel died by suicide a month later.

Pierre's legacy leaves a mark in our lives forever in how the criminalization of Haitian immigrants can be an unnecessary death sentence.

In a church in Springfield, Ohio this Saturday, mourners gathered to remember a young man's life and one whose death by suicide has come to symbolize the toll of America's immigration policy.

Pierre Damas Bel was a 20-year-old from Haiti, an honor student, a member of Junior ROTC, a young man just starting college with dreams of going into medicine.

Patricia Blair, Former Teacher of Pierre Damas Bel: Pierre was probably one of the most hardworking and dedicated students I have ever worked with in my 30 years of teaching.

Patricia Blair was Bel's English teacher at Springfield High.

Always so happy to be there, and I always felt like he was happy to see me. And it was just one of those moments in your teaching career where you knew that this was a student who was going to succeed.

But, in July, Bel, along with hundreds of thousands of other Haitians in the U.S., were told that TPS, their Temporary Protected Status to live in America, was being ended by the Trump administration.

In a statement to the "News Hour" in August, ICE officials emphasized: "Temporary Protected Status is exactly that, temporary."

So Bel was summoned to an ICE processing center and fitted with an ankle monitor.

Pierre was very proud. He always was very immaculately dressed. He cared about how people perceived him. And the ankle monitor for him was a shackle.

Bel wrote on social media: "I came to this country to pursue my education. I didn't come here to commit a crime or hurt anyone. Yet now I'm walking through the streets of the United States with a GPS monitor on my leg, carrying a feeling of shame and humiliation that I never imagined I would experience."

Hundreds of other Haitians in Springfield, a town with a large number of TPS holders, were also given ankle monitors, which allow authorities to track their whereabouts while the administration plans their deportations.

Bel's father, Pierre Ronal Bel, said he spoke with his son right before he died. He said his son complained of being treated like an animal, being bullied and called a criminal.

"He called me at 8:03 on a video call. 'I'm not feeling OK mentally because of the bullying.' And I told him to come home so we can talk."

A short while later, Bel stepped out into oncoming highway traffic and was killed.

Let them see Pierre Damas Bel. Let them see.

His family displayed many of his personal belongings at his funeral and welcomed in the media, an echo of what Emmett Till's mother did back in 1955 to show the world the reality of the racist killing of her son, and thereby help galvanize the civil rights movement.

Like Emmett Till, Pierre Damas Bel died under the weight of a racialized system that did not provide him with equal liberty and equal justice. And this is a story that we must continue telling.

Among those gathered was Ohio's Republican Governor Mike DeWine, who has publicly criticized the Trump administration for ending TPS.

This is a personal tragedy for the family and for the community here in Springfield. But it's also a tragedy for the state of Ohio and our country. We need people like Pierre. So the policy is what's wrong. We want to get rid of anybody who's violent and committing offensively.

I think we all agree on that. But someone like Pierre, who all he wanted to do is live the American dream, I mean, he was -- that's what he wanted to do, and now he's gone.

That same day, about 25 miles away, President Trump defended his immigration policy at a rally for Republican Senator Jon Husted, who is in a tight race against former Democratic Senator Sherrod Brown.

In Ohio, Democrats are running radical left phony Sherrod Brown, who was a disaster when he was in.

He helped dump thousands of migrants into communities like Springfield, Ohio, and destroyed those communities, and we're now bringing those communities back.

President Trump hasn't commented on Bel's death, but in a statement to the "News Hour," a DHS spokesperson said -- quote -- "Any attempt to blame ICE for the death of Pierre Damas Bel are false. No one is required to stay in the United States illegally and wear a GPS monitor."

In the weeks since Bel's death, immigration authorities have reportedly begun ordering Haitian immigrants to download a phone app that tracks their location, instead of requiring an ankle monitor. It's an enforcement shift that might have made a difference for Bel, one of many painful what-ifs, says his former teacher.

It just haunts me. Had I e-mailed him one more time, had I reached out, would that have made a difference? And we won't know. But I'm hoping that what comes out of this is that I don't have to worry about any other student suffering that same kind of indignity and trauma.
