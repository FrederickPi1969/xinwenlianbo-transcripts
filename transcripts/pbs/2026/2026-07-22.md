## Thomas Jefferson's complicated legacy 250 years after the Declaration of Independence

It has been 250 years since Thomas Jefferson wrote the Declaration of Independence, a document that declared all men equal, even while hundreds of thousands of people remained enslaved.

Judy Woodruff recently traveled to his Virginia home of Monticello to learn more about the third president of the U.S. and about how his own contradictions reflected the nation's.

It's part of our series Crossroads: America at 250.

Thomas Jefferson's legacy is written all over his beloved Monticello, his home and plantation perched on a mountaintop in Charlottesville, Virginia.

But some of the newest words here aren't his; 610 names are engraved on the 60-foot-long curved memorial wall, one for each of the enslaved people who lived and labored here. Made out of weathered steel, it opened in 2023.

Annette Gordon-Reed, Carl M. Loeb University Professor, Harvard University:

For generations, their stories were largely absent from the way Americans remember the author of the Declaration of Independence.

What does it say that it took this many years to recognize the lives of these enslaved people?

Well, it's the state of the country. We became interested in the issue of race and slavery in the 20th century as we realized there was a broader story to be told.

That story has been at the center of Annette Gordon-Reed's work for decades. A professor of law and history at Harvard University, she is one of the country's leading scholars on Jefferson. She took me on a tour of the home and grounds he designed.

So this is all the mind of Thomas Jefferson in this one section here.

Oh, absolutely. Yes, he did love this place. And that's one reason, when we think about giving up slavery, this place ran on slavery.

Over his lifetime, Jefferson enslaved hundreds of people.

How large a contradiction was there between what he wrote in the Declaration, all men are created equal, and how he lived his life?

Well, it's a huge contradiction, but it's a contradiction that I think that lots of people lived with. One of the things that happens when you put things down on paper is that you're held to a particular standard. But because he created this document that we all look to, we have higher expectations of him, I think, than we do of other people.

Jefferson was also a farmer, violinist, scientist, and an architect who never quite finished work on his own home here.

Throughout his 83-year life, he wrote tens of thousands of letters and documents that helped shape the early United States. That's one of the things about being a prolific writer and keeping a record. People can note what you said when you were 20 and know what you said when you were 80, and sometimes they don't go together.

In her latest book, "Jefferson on Race: A Reader," which he edited, Gordon-Reed revisits those writings to ask what they reveal about his views on race, slavery, and the country he helped create.

It was not until the 20th century and the civil rights movement that people began in earnest to really question what he said about it.

But she also argues that Jefferson was trying to carefully shape how history would remember him.

You have spent, what, 30 years studying him. What new were you able to learn from all of this?

We focused on the way that Jefferson fell short and was conservative in ways. But I think the letters show and the documents show that this is a person working in a very, very complicated situation.

He wants to be remembered for the revolution and for the University of Virginia and the statute of religious freedoms. Those are things that are progressive, creating a university that didn't have a chapel at the center, but had a library at the center of it, championing religious freedom. That was a progressive kind of thing, and the Declaration of Independence, helping to create a nation that was a republic in a world that was mainly monarchies.

But these writings also revealed deeply racist beliefs.

The interesting thing about putting these letters together and these documents together is to see how people live white supremacy, how they live race in the country. He has particular views about race, particular views about African American people.

In the "Notes on the State of Virginia," for example, he says that he never saw a Black person utter anything beyond plain narration, in other words, telling a story. He's basically saying they couldn't reason. And yet, at Monticello, he asked people to do things all the time that required reason.

And in some of these letters, he talks about individual people as intelligent and capable of doing things.

But perhaps no part of Jefferson's life has forced a greater reckoning than his relationship with the enslaved Hemings family.

Sally Hemings and her siblings, five of her siblings, had the same father as Martha.

As many as 70 members of the Hemings family were enslaved at Monticello over the course of five generations. After his wife, Martha, died, Jefferson began a decades-long relationship with Sally Hemings, an enslaved woman who was also the half-sister of his wife. Together, they had six children.

They weren't seen as important. You change the whole story when you identify, you give the person a name and you give them a context. This isn't just a 14-year-old manservant. This is his wife's half-brother.

And so we don't know how they dealt with each other, but immediately the reader can think, well, what was that like? How did they do that?

Of the more than 600 people Jefferson owned, he freed only 10. Most were members of the Hemings family.

Well, the question of race. He lived in a community that was suffused with these ideas. And it's not just about an individual who has bad thoughts about race. It's a community that has values that promote these kinds of things.

Despite Jefferson never giving up the system of enslavement that sustained his way of life, Gordon-Reed resists reducing him to a single dimension.

He thought he had done a lot. Jefferson was obsessed with the revolution and the United States of America, the creation of the United States of America. It was a big deal to create a new country.

And I think his record shows that we have expectations of him that could never have been fulfilled. He was on Earth to do the things that he did. And the things that I want him to do or I would have wanted him to do, he wasn't.

Given everything we know, should he be more honored, or do the negatives outweigh the positives?

I don't think the negatives outweigh the positives. I think we're sitting in a country that he helped to found, and so many aspects of the country have moved forward because of the words that he decided to put in the Declaration.

It could have been written in a completely different way. But to have those words about equality and the pursuit of happiness, those things have been enormously useful to all Americans.

Have your own views on this evolved over time?

Yes, I think maybe it's because I'm getting older, but I am less, I would say, negative about Jefferson. I see how hard it is to do anything in the world.

And the fact that he did a lot is impressive to me. And I think it's as much about me and my growth and my understanding about how to grapple with these kinds of contradictions. It's been a learning experience for me.

For Gordon-Reed, the question isn't whether Jefferson was a hero or a villain. It's what Americans do with a founder whose ideals reached farther than he did.

You have said that understanding Thomas Jefferson calls on -- in a way, calls on all of us to think about, are we living up to our ideals at this moment? What did you mean by that?

In looking at him and seeing flaws and the good parts and the bad parts, we -- and also the optimism that he had about the country, we have to look to ourselves to decide, are we up to this, this notion of keeping a republic?

And are -- do we really value the idea of science? Do we really value the idea of a multicultural society in a place that is -- has been from the very beginning full of all different types of cultures and religions, religious diversity? That's always been a part of America.

But do we value that? And it's not clear to me that many of our fellow Americans actually do. So I think it's an opportunity for us to search as we approach the 250th anniversary, do we want to go forward with all of this, this American experiment that he thought was something new under the sun?

He said, we can now say that there's something new under the sun, and he thought that was the United States. And the question is, do we believe that?

For the "PBS News Hour," I'm Judy Woodruff in Charlottesville, Virginia.

Judy Woodruff is a senior correspondent and the former anchor and managing editor of the PBS News Hour. She has covered politics and other news for five decades at NBC, CNN and PBS.

## 'This is about the rule of law': U.S. attorney fired by Trump sues to overturn dismissal

A U.S. attorney in Washington state who was abruptly fired by the Trump administration is now suing the government over his dismissal, starting a major legal fight over the president's power to shape the judiciary.

Our justice correspondent, Ali Rogin, has more.

Roger Rogoff, a former Washington state judge and prosecutor, was sworn in last week as U.S. attorney for the Western District of Washington after a panel of judges in the district unanimously selected him. Less than an hour later, he was fired via e-mail by a senior Trump administration official.

His was the latest in a series of similar dismissals. The Justice Department argues his firing is lawful and that the president can remove any court-appointed interim U.S. attorney. Rogoff disagrees and has filed a lawsuit to overturn his firing.

He says -- quote -- "The president's actions violate the law and they ignore the protections of the Constitution of the United States."

You were sworn in last week and then fired within the hour. I want to ask, what was going through your mind at the time?

Well, it wasn't unexpected. This had been a long process, and I knew that I was going to be appointed a couple weeks before. And I know that the judges had reached out to the administration and they were aware as well.

And we tried to reach out to the administration. So it was not a surprise, given that they had not responded, that that's how it would end up.

You are not the first U.S. attorney President Trump has fired in this way. He's not the first president who has fired U.S. attorneys. So why file suit? And what argument are you making?

So this is about the rule of law. This is about ensuring that the constitutional directive for divided government and the constitutional directive for the advice and consent of the Senate and the involvement of Congress is followed vigorously.

That is what this is about. We want to make sure that someone doesn't continue serving as the head of the U.S. attorney's office at the Western District of Washington who's not a U.S. attorney. He is not the person designated to lead the office, and it should be a U.S. attorney leading this office.

Right. He has been doing so on a de facto basis after his temporary appointment to that role expired in February. So there hasn't been anybody in that job officially since then.

What does it do to cases in the district when there isn't someone officially in charge?

Well, I think it puts them in danger. I think they are subject to motions by criminal defendants. I think they're subject to motions by civil litigants, saying that indictments, subpoenas, and other documents signed by a purported head of the office are not valid documents.

I also think it creates problems in terms of leadership within the office, morale within the office. You do not want to have the office led via this chaotic situation. It hurts those folks who are doing the work every day.

Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche has defended this firing, others like it, saying that this is a presidential prerogative and that there are rogue judges appointing their own choices. What do you say to that?

There are no rogue judges. The judges in this district followed the law.

They followed the Vacancies Act and did exactly what the act requires them to do, which is, after 120 days with no U.S. attorney appointed and confirmed, the judges have the right and the duty to put a U.S. attorney in place until the president does what he's supposed to do, which is get a U.S. attorney appointed and get them before the Senate, so that they can be confirmed.

Until that happens, we follow the law, and the law says that the judges get to do exactly what the judges did.

I want to ask you about what your role would be in this Department of Justice at this time. It's been well established that this DOJ maintains its role is to zealously defend the president's agenda and interests.

And the Supreme Court recently affirmed the president's ability and authority to fire any government -- almost any government official at will. So if you don't agree with the Department of Justice about what your role would entail, why wouldn't the president be justified in firing you?

First of all, as I told the judges, as I told the bipartisan commission that pushed me through to be interviewed by the judges, I agree that that's my job is to is to push forward the priorities of this administration, and it's what I promised I would do.

The priorities of this administration are not a mystery, and they're not something that's out in the ether. They are written down in a memorandum that was put together by A.G. Pam Bondi in 2025, and they set out, I think, six priorities, none of which are particularly controversial or difficult.

They include focusing on human trafficking. They include focusing on international drug and gun trafficking organizations. They focus on criminal immigration cases and a couple of other things, and all of them are reasonable. And I read that memorandum, and I can certainly put forward the president's priorities and agenda for the U.S. attorney's office in the Western District of Washington.

So that is not a problem. The case that you referred to, the Slaughter case, which came down a couple weeks ago, is not the same situation as U.S. attorney appointments that are at issue here. There is a clear statutory description of what should happen when there is a vacancy in an office of the U.S. attorney's office, and we have followed, we the judges have followed that to the letter.

And that Vacancies Act is designed to ensure that congressional involvement, which is part of the Constitution, is respected and that we follow through on that requirement.

Roger Rogoff, thank you so much for sharing your story.

Thank you so much. It's great to be here. Great to meet you.
