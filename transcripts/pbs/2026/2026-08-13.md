She was super cute playing around and she looks really active.

She's the result of the zoo's yearslong process to build a herd that would aid in the survival of endangered Asian elephants.

But the elephant in the room, calves like Linh Mai, are vulnerable to an often deadly disease. It's called elephant endotheliotropic herpesvirus, or EEHV, and all Asian elephants will get it in their lifetime. While it stays latent in some, roughly 60 percent of baby elephants that develop the disease will die from it.

Scientists think it spreads through trunk-to-trunk contact.

## Trump administration ends funding for Arctic climate report

This week, the Trump administration said it would stop funding the Arctic Report Card, a longstanding effort to synthesize the latest research about the fastest warming place on Earth.

Now scientists are scrambling to find a way to publish this major annual report anyway to continue to track how climate change is transforming the Arctic, impacting everything from glaciers to native communities to global fishing and shipping routes.

This decision is the latest in a series of moves by the administration to curtail scientific research into the impacts of climate change, which the administration argues is plagued with misinformation and alarmism.

For more on what this means, we are joined again by glaciologist Twila Moon. She's been a lead editor of the Arctic Report Card, and she's the deputy lead scientist for the National Snow and Ice Data Center at the University of Colorado Boulder. This conversation is part of our ongoing series Tipping Point.

Twila Moon, so nice to have you back on the program.

The administration argues that the underlying research will continue, will not go away, just their support of the synthesis of it into this report. If that's the case, what does it matter if the report goes away?

Twila Moon, U.S. National Snow and Ice Data Center: Well, unfortunately, the simple existence of data does not mean that people are finding that data and being able to access it, use it, and understand what it means.

So the Arctic Report Card has played a really important role in bridging the gap between raw data, which is really difficult for most people to understand or work with, towards something that can serve nonscientists so that they can be making good decisions with proper, up-to-date environmental awareness.

Could we step back for a second and explain why we care about what's happening in the Arctic? Because, to many people in the Lower 48, they might think this is just a distant place that they will never go to, and who cares really what happens up there? Why is it going to impact me?

Well, what happens in the Arctic does not stay in the Arctic.

Changes that we see happening with permafrost or frozen ground, also changes with sea ice that forms on the surface of the Arctic Ocean, those changes actually themselves influence the weather we experience down here in the Lower 48.

But we can also see, because the Arctic is warming so quickly, we're seeing a lot of increase in geopolitical tensions, an increased attention to security and military activity in the Arctic, and that means that the Arctic has become a U.S. administration priority in regards to paying attention to security, international relations, changes with shipping, and also economic development in the area.

I mean, we have seen other instances, like these former NOAA staffers who were pushed out and then started their own independent Web site called Climate.US to maintain a repository of climate data.

Do you think that that's something that you and your colleagues are going to have to do now here?

Well, myself, the other lead independent editors and our incredible authors -- we have usually more than 100 authors from a dozen countries. This is an international effort. We're going to do everything we can to get those data to the people who need them for making decisions and having environmental awareness.

But I want to point out that simply being able to do this in another space does not mean that it's going to have the depth, all of the resources and tools that people expect. So there's a real loss in moving this to being something that's no longer supported by NOAA, where we have spent two decades building tools, resources, and a really trusted resource for folks.

I mean, as I mentioned and as you well know, the Trump administration has taken other steps like this. They have dismissed scientists who were working on the National Climate Report. They put a climate change skeptic in charge of that document.

They have dismantled the preeminent atmospheric research center, NCAR, in Colorado. They have stopped updating the federal database that tracks the cost of hurricanes and floods and things that we know are driven by climate change.

The president argues that climate change is a hoax. Members of his administration argue that all of this work is full of alarmism and is just meant to terrify people. As someone who does this benchmark science, what -- how do you respond to that?

Well, the Arctic Report Card and all of the science that we're reporting in it is specifically emphasizing direct observations of changes.

So, this is not a report that is projecting about the future or making inferences about what's happening on the ground. This is a resource that is providing real, accurate, and complete, peer-reviewed, trustworthy information about what is actually happening in place.

And there's no arguing with reality. It's not a political position. It's a scientific resource.

I mean, I know you have spent a lot of time on Capitol Hill talking to legislators and their staff about the importance of this. We have seen in the past pushback by Republicans and Democrats to certain cuts to climate research.

What is the argument that you make to legislators? And how do they respond to you when they see substantive cuts like this going on?

I think that many representatives really, clearly understand how valuable environmental intelligence is for making decisions right now, especially in a world that is changing quickly and giving us many different risks and hazards that we have never faced before.

And so I honestly hold on to hope that we may still have a pathway to see the Arctic Report Card as a NOAA report. But, like I say, we are going to do our darndest to ensure that this data is getting to those people who need it, because there are many people within the U.S., within states, local communities, Alaska, our Arctic state, and also folks in other countries who look to this report for getting information, making decisions, and educating their students and work force.

Twila Moon of the National Snow and Ice Data Center, always great to talk to you. Thank you.

## DOJ deploys tactic to strip naturalized Americans of citizenship

Earlier this month, federal prosecutors moved to strip citizenship from 25 naturalized Americans, accusing them of lying about their pasts and hiding serious crimes. It's called denaturalization, and the Justice Department now calls it one of its top priorities.

Justice correspondent Ali Rogin looks at what's behind this dramatic shift in law enforcement strategy.

Since President Trump returned to office, the Justice Department has filed more denaturalization complaints than the Biden administration did during its entire four years.

To help us understand why they're using this tool more, I'm joined by Cassandra Burke Robertson, a law professor at Case Western Reserve University.

Cassandra, thank you so much for being here.

I first want to ask you, how does the Trump administration's use of this tool differ from what we have seen from previous administrations over the past few decades?

Cassandra Burke Robertson, Professor of Law, Case Western Reserve University: Yes, I think that the Trump administration's use of denaturalization differs in a couple big ways.

One of those is that the Trump administration is just filing many, many more cases. Another way that they are differing from previous administrations is that these statements put out by the Trump administration seem to suggest that denaturalization is an end goal all by itself and that it's in fact part of the administration's immigration priorities.

And then the third way that the administration has used it differently is that the administration has used denaturalization as a political tool. So the administration has talked very specifically about pursuing denaturalization against individuals that have made statements that perhaps angered the government.

And you mentioned how this administration is using denaturalization as an end goal in and of itself. How does that differ from what used to be done? What sort of cases might be subject to denaturalization?

So, in the modern era, denaturalization has been incredibly rare, less than 10 cases a year, on average.

And, generally, the cases that we have seen have been cases involving issues of national security. So, for example, we saw denaturalization cases against former Nazi concentration camp guards. And we saw a few denaturalization cases brought as part of the war on terror, trying to focus in on national security interests.

And, in your review of these cases, do any of them strike you as ones prior administrations would have also sought denaturalization for?

there are a few that likely would have been brought under any administration.

But the current administration is filing some cases based on facts that happened years ago or decades ago, when other administrations could have reviewed those same facts and decided to file a denaturalization case, but didn't decide to do so.

Could this be further politicized or weaponized? I'm thinking here of New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani, himself a naturalized citizen.

Some of his critics, including a couple of Republican lawmakers, have called for his citizenship to be revoked and for him to be deported. Is he a possible target here?

It's not a realistic proposition, in the sense that if they were to try to denaturalize somebody like Zohran Mamdani, he has access to the absolute best attorneys. The constitutional issues would be fully litigated. I think he would lodge a very quick and strong defense.

The Trump administration in most of these cases cites as part of the basis for bringing these complaints lying about somebody's past in order to gain citizenship. Is that in and of itself enough to qualify for denaturalization?

The Supreme Court has said that, if somebody makes a misstatement as part of the naturalization process, the government has to show materiality, that the misstatement would have made a difference, that somebody -- in the most common situation, the person would not have been granted citizenship if the government had been told the truth.

Who adjudicates these cases? Where -- how do they move through the system?

They go to federal court. And, in court, the government has to prove that the elements of the denaturalization case are satisfied, so typically showing that somebody maybe lied materially in the process.

So, once somebody loses citizenship status, whatever facts were brought as part of that denaturalization proceeding can also then be used in a subsequent removal proceeding or a deportation proceeding.

Cassandra Burke Robertson with Case Western Reserve University, thank you so much for joining us.

## 'Act of terror': U.S. condemns Israeli settler siege of Palestinian homes in West Bank

Today, the U.S. condemned the siege of Palestinian homes by Israeli settlers in the West Bank, where they have cut off water, electricity, and essential supplies to some residents.

The U.S. ambassador to Israel, Mike Huckabee, called the settlers' actions a -- quote -- "act of terror," adding American pressure that appears to have prompted Israeli forces to intervene.

Nick Schifrin spoke with one family victimized by the recent violence, but, first, a look at how it started.

The siege began when Israeli settlers turned a home into a prison brick by brick. They closed the only road in, cut off the water and power, and trapped nearby families. Dozens of settlers, as seen in security camera footage, swarmed a home in Qusra, built by a Palestinian-American, trapping his family inside, Qusai Ridi and his teenage son, Ahmed.

Just 2.5 weeks ago, settlers set fire to the village mosques, burning the inside, and on the blackened facade left a menacing message: "The land of Israel is redeemed with blood. Revenge."

Outside the Ridi family home, the settlers remained even overnight. Their threat forced much of the rest of the village to flee, including 28-year-old Ruqaya Hassan and her daughter.

Ruqaya Hassan, House Besieged by Settlers (through interpreter): We woke up many times at night to the sound of stones being thrown at our house, just like the sound of bullets.

She escaped with the kids. Her husband remained behind to try and defend their home.

My daughters were overcome with fear and panic because we were trapped in the house. My daughter kind of became scared. She doesn't go to the toilet by herself for fear that settlers might come.

The U.N. says attacks by Israeli settlers have reached unprecedented levels, more than 1,400 incidents in 260 communities just this year, displacing more than 3,800 Palestinians and killing more than 75.

Benjamin Netanyahu, Israeli Prime Minister:

It's actually 150 or so what you would call juvenile delinquents.

On CNN last month, Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said it was Palestinians who attack Israelis in the occupied West Bank. Israeli officials speaking to "PBS News Hour"s cite more than 300 Palestinian attacks this May alone. And they argue they have also arrested dozens of settlers in the West Bank.

Last month, Netanyahu also vowed to reduce settlers' crime.

I think it's wrong on his own account. I don't want and don't accept vigilantism, and I don't care if it comes from Arabs or Jews. Our citizens cannot practice violence against anyone.

Danny Danon, Israeli Ambassador to the United States: We are not going anywhere.

But just this week at the United Nations, Israel's government vowed to expand Israeli control in the West Bank, referred to in the Bible as Judea and Samaria.

Jews have never been and will never be settlers in Judea and Samaria. The vast majority of residents in Judea and Samaria are law-abiding citizens. Those committing violence are tiny, a tiny fraction of the people living there.

Palestinians say the state and settlers are working together. Outside the Ridi family home last weekend, an Israeli soldier joined the settlers in prayer. On Tuesday, the Israel Defense Forces released a statement vowing that any soldier involved will -- quote -- "face disciplinary action."

And the statement described the settlers taking over land as -- quote -- "illegal, reprehensible, and unacceptable activity that harms the residents of the areas and disrupts their daily lives."

Yesterday, the IDF fired tear gas at the Israeli settlers, but the family remained besieged. So, last night, the U.S. stepped in. U.S. Ambassador to Israel Mike Huckabee issued a rare rebuke, labeling the settlers Israeli terrorists, and: "Actions by those who carried out this horrific act of terror meant to intimidate and harass this family are disgusting."

This morning, Israeli soldiers evacuated the Ridi family, although they have since returned to the house. The IDF also said it's reinforcing the area with an additional battalion and dismantled nearby settler outposts, which are illegal under Israeli law.

But the Ridi family provided the "News Hour" this video today, settlers still roaming outside the family home.

## News Wrap: Earthquake death toll in Colombia tops 260

Enrique Marin, Bogota, Colombia, Resident (through interpreter):

At the moment of the quake, she was at the door. She was in her pajamas and she was going to open the door. He said they found her with the keys still in her hands.

In the wake of the disaster, Colombian President Abelardo de la Espriella has declared an economic emergency. More than 150 aftershocks have been recorded since the main quake on Monday. The Colombian government says nearly 500 people remain missing, though that number is believed to be a vast undercount.

Elsewhere, millions of people in Europe are sweltering in their fifth heat wave of the summer. The U.K., in particular, recorded its hottest day of the year so far. The air in West London exceeded 100 degrees Fahrenheit, only several degrees short of the country's all-time record for August.

Seen under thermal imaging, this souvenir stand sizzled at over 60 degrees Celsius. That's 140 degrees Fahrenheit. The heat is exacerbating drought conditions across the continent, and crews are racing to contain a number of major wildfires, like these in Spain.

Here at home, nearly 20 million people across the Midwest were under flood alerts today as severe storms refused to let up.

And it's a downpouring. My car is completely done for. It ain't going nowhere.

Indiana neighborhoods sat in several feet of floodwater yesterday, and the National Weather Service warns that some parts of the state could see another four inches of rain or more.

Some homeowners say they've never seen anything like it, and their road to recovery is unclear.

Michelle Wilson, Cambridge City, Indiana, Resident:

I rush home, and I open the basement door, and it's all the way filled. And the backyard was flooded. My garage is flooded. I don't know when we can turn our power back on, if it would be safe to even turn it back on without our house catching on fire because it was sparking and stuff like that from the outlets downstairs.

Today, Indiana's governor signed a statewide emergency declaration. Over 250,000 homes and businesses remain without power because of the storms and tornadoes that have slammed this region.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth disputed reports that poor conditions aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln have led to low morale and even reported suicide attempts. He called those reports -- quote -- "completely misrepresented."

Two military-focused publications were first to report that multiple sailors aboard the aircraft carrier have attempted to jump overboard amid burnout and stress. The Abraham Lincoln has been on a nine-month deployment to the Middle East due to the ongoing war with Iran. But the Pacific-based aircraft carrier the USS George Washington has begun heading toward the Middle East to relieve it.

The board of the Kennedy Center has voted to move forward with President Trump's plan to close the building for two years of renovations. And it voted to add Trump's name back to the building's facade. A board member tells the "NewsHour" that the proposed renovation will include an inscription that reads -- quote -- "Restored and renovated by President Donald J. Trump."

Today's decision will be subject to approval from a federal judge, who has previously rejected both the addition of Mr. Trump's name and an earlier attempt to close the venue.

A federal judge in Boston has dismissed a Trump administration lawsuit against Harvard University that had accused the Ivy League school of turning a blind eye to antisemitism. Siding with Harvard, the judge today said the administration failed to show any institutionalized noncompliance with Title VI, which is the ban on discrimination in programs that receive federal grants.

It marks a blow to President Trump, who had tried to get the school to comply with a number of demands, including limiting admission of foreign students.

And on Wall Street today, the major stock indices were positive across the board. The Dow Jones industrial average inched up by nearly 70 points. The Nasdaq rose by 0.8 percent, while the S&P 500 closed at a new all-time record.
