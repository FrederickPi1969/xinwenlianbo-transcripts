## How exit polls are evolving as more Americans vote early

Well, on this Election Day, candidates and campaigns and all of us watching closely are interested in more than just how many people voted. We want to know who turned out, what motivated them to vote, and which issues mattered most as they cast their ballots.

Traditional exit polls are evolving, as the way people vote has changed.

Deema Zein takes a closer look at some of the data behind the election.

To know exactly who showed up to the polls and why they voted for a particular candidate, you have to ask the voters. This year, "News Hour" is getting some of that information from the voter poll. It's a new collaboration between the Associated Press and other news outlets to survey voters directly by phone, Internet, and in person.

To explain how it works, I'm joined by David Scott, V.P. of election services at the Associated Press.

So this is a new system this year, joining forces with broadcast networks after about a decade of developing and using your own system to measure the vote. Why?

We wanted to reach voters where they are. That's the most important thing. So if you're conducting a survey as Americans are casting their ballot, you need to be in all of the places where they cast a ballot.

We needed to be able to interview voters who are casting their ballots in person on Election Day. But, at the same time, most Americans vote in advance. And so we look at the voter file and look at who's cast a ballot, and then we try to interview them either online, by mail, by phone.

Whatever way that they would like to interact with us once we see that they have cast a ballot, then we're able to interview them, ask them questions about who they voted for and why.

So people are familiar with this term exit poll. This is different. Can we talk a little bit about how exactly?

Well, part of it is an exit poll, right? We are going to have thousands of people who are out in the 50 states interviewing people as they cast their ballot on Election Day.

But we also will interview people before Election Day, because people cast their ballots before Election Day. Really, what we have tried to do with our colleagues at the broadcast networks is design a methodology that reaches Americans. No matter how you cast your ballot, you have the opportunity for us to ask you questions, so we can understand the fullness of the American electorate.

And how many people are you talking to as part of this new process?

So, like a normal political poll that you might see in a state about a Senate race might be based on 500 interviews. We're going to aim for somewhere between 80,000 and 100,000 interviews taking place leading up to Election Day and then on Election Day itself. So it's an enormous survey.

And it's one of the things that we think is really important, that we're going to have results in every state where there's a statewide election this year. It's not just about the battlegrounds.

What does this help tell you guys at the AP?

We're looking for insights in the data about who has voted, what they cared about, what issues were resonating. We know which issues each candidate was talking about in a race, and so which ones were most important to voters.

We look at the demographics of a state and we look at the demographics of the electorate and see how that matches up. One of the things that we saw in 2024 was some of those demographic shifts that pointed early in the night to success for President Trump. You could see in the data about who was voting that he was poised to have a good night.

We were able to get a sense that this is where the election was going, and so those are the types of things that we will be looking at.

David Scott, vice president of election services at the Associated Press, thanks again for joining us.

## Former RNC chair Ronna McDaniel on GOP's political challenges

And now, for a Republican perspective, we're joined tonight by Ronna McDaniel. She chaired the Republican National Committee for seven years, including throughout President Trump's first term. She's now a campaign surrogate for Republican Senate candidate Mike Rogers in Michigan. And she joins us tonight from Lansing.

It's good to see you. Welcome to the "News Hour."

Great to be with you. Thanks for having me.

And we should say you chaired the RNC during President Trump's first midterm back in 2018. House Republicans then lost a net total of 41 seats. In your view, how does the political environment right now compare to then, eight years ago?

Well, I would also add that we picked up Senate seats in that first midterm, which was unprecedented to pick up Senate seats in a midterm.

Listen, in the first midterm of a sitting president, you typically lose seats in the House or flip a chamber. That's been the precedent for over 100 years. And when I was RNC chair in 2022, we actually flipped the House and won that back for Republicans under Biden. So that's what happens in midterms, and I think this is something that we're seeing this midterm.

I do think that the House probably will flip to Democrats this cycle.

Let me ask you this, though. As you look across the electorate, where do you see the greatest opportunities for Republicans? What are you watching most closely tonight?

Well, I'm in Michigan, so I'm looking at Mike Rogers. This is a Democrat seat that they have held. We haven't had a Republican in the Senate here since 1994. Mike has run a very good race. He has made inroads in traditionally Democrat constituencies, the Jewish vote, the Black vote.

He actually has a whole Democrats for Rogers coalition. And then we saw the endorsement of Haley Stevens, a sitting Democrat congresswoman come out and endorse Mike Rogers. So I think this is a seat that we're looking at very, very closely.

And looking nationally, you said recently that Republicans shouldn't be spending hundreds of millions of dollars defending a Texas Senate seat. How did the party find itself fighting for seats that should have been safely Republican?

Well, I don't know if I said they shouldn't be. I said it's concerning to have to spend that much, because that means you're not spending in races where you can pick up, right?

Like, if you're spending in a safe -- in a safe seat in a safe state, that takes away resources from potential gains. But, of course, we are battling in red areas right now. I do think we're going to hold the Senate. I do think Texas comes through, Kansas comes through. These are states that Donald Trump won by double digits.

And so, while I think we may lose the House, I think the Senate stays in Republican hands.

Republicans are asking to give -- asking voters, rather, to give President Trump two more years of unified Republican control. What would Republicans deliver that they haven't already?

Well, the Big Beautiful Bill did provide no tax on tips, no tax on overtime, no tax on Social Security. I think getting out of this Iran war, you will see gas prices go down, which I know is heavy on the minds of many voters across this country. Affordability is a huge issue.

And as you see the policies of deregulation come forward, things that Trump has enacted, I think you will see a better economy in the next two years. And he wants to complete that with Republicans. He may not have that opportunity.

I hope the Democrats don't go in and just go into lawfare and trying to attack Republicans in retribution, which we have heard our own Senator Elissa Slotkin say is on the agenda for them. I actually hope that we can work together and find ways to help the American people, because that's what they want.

President Trump will not be on the ballot again. Is the Republican Party prepared for a political future without him?

I think he will have a lot of say in the nominee for 2028. I -- he's not somebody who fades to the background very easily. The Democrats look like they're going to have something like 20 candidates running for president.

I don't think our field has shaped up yet. And I think whoever our nominees are or whoever's running, they're probably going to go and talk to President Trump first, and he will have a lot of impact in that primary because he does have huge sway with our base, specifically in primaries, and we saw that across this cycle as well.

President Trump, his approval rating is underwater. His numbers on the economy are even worse. They're in the 20 percent range.

When he tells voters to act as if he's on the ballot, is he helping Republicans or hurting them, in your view?

Yes, there are Republicans that just don't turn out, or there are Trump voters that just don't turn out unless he's on the ballot. So him saying that to his voters is key, because we have seen in past midterms -- and I know this personally as former RNC chair -- those Trump voters sit out without his name on the ballot.

So those are the people he's talking to. And Republicans are going to need those voters to turn out, traditional Republicans to turn out, and we are certainly going to need independents if we're going to win in states like Michigan, Maine, some of these battleground states.

In Kansas and Texas, I think him saying that does mean a lot to those Republican voters because he did win those states by double digits, and that is where he can really help.

Ronna McDaniel, former RNC chair, great to have your perspectives as part of our coverage. Thanks for being with us.
