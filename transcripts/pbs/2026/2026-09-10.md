## Iran-backed Houthis capture Red Sea port, putting more pressure on oil markets

Yemen appears to be sliding back toward all-out war after Houthi rebels backed by Iran seized the key port city of Mokha from allies of the internationally recognized government. Mokha sits on the Bab al-Mandab, a critical shipping choke point and gateway to the Red Sea.

Nick Schifrin has more on the escalating conflict, which is pushing oil prices higher and raising fears of a wider regional war.

In Yemen today, a Houthi advance that once again threatens a global fault line. Iran-backed Houthi fighters chanted death to the U.S. and Israel as they walked into a government building today and released these videos of Central Mokha, seizing Western armored vehicles, releasing prisoners held by Yemeni government allies, and on a joyride on the Mokha Airport.

This is territory that's been contested for more than a decade. For weeks, the fighting's been simmering. Saudi-backed Yemeni fighters have tried to hold the line. But they have been pushed back quickly, as the Houthis have captured a string of positions in Yemen leading to Mokha, which sits on Yemen's coast just a short distance from the Bab al-Mandab, the 20-mile-wide gateway to the Red Sea.

It was already one of the world's most important maritime choke points, more so since Iran has maintained its choke hold on the Strait of Hormuz. Already, the Houthis had been threatening Saudi ships, and they attacked key Saudi energy and military facilities across the border.

Saudi officials say the Houthi attacks have destroyed civilian homes and wounded more than 70 people.

Mohamed A-Bukhai, Houthi Spokesperson (through interpreter):

The fight for the west coast of Yemen had to be decisive. We are very keen to face the enemy directly. The next step would be to get into a dialogue that involved the political and leadership parties in Yemen.

I spoke remotely today to Houthi spokesman Mohamed A-Bukhai.

You're calling for dialogue, but are you planning to expand the military operation? Will you try and seize more territory alongside the Bab al-Mandab? And will you also try and seize Perim Island inside the Bab al-Mandab?

We don't need to actually take control over Bab al-Mandab or the island. Our missiles can reach any ships in the sea.

The Houthis and Saudis fought a nearly decade-long war that for the last four years has been in a tentative cease-fire. The current tensions spiked in July when Saudi Arabia bombed a Houthi-controlled airport to prevent an aircraft landing that the Saudis say was carrying Iranian weapons.

There are reports that there are IRGC members in Yemen helping direct or assist in some of these attacks. How much of this are you doing on behest of Iran?

There are no Iranian forces in Yemen. We actually depend on ourselves to manufacture all kinds of weapons, whether they are an AK rifle or a drone or even a hypersonic ballistic missile.

Tonight, the U.N. special envoy for Yemen, Hans Grundberg, warned, the new violence was dragging Yemen back into civil war and toward a regional conflict.

Hans Grundberg, U.N. Special Envoy for Yemen: This renewed war should alarm us all. It effectively marks the end of the relative calm that Yemen has experienced for over four years since the U.N.-brokered truce. It not only threatens to plunge Yemen into a prolonged and expanding war, but also risks yet again dragging the country into the wider and unresolved regional confrontation with global repercussions.

And, today, the Houthi advance pushed the price of Brent crude above $105 a barrel over concerns of disruption to global supply routes.

For perspective on the latest development in Yemen, we turn to Gregory Johnsen. He is a longtime Yemen expert currently with the Arab Gulf States Institute in Washington, D.C.

Gregory Johnsen, thanks very much. Welcome back to the "News Hour."

As I just mentioned, the Houthis have already been threatening Saudi ships going through the Bab al-Mandab. You heard the Houthi spokesperson there saying that their missiles and drones can cover all of the territory in the Bab al-Mandab.

So how significant is it that they have now captured the port city of Mokha? And I'm going to bring up the map here to understand its geography.

Gregory Johnsen, Arab Gulf States Institute:

Yes, thanks, Nick, and always a pleasure to join you.

It's an incredibly significant development in Yemen. So the Houthis, as you can see there on the map, had territory on Yemen's western coast. That was the north.But the Bab al-Mandab in the Red Sea sort of acts as a funnel, so it narrows down to about 19 miles across at its narrowest point.

That's a choke point for the Houthis. So by moving south and taking Mokha, the Houthis are actually able to project a lot more power into the Red Sea. So you have boats that are sort of congested there at the choke point. This gives the Houthis more targets to aim at. They say they're only going after Saudi oil tankers at the moment.

And it gives the ships less room to navigate. It also allows the Houthis, at least on the defensive, to then spread their mobile missile launchers, some of the drone launchers that they have out across more territory, more geography, which makes hitting those, which was already a very difficult task, even more and more difficult, as we're seeing sort of on the other side of the Arabian Peninsula in the Strait of Hormuz.

And you just referred to more territory. You heard the Houthis spokesperson claim that they don't want more territory. But it does seem like that they're pushing not only into Mokha, but down along the coast and could threaten these islands that are in the Bab al-Mandab, right?

I think that's what the Houthis are going to do. I mean, for a long time, for the past several years, they have been trying to move east inland to get some of the oil and gas reserves that Yemen has, because the Houthis, while they control a lot of territory in Yemen, they don't actually have sort of the economic base to survive long term.

What's changed recently is the Houthis looking at what Iran has done in the Strait of Hormuz and saying to themselves, we can do something similar. So, now instead of sort of pushing all their resources east toward Marib, where the Saudi air defenses and the Saudi air force was able to sort of push them back, now the Houthis are pushing their forces south along the coast to try to really take control of most of the Bab al-Mandab.

And the Yemeni national forces, the sort of anti-Houthi coalition, which was in Mokha, has withdrawn, but they have not withdrawn south. They have withdrawn inland, which means that the Houthis can continue their advance, and there's really not that much to stop them, at least on the ground.

So let's talk about how capable or not the Yemeni government and its allies are to be able to stop the Houthis.

A senior U.S. government official pointed out to me today that the U.S. had assumed that the particular militia guarding Mokha was actually one of the more capable allies of the Yemeni government. So what does it say that that militia wasn't able to defend Mokha?

Yes, there's a long backstory here, Nick.

So, the guy who is in charge of that force in Mokha is a guy by the name of Tareq Saleh. That name may sound familiar to some of your viewers because his uncle was the former President Ali Abdullah Saleh.

For the first few years of this war, from about 2015 to 2017, Tareq Saleh actually fought with the Houthis. It was only when the Houthis killed his uncle, the former president of Yemen, that he defected and joined this anti-Houthi coalition. That's one piece.

The second piece is, that late last year, Saudi Arabia really pushed the United Arab Emirates out of Yemen. And Saudi Arabia then basically tried to take all of the various militia groups that the UAE had been supporting in Yemen, one of which was Tareq Saleh's force, and bring them under the Saudi umbrella.

So, really, what's happening on the ground is that what we call the anti-Houthi coalition or these the Yemeni national forces or whatever term we want to apply to them, we think of them as one group. They're not one group. They're about eight to 10 different groups, all of which are riven with infighting and bickering.

They have diametrically opposed views about what -- the end state that they actually want to see in Yemen. And so it's not at all surprising, at least to me, and I don't think it should be to the U.S. official, that this group was driven out of Mokha.

They -- the Houthis are experts at exploiting some of these fissures and the fractures that exist within the anti-Houthi coalition. They have been doing it for over a decade now, and now they're putting their forces south, and they were able obviously to take the port city. And it looks like they're going to continue to advance farther south.

We heard the U.N. today warn that Yemen is headed towards civil war. At what point do the Saudis reenter this war? At what point does this become yet again a regional conflict?

Well, I think Yemen has been in a civil war for several years, and Saudi Arabia's been involved. Saudi Arabia was bombing targets in Yemen in July and in August as well.

The real choice that Saudi Arabia faces right now -- and we're in September of 2026 -- it's the same -- it's the same choice that Saudi Arabia faced in March of 2015, when they initially made the decision to go into Yemen.

And that is, they want to defeat the Houthis. They want to get the Houthis out of Yemen, or at least out of Sanaa, and put what passes for the internationally recognized government back in the capital. What Saudi needs to do that, though, is actual troops on the ground, because the Yemeni forces, as we have seen overnight with their loss of Mokha, aren't capable of defeating the Houthis on their own.

Saudi airpower isn't capable of defeating the Houthis on their own. So we need some foreign ground troops. But Saudi Arabia doesn't want to put foreign ground troops into Yemen for any sort of a long, bloody conflict with no guarantee of success.

And so they rely on the next best option, which is basically bombing and hoping. And that appears to be what it is that we're headed back to, where Saudi will carry out some airstrikes, the Houthis will absorb some airstrikes, they will still make some gains on the ground, and the problem itself won't be solved.

So, essentially, we're in a situation where the Saudis know what it is that they want. They know the objective, getting the Houthis out, but they are not willing to pay the price that it would actually cost to bring about that objective.

## A look at the young conservative movement a year after Charlie Kirk's killing

As Liz mentioned, one figure whose influence is still deeply felt at the Republican gathering is Charlie Kirk, the founder of Turning Point USA. His killing one year ago reshaped the young conservative movement he built and left his friends and political allies with the task of carrying it forward.

Today, Vice President J.D. Vance appeared on Kirk's former podcast to reflect on the future of the movement.

J.D. Vance, Vice President of the United States: This was a guy who just had so much life to live, who had so many people who loved him, and so many people who relied upon him, who depended upon him.

All of us, I think, have this hole that we're all trying to fill or all trying to at least find some way of making sure that we carry on in Charlie's absence.

For more on the future of Turning Point USA, we're joined now by Robert Draper, a "New York Times Magazine" reporter who profiled Kirk before his death. He continues to cover the young conservative movement and the fight over its future.

Robert Draper, "New York Times Magazine": My pleasure.

When you look at the conservative youth movement that Charlie Kirk built, what has changed the most in his absence and who, if anyone, is stepping in to fill that leadership vacuum?

I mean, I on a certain level, Turning Point USA is still the predominant youth organization, conservative or otherwise. I mean, it in fact added numerous chapters after Kirk was assassinated. A lot of young conservative kids on campus really felt inspired by Kirk and wanted to properly pay tribute to him by starting chapters of their own.

And they continue to raise a lot of money to host events. But certain things certainly have changed. I mean, no one, including his widow, Erika Kirk, can sort of really fill the shoes that Charlie Kirk walked in.

I mean, he was a generational talent. I mean, that's -- it becomes trite to say that, but in his case it's really true, not just because of his fluency as a conservative messenger, but also his deftness in being able to hold a very fragile coalition together and to help get out the vote, to raise money.

And he was a very trusted adviser to President Donald Trump. So it's -- so replacing all that with just one person is a pretty impossible task.

And, as you say, Turning Point USA has exploded in size. What exactly has it grown into?

Yes. I mean, it's still -- it's -- well, they're doing a couple of things.

So, first, they're growing more and more college campus chapters, but something Erika Kirk had told me she wanted to focus on, and which they're doing, is adding more and more high school chapters. So they're trying to get kids at an earlier age and sort of acculturate them to conservative values. And that largely explains the proliferation of the chapters.

And you have reported on how openly racist and antisemitic elements of the far right have gained traction among some young conservatives...

They haven't seeped into Turning Point. I think that is a battle that they have been fighting, not internally, but rather sort of waging a kind of conservative civil war over the hearts and minds of disaffected young males.

And there are some people who are Groyper-curious -- that's the name of Fuentes' movement -- who show up to Turning Point USA events. But, by and large, they have been very clear, Turning Point has, that -- and Charlie Kirk used to talk to me about this -- that we utterly reject Fuentes' virulent racist form of white nationalism, and so, kids who are interested in that, look elsewhere.

There's another tension here. Turning Point is growing, while some young voters who backed President Trump are growing disillusioned over affordability, the war in Iran, the Epstein files.

Are you seeing that frustration manifest in the campus movement?

Yes. Yes, I mean, now you're really putting a finger on, I think, the true war that's being waged here. It's not so much against one conservative flank versus another, another organization.

And so it's very, very hard. And I think the Iran war in particular has made it difficult for conservative groups like Turning Point to come to young people and say this is your kind of place after President Trump had talked so much about no more forever wars.

We certainly are seeing that sort of above in the ecosystem, in the influencer ecosystem, whether it's Tucker Carlson and Marjorie Taylor Greene, both former Trump loyalists who have fallen out of favor with him, or other individuals, that has not yet seeped into the sort of young conservative movement.

But there's no question that the conservative movement writ large is fracturing. I mean, it's -- and Charlie Kirk was someone who I would argue actually died trying to hold it together. It was already starting to fall apart while he was there. It still may be enough, by the way, to help them in the midterms and in 2028.

But that movement has really begun to be in tatters, and we will eventually see that in the youth groups as well.

And here is my promise. If the Republicans win the House of Representatives and the United States Senate, both of them, because of our economic, tremendous economic success -- in history, we have never had anything like what's happening.

I will issue a dividend to every adult citizen in the United States of America for $5,000.

It's a promise that could cost more than $1 trillion and had some Republicans questioning the price tag.

Texas Congressman Chip Roy writing on X that -- quote -- "Dependency is evil and soul-sucking in all its forms."

I think if a state actually tried to prosecute Trump, which seems very unlikely, that the courts would say that his activity is protected by the First Amendment because it's a campaign promise.
