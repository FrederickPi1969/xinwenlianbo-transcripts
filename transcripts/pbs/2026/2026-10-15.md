## Pennsylvania doctor urges vaccination as measles outbreak grows

The CDC has now confirmed more than 4,000 cases of measles this year as the country grapples with the largest outbreak of the highly infectious virus in 35 years. Nearly a fifth of cases comprise children under the age of 5, and more than 90 percent are among people who are unvaccinated.

Pennsylvania is the epicenter of the outbreak with more than 1, 100 cases. That includes five measles-related deaths and potentially a sixth still being investigated.

For more on what we're seeing and its impact, we're joined now by Dr. Evan Shirey. He's a pediatric infectious disease doctor based in Hershey, Pennsylvania.

Dr. Shirey, welcome and thanks for joining us.

So you're in Hershey in Central Pennsylvania, the middle of your state's measles epicenter. Just tell us what you're seeing at your hospital right now.

Yes, unfortunately, I have been seeing a lot of children and a lot of babies who have been admitted to our hospital and have been very sick. I am seeing the severe consequences that can come from this virus.

Tell us about those consequences. What are the measles like in those young children you're seeing? How does it present?

So there are a number of complications that can come about from measles. One that we really worry about is encephalitis, and that's when the virus causes infection of the brain. Children with encephalitis from measles in some cases may be unresponsive.

In other cases, they may be awake and aware, but confused. They don't know where they are. They're scared, in some cases, not even able to recognize their own parents. These cases are really, really tragic to see. We see infections where the virus infects the lungs or the airways, and we call that a bronchopneumonia.

These children might come in breathing really fast, breathing really heavily, gasping for air, in some cases needing different forms of respiratory support and oxygen, and in some of the extreme cases we have seen have even need to be put on a ventilator.

We have seen babies who have come in who are unable to nurse or unable to take bottles, and just all forms of various different kinds of suffering from the manifestations of these infections.

And, Dr. Shirey, do we know what's driving this outbreak, how it's gotten so severe in Pennsylvania?

So we know that, historically, the measles virus caused a lot of infections. We had three or four million infections every single year in this country, and that also resulted in 50,000 hospitalizations, around 500 deaths every year.

So we know this is really bad virus. We know the measles virus can spread like wildfire. It is famous for how infectious it is. One person with measles can infect up to 18 others. The reason that we have not been seeing measles cases up until now is because we had a very safe and a very effective vaccine that we had been using for decades that actually resulted in elimination of the virus from this country.

The virus is back because of a failure to vaccinate.

Given that we thought measles was eliminated about 25 years ago, is this something you ever thought you would be seeing during your time practicing as a doctor?

I spent a lot of time learning about measles as a medical student and as a resident and as an infectious diseases fellow going through all of my training. I always expected that measles was going to stay in the textbooks, and I did not expect that I would live in a world where I was in an ICU taking care of patients in real life.

Can I ask you about the rhetoric we're hearing from some of the nation's leaders? I mean, famously, the HHS secretary, Robert F. Kennedy Jr., has had some anti-vaccine views and vaccine skepticism he's expressed.

How does that complicate your job on the ground?

I think it's a real shame. I think that this outbreak just really highlights how important it is that, when we communicate about these important health issues, that we lead with honesty and the truth.

I think it's -- overall has been a shame that the anti-vaccine movement has gained the power that they have and that they have gained the influence in reaching ordinary Pennsylvanians. I see in my clinic when I talk to people about vaccines that the types of fears that people have over these last five, 10 years are really just a different dynamic from the way things used to be.

And I think that is largely -- unfortunately, it's related to the success of the anti-vaccine movement as a whole.

Whenever we cover these kinds of stories, I should mention to you we hear from people who say, look, if you or your children are vaccinated, you will be fine, so there's not much to worry about.

So, first and foremost, I want to be very clear that the vaccine is extremely safe and extremely effective. We have been using the MMR vaccine really in the same form since the late 1960s. So we have decades of experience with this. Billions of people have received this vaccine.

So I lead with reiterating the truth that the vaccine is very safe and very effective. For people who are vaccinated, I do want to give reassurance, if you are vaccinated, the vaccine is protective. And so the vulnerable among our communities have been relying for decades on the rest of us to be vaccinated at a high enough rate to achieve herd immunity.

One really important point that I can share personally, infants are an important group that can't be vaccinated. And I'm a father of a 2-month-old. Dr. Evan Shirey from Penn State Health joining us tonight.

Doctor, thank you so much for your time and your insights. We appreciate it.

The tense alliance between Ukrainian President Zelenskyy and President Trump is nearing a new low point tonight. After days of bitter back-and-forth between the two leaders, Ukraine has resumed strikes on Russian energy targets following three days of Ukrainian cease-fire.

The president had demanded an energy cease-fire this past Sunday between the two. Russia never stopped its attacks.

In Ukraine's capital city today, Russian strikes reverberate in broad daylight, that after Ukrainian officials say Russia launched a large-scale overnight attack, more than 850 drones. But Russia too is burning. The Associated Press reports Ukraine hit this oil refinery.

Volodymyr Zelenkskyy, Ukrainian President:

Three days, we didn't attack any energy, diesel and et cetera systems of Russia, and three days they attack us.

Late yesterday, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenkskyy told The Financial Times that Russia never abided by the cease-fire announced by Trump. Zelenskyy criticized the U.S. for limiting intelligence resources like Starlink. He was blunt. His patience is gone.

If they keep doing this, how much longer will you maintain the stoppage of attacks on Russian refineries?

His candor continued when asked about Trump's relationship with Russian President Vladimir Putin and their deal on diesel.

He speaks about it, I mean, openly and loudly that he loves such people and he respects such people, because, for him, I don't know, people who can be leaders 20 years, 30 years, 40 years, it's means they're very strong because they -- you can, I mean, hold the power.

How it can be when some people are surviving, fighting, dying, you make some business with the guys with such aggression?

For his part, Putin accused Ukraine of violating the truce.

Vladimir Putin, Russian President (through interpreter):

They made a first step. We started to respond by striking at energy facilities, which they are now squealing about. It was them who started it. They should have thought properly about consequences.

And Russia's foreign minister said Zelenskyy's comments point to a deepening rift between Kyiv and Washington.

Sergey Lavrov, Russian Foreign Minister (through interpreter):

The fact that this is such a direct and indeed crude challenge to President Trump is, in my view, something everyone already understands.

Zelenskyy spent his day at an E.U. summit in Brussels focused on support from his European allies.

Kaja Kallas, High Representative, European Union:

We definitely have different approach to this war. We see that we have to pressure the aggressor to stop the war, not the victim to surrender. Squeezing Ukraine is not the right approach.

The talks come as the entire region grows more war-weary. Two cargo ships were set ablaze today in Romanian waters in the Black Sea. Seven crew members are missing.

Nicusor Dan, President of Romania (through interpreter): There are attempts by Russia to destabilize societies and to reduce support for Ukraine. And our response is, we continue to support Ukraine.

European leaders also rejected Trump's push to control and release frozen Russian assets, he said, as a bargaining ship.

Friedrich Merz, German Chancellor (through interpreter):

These are assets that are frozen in Europe. And they should remain frozen in Europe. This is our business. And we decide on it.

And, as world leaders talk, the war grinds on, on the ground.
