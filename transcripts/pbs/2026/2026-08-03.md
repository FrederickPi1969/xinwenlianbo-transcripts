## What we know about the cyberattacks on water systems in 7 states

Well, according to the FBI, at least seven states have now been targeted in a growing wave of cyberattacks on critical water infrastructure across the country. Federal officials are reportedly investigating Iran as the likely culprit.

Liz Landers has the latest on this story.

Amna, the coordinated attacks follow urgent warnings issued last month by cybersecurity agencies who said Iran was actively targeting water and energy infrastructure nationwide.

In Minnesota, officials said at least 30 municipal water systems were hit. Utilities providers across the country are now scrambling to beef up their security.

For more, we're joined by Cynthia Kaiser. She is former deputy assistant director of the FBI's Cyber Division and a senior vice president at the cybersecurity company Halcyon.

Cynthia, thank you for joining "News Hour."

Cynthia Kaiser, Former FBI Cyber Deputy Director:

What do we know so far about these attacks? What kind of damage were they able to do and who is being targeted here?

Attackers have been breaking into the small computers that run America's water system. In a very small number of cases, there have been some disruptive actions.

Now, FBI and EPA have reported that utilities in at least seven states have known incidents that are of similar kind of severity, which shows just how broad this issue really is.

So far, federal authorities have not linked this to any specific actor. Who do you think is behind this attack?

I would be shocked if Iran wasn't behind this attack.

When I was at the FBI, we looked at several different aspects when we were thinking about attribution, especially initially in a campaign. One was, have we seen this type of targeting recently? The answer here is yes. Just a week ago, FBI and CISA, as well as a host of other U.S. government agencies, put out an urgent warning saying that Iran was continuing to target these mini-computers that control much of our critical infrastructure, including water infrastructure.

The second aspect we looked at on attribution was, do they have a motive to do it now? Is the attack actually for money or is it for disruptive actions? This appears focused on disruption, not money. And, geopolitically, the actor that has the main motive here is Iran.

And then, third, we would look at, who just has a longer history of doing these types of attacks and is willing to cross that red line of targeting critical infrastructure? The answer here also is Iran. Going back several years, they have conducted attacks against those these many computers at these facilities, including just kind of stopping at these devices and not going further, which is what's occurring in this operation here.

So we should all be moving forward assuming it's Iran, because there isn't really a plausible other scenario.

Why would Iran want to target these kinds of municipal water systems?

Iran wants to target these types of municipal water systems because they just want to get into anywhere. The main aim of these operations is to sow chaos, confusion and fear. It's to say, we got in somewhere and think about what we could do next.

So when you look at all of these different types of targets, they're going after the ones that they have the ability to get into, and oftentimes that is the smaller municipalities, the smaller locations' water systems because they lack the funding or the I.T. personnel to be able to fully secure them.

On Friday, the president blamed the state of Minnesota and its Governor Tim Walz for the attacks, calling them grossly incompetent and corrupt. Is that a fair criticism?

The FBI itself has noted that at least seven states have been impacted. And even since Friday, Michigan has come out saying that they have also been victims or at least targeted as a part of this attack. So it really is broader than the state of Minnesota.

Just how vulnerable is our country's infrastructure to these sorts of attacks, especially to the local utility systems?

The truth of the matter is that our -- especially our water facilities are very vulnerable to attack. And that should be scary because everybody depends on water.

When I was in the FBI and policymakers asked me which sector I was most worried about, it was water, because they lack the funding. They lack the I.T. personnel necessary. And they're run by these small municipalities. They just don't have that full resources, which means the attackers are able to get into something.

It may not be everything, but something within those systems. And if those systems goes down, we have a lot of problems across our communities.

The Trump administration has fired nearly a third of the employees at the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, CISA, and they're proposing to cut hundreds of millions of dollars more from the budget next year. That agency typically maintains those relationships between the state, local, and federal governments and some of the infrastructure of those various places.

Have those cuts impacted the United States' ability to counter these kinds of threats? Does this worry you?

What I really get worried when we're talking about cuts is not the big events. Whenever there's been a big event, historically, like, throughout all of these cyber operations, the FBI, CISA, NSA, everybody is able to pull together, push resources towards that and ensure Americans are safe.

But if there aren't additional personnel, what happens to the other types of operations that are happening every day? What do we miss? I think that's the first concerning aspect. But the second doesn't necessarily have to do with CISA at all. It's the need to renew the funding for state and local cybersecurity grants that all of these municipalities are dependent on to be able to have the right kind of cybersecurity.

It's with the Senate now to act, but it will lapse if it's not renewed by this fall.

Cynthia Kaiser, thank you for your expertise.

The officials add that the intestinal parasite disease is not usually considered life-threatening. The CDC has confirmed some 6,700 cases nationwide since early may, though Michigan alone has reported more than 11,000 suspected cases.

More than two dozen states, nearly all led by Democrats, are suing the Trump administration over its latest tariffs. Last month, the government announced double-digit levies on 60 trading partners, saying they'd not done enough to crack down on imports produced by forced labor. The new tariffs took effect just as prior ones expired.

In their lawsuit, the states argue the president exceeded his authority and is trying to replace tariffs that the Supreme Court struck down earlier this year. They're asking the U.S. Court of International Trade to halt the tariffs and refund duties paid by the states.

Officials in Southern Ukraine say Russia dropped eight glide bombs in a span of 90 minutes last night, killing at least one person and wounding dozens more. The attacks damaged residential buildings and a warehouse complex in the southern city of Zaporizhzhia.

Meantime, Russia says two separate attacks by Ukrainian forces killed at least nine people nationwide. Also today, Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy announced military cooperation deals with nine countries. And he continued to shake up his top ranks, dismissing Ukraine's ambassador to the United States and announcing the top negotiator, Rustem Umerov, would lead the foreign intelligence service.

Firefighters in Greece are battling a major blaze outside Athens for a fourth straight day. Strong winds returned to fan the flames, and a major highway was closed. Authorities say that areas around the city remain at high risk throughout the week with temperatures set to rise.

Water-dropping flights were able to resume today after a midair helicopter collision killed two crew members on Sunday. In the meantime, the U.K.'s weather service says a long heat wave and lack of rain led to the driest July in England and Wales in nearly two centuries. All of Wales and more than half of England are now officially in drought.

Mauricio Pochettino is staying on as coach of the U.S. men's soccer team through the 2030 World Cup. The 54-year-old signed a four-year deal after guiding the team to a record three wins in this year's tournament. The team fell to Belgium in the Round of 16, but managed to drive up enthusiasm for the sport nationwide.

Also today, the European soccer body UEFA threatened lawsuits against the international overseeing group FIFA for the now-canceled proposal to sell commercial stakes in the World Cup to private investors. UEFA threatened to boycott the World Cup if the plan went forward and has led calls to replace FIFA President Gianni Infantino.

The world's third-smallest country, the Pacific island of Nauru, has changed its name, in a return to its roots. The country's president says the new name, the Republic of Naoero, stays truer to its national language rather than Nauru, which was considered easier for foreigners to pronounce.

The country's international code will also change from NRU to NRO. And its people will now be known as the dei-Naoero, instead of Nauruan. It's the latest nation to change its name, joining countries like Turkiye and Eswatini, which is formerly Swaziland.

In movie news, "Spider-Man: Brand-New Day" now holds the box office record for the biggest opening weekend in North America. The latest installment of the web-slinging series brought in $360 million across the U.S. and Canada, topping another Marvel outing, "Avengers: Endgame."

Spidey also teamed up with "The Odyssey" to help set a record weekend overall for theaters. Despite years of decline in theatergoing, the movie industry is enjoying a strong 2026, with one estimate showing ticket sales up roughly 15 percent over last year.

She was the mayor of Fort Worth in the early 1990s before her election to Congress in 1996, where she stayed for nearly three decades. As a longtime member of the House Appropriations Committee, she focused on defense spending, including production of the F-35.

Lockheed Martin assembles the fighter jet in her former district. Granger was the first Republican woman to chair that committee. She didn't run for reelection in 2024 as her health declined. Granger's son said she died from Alzheimer's. Kay Granger was 83 years old.
