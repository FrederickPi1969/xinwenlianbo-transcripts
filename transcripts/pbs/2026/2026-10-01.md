## On the ground with UN peacekeepers as Lebanon-Israel mission nears its end

After 48 years, the U.N. peacekeeping mission in Southern Lebanon is nearing the end of its mandate.

Its planned withdrawal comes at a volatile moment, with the Israeli military occupying roughly 6 percent of Lebanese territory following years of fighting with Hezbollah and amid the broader fallout from the U.S.-Israel war with Iran.

Special correspondent Simona Foltyn and videographer Adrian Hartrick gained rare access with the peacekeepers traveling south of Israel's so-called Yellow Line.

We are embedding with U.N. peacekeepers headed towards Israel's occupation zone in Southern Lebanon.

This is one of the few ways journalists can access this border region where the Israeli military has been consolidating its control. We passed the Israeli checkpoint some four miles inside Lebanese territory, crossing the Yellow Line that demarcates Israel's areas of control.

The United Nations Interim Force In Lebanon, known as UNIFIL, was set up in 1978 to ensure the Israeli army's withdrawal after its first invasion of Lebanon. Israel ended up staying for 22 years, leaving in 2000. Now the U.N. Security Council has told UNIFIL to pack up, even as Israel has once again occupied parts of the country, seizing a strip of land along the border where the Iran-backed Shia militant group Hezbollah used to hold sway.

Along our route, the Israeli army has destroyed almost every village, pushing out the civilians who used to live here. there is no presence of the Lebanese state, only the Israeli army and UNIFIL. ## A look at Hegseth's attempts to reshape the Pentagon

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth says he is fundamentally reshaping the culture of the U.S. military, removing senior officers, dismantling diversity initiatives, and putting a new emphasis on religious faith and what he calls a warrior ethos.

The ideological clowns are out. The patriotic cowboys are in with testosterone testing on top.

For insight and analysis, I spoke earlier with Peter Feaver, a professor of political science and public policy at Duke University. He's also a specialist in civil-military relations.

Peter Feaver, welcome to the "News Hour."

Let's start with what we just heard. Secretary Hegseth says he's building a military that is colorblind, gender-neutral, and merit-based. But he's describing that vision with explicitly political and culture war language.

What happens when a defense secretary brings that kind of rhetoric into an institution that has traditionally tried to remain above partisan politics?

Well, it presents a test to the audience. They have to listen respectfully. He is their boss, of course, just like you see at a State of the Union address where the president's giving comments and the members of Congress cheer, but the Supreme Court and the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff sit quietly.

That's what the audience needed to do, and that's what they did do.

Hegseth has also moved aggressively against the senior officer corps. He wants to cut the number of generals and admirals by 20 percent. He's already removed a number of senior levels. And there is an argument that the military has become too top-heavy.

Is there merit to what he's doing, in your view?

I think that a lot of experts would agree that you could reduce the number of senior military three- and four-stars, and still maintain military effectiveness, provided that those reductions were done carefully, transparently, so people knew why the billets were going away and it was done gradually over time.

That's really the test, not whether there's a reduction, but whether it's being done wisely and transparently and with congressional buy-in.

Well, it's not transparent enough, I think. There's a lot of questions that were raised about the 30 or so senior military officers that have already been relieved.

Many of them were considered the finest in the force, including some that were most aggressive about modernizing and doing the kinds of initiatives that the secretary announced later on in his speech. And so it was hard to square what he was advocating in the second part of the speech with his claim that he got rid of the poor performers in the first part of the speech.

And for all of the attention on Hegseth's rhetoric, there is part of his agenda that you could argue is ultimately more consequential than that. He's creating a new four-star autonomous warfare command focused on drones, robotics, artificial intelligence.

## Investigators search for motive after alleged attack by FlyDubai co-pilot

Tonight, investigators across the Middle East are hunting for answers after a co-pilot stabbed his captain and allegedly attempted to crash a Flydubai aircraft bound for Israel with 174 passengers and crew on board.

The plane, traveling from Dubai, plunged 16,000 feet in roughly 60 seconds. Passengers were ultimately able to subdue the co-pilot after the injured captain, now hailed as a hero, managed to open the cockpit door. Today, President Trump said the attack could be linked to Iran, without providing evidence.

The suspect has not been publicly identified. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said this morning on FOX News that the suspect was an Omani national. But many major questions remain unanswered.

## What we know about Tennessee's botched execution of Christa Pike

Tennessee halted executions today after officials failed to carry out the lethal injection of death row inmate Christa Pike last night.

It was the second time this year that officials in Tennessee botched an execution attempt.

It's a tragedy. And it's deeply disturbing to me that that has happened in this state. The people deserve better than that. And we're going to make sure that happens.

Her attorneys say she remains alive and in critical condition after the state administered two doses of pentobarbital intended to execute her.

A media witness described the scene last night.

They went through two rounds and she was still breathing at the end. It was very ragged. She lifted her head up and was looking around at one point. She said that -- she said that there was a spot that was burning and hurting, and asked if that was normal.

None of it was normal. I covered all the executions last year and most of the ones this year. We haven't seen anything like it.

The state's Department of Corrections said in a statement it -- quote -- "followed every step of the state's lawful established execution protocol approved by the attorney general's office."

Pike was sentenced to death for the gruesome murder of her classmate Colleen Slemmer in 1995. Pike does not deny committing the murder as an 18-year-old, but her lawyers argued the state should have considered her age, mental illness, and trauma from alleged sexual abuse. Slemmer's family supported the execution and have called last night's failed attempt -- quote -- "a mess."

Professor, welcome to the "News Hour." Thanks for joining us.

Deborah Denno, Fordham University Law School:

As someone who's been following this case, let me just start with your reaction to the news that Christa Pike has survived this failed execution attempt.

Well, first of all, I was shocked. But then, after a moment, I wasn't surprised, given that this had just happened months before in Tennessee.
