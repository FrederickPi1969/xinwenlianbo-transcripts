## Epstein survivor criticizes Blanche, calls meeting 'a performative move'

The U.S. Senate is weighing President Trump's nomination of acting Attorney General Todd Blanche to lead the Justice Department, as questions continue over the administration's handling of legal investigations into sex offender Jeffrey Epstein.

It comes days after Blanche met privately with several survivors of Epstein's abuse.

Our justice correspondent, Ali Rogin, spoke with a survivor who attended that meeting.

Geoff, the meeting came as Blanche works to win Senate confirmation while facing continued questions about the Justice Department's handling of the Epstein case.

Politico is reporting today the White House is now actively lobbying two Republican senators seen as potential holdouts, Thom Tillis and John Cornyn, to support the nomination. A group of survivors who met with Blanche Thursday wrote an open letter to those senators today, urging them to vote against Blanche, calling their meeting with him -- quote -- "an hour of deflection, interruption, and gaslighting."

I don't know if we had extremely high hopes going into the meeting, but we really met Todd Blanche in hopes to work with him. We have been more than willing to work with the Department of Justice. We have been requesting meetings for months with Todd Blanche and the Department of Justice, and we have gotten nowhere until last week.

And it feels to us that it was a bit of a performative move so he could check a box for his promotion.

Can you expand on that a little bit? What about it made you come away with that impression?

Well, like I said, we went into the meeting really in earnest looking to work with Todd Blanche in the Department of Justice. We went into the meeting with an agenda, questions that we had that we wanted answered.

I think that Tillis wanted us to have a substantive meeting with Todd Blanche, and that's not what happened.

## Trump raises tensions with Canada by announcing new 50% tariffs

The Trump administration is imposing a new round of tariffs on Canada, accusing the country of unfairly discriminating against American autos, dairy products, and alcohol. The 50 percent tariffs would cover some $20 billion worth of Canadian imports on goods ranging from cement to hockey sticks. They're set to take effect in 30 days.

In the Oval Office today, President Trump defended the move.

Canada has been very, very tough on us over the years, for many years, and no other president's done anything about it.

I love Canada. I love the people of Canada. Many friends that live in Canada. But their leadership from Trudeau to the current leadership, I mean, in all fairness to them, they need us to survive. Without us, there's no way they can survive.

We're joined now by Natasha Sarin, co-founder of the Yale Budget Lab.

Natasha Sarin, President, Budget Lab at Yale University: Thanks for having me.

Yes, the important thing to understand about these tariffs, to put them in some context, is, they cover about 5 percent of imports from Canada. And so they're hugely important to the particular product lines that we're talking about here.

My colleagues at the Budget Lab estimate that's about 550 of them, things like dairy, alcohol, hockey sticks, as you mentioned. But in the aggregate picture of our trading relationship with Canada, this is one particular sliver.

## Southern Lebanese skeptical of Israel agreement as violence continues

When Lebanon's president, Joseph Aoun, met President Trump at the White House today, it was the first such visit by a Lebanese head of state in nearly two decades. It comes after Lebanon and Israel resumed diplomatic relations under the auspices of the Trump administration, signing a trilateral agreement last month meant to end a long history of wars.

But, as special correspondent Simona Foltyn reports from Southern Lebanon, the deal is facing stiff opposition.

The Lebanese government has agreed to make peace with Israel, but there's little hope for that here among the ruins of a war that has yet to stop.

We're in Nabatieh, once a bustling commercial town, now devastated by Israel's punishing aerial campaign.

Mohammed Awada, Nabatieh, Lebanon, Resident (through interpreter):

The shops are all gone, and Nabatieh, as you see, is mostly destroyed. With these strikes, they set us back 10, 15, 20 years for Nabatieh to return to what it once was.

Israeli troops have occupied the hills southeast of Nabatieh. Even as talks with Israel continue, the fear of renewed escalation hangs in the air.

With shovels and bare hands, Mohammed, his son Hadi and a nephew are trying to salvage what's left of their shop in the old market. Like much of the city, the Ottoman era buildings lie in ruins.

Hadi Mohammed, Nabatieh, Lebanon, Resident (through interpreter):

Israel are criminals who like to destroy. They don't want us to have a good country.

Many here do still support Hezbollah, in spite of the group's decision to reenter the regional war last March in support of its ally Iran. Mohammed is against Lebanon's deal with Israel, which he sees as a capitulation to Israeli and U.S. demands.

This agreement protects them more than it protects us. It's more in their than in our interests. They are playing games. Why haven't they withdrawn? What is their intention? Is their intention to take the south?

Israel currently occupies a six-mile strip all along its border with Lebanon demarcated by a Yellow Line similar to Gaza. This area accounts for roughly 7 percent of Lebanon's territory.

As per the agreement signed on June 26, the Lebanese government must first disarm Hezbollah, and only then will there be a -- quote -- "progressive redeployment" of Israeli troops. In Washington, the deal has been hailed as a success.

Nada Hamadeh Moawad, Lebanese Ambassador to the United States: The trilateral framework we signed today is a first step on the road to restoring Lebanese sovereignty and territorial integrity, securing a permanent and final cessation of hostilities, enabling our people to go back to their land.

But not much is left to return to. Israel has continued to raze border villages to create a depopulated buffer zone. Rights groups say such systematic demolitions could amount to war crimes.

Israeli attacks have killed dozens of people since the agreement was signed, according to Lebanon's Health Ministry. Esperanza Ghandour, a beloved kindergarten principal, is one of them. This is her when the kindergarten celebrated Lebanese Army Day, and here she is during last year's Christmas party.

She was killed on July 6 while on a quick trip to check on her house near Nabatieh. Her husband, Wissam Kanso, explains what happened.

Wissam Kanso, Husband of Israeli Strike Victim (through interpreter): Esperanza was driving. Her mother was next to her. The maid and the plumber were sitting in the back. The strike came from here directly at my wife.

None of the car's four occupants survived. Given Israel's advanced surveillance capabilities, Wissam finds it difficult to believe that a strike was a mistake.

The Israeli enemy knows every needle on the ground. They know her car. They know her plate number. They have the phone numbers. They have all the information.

Wissam feels that Lebanon's deal with Israel has come at the expense of justice for victims like his wife.

We haven't heard any condemnation for the killings, not from the president of the republic, not from the prime minister, nor from any official in this government.

To better understand why the school principal might have been targeted, we drove towards the strike location. Only a few cars make the perilous journey this close to Israel's Yellow Line. In describing the incident, the Israeli military said it had struck a vehicle that was approaching what it calls a security zone, posing a threat to its forces.

This is where Esperanza Ghandour was hit by an Israeli drone while on her way back from her village driving away from the front line. So it's difficult to imagine how she would have been perceived as a threat to Israeli forces, who are stationed over there.

At the entrance to the village flanked by billboards of Hezbollah's past and present leaders, we met the mayor of Nabatieh al-Fawqa.

Zain Ghandour, Mayor of Nabatieh al-Fawqa, Lebanon (through interpreter): Her house is about 400 yards away from here on the main road.

Zain Ghandour believes Esperanza's killing was intentional.

It's clear that this is a message to every civilian who wants to go to this area that they will target him. She wasn't just a school principal. She was a symbol of patriotism, a symbol of resisting the occupation, a symbol of dignity and steadfastness and clinging to the land.

The municipality has now erected a symbolic barrier to signal to civilians that it's not safe to go any further. Inquisitive young men on motorcycles occasionally whiz by. Hezbollah, like Israel, keeps a close eye on this area.

The village sits at the foot of the strategic Ali al-Taher Ridge, an embattled hilltop the Israeli military has yet to fully capture.

Why have the people of Nabatieh al-Fawqa not returned home until now despite the cease-fire?

This is the crux of the matter. There is no occupation here, and the people are supposed to go back. But the Israeli enemy is occupying these areas through the media and through fire from the sky.

The villages adjacent to the occupation zone have become a no-man's land with no people and no state.

The Lebanese state should take the political decision for the Lebanese army to deploy to the heart of the whole village to make people feel safe that the state is present.

We traveled further south to the village of Froun. It lies in one of two areas designated as so-called pilot zones. The Lebanese Army is building up a presence here.

This is one of the new Lebanese army positions that have been established to implement the Israel-Lebanon framework agreement and to ensure disarmament of Hezbollah in areas that the Israeli army is not occupying. Once Israel is satisfied with this process, it should redeploy from Lebanese territory.

But the agreement doesn't mention the word withdrawal, nor does it stipulate a timeline for it, raising fears of an indefinite Israeli occupation.

To people here, it seems as though Israel wants the Lebanese army to finish its job and disarm Hezbollah in areas it could not seize. The Israeli army is based on the other side of the valley, about 2.5 miles away.

Hassan Bazzi, Mayor of Froun, Lebanon (through interpreter): They chose Froun as a pilot zone because of the stupidity of the Lebanese state.

Israel is inside the Yellow Line, but they chose areas adjacent to the Yellow Line, which Israel couldn't take during the war, so they're trying to take during the cease-fire.

The mayor says there's no Hezbollah military infrastructure left in the village. But the group is deeply embedded in the social fabric of the south. Hezbollah's yellow flags are firmly planted in the ruins, a message that it's here to stay with or without weapons.

Posters thank fighters for their sacrifice.

Zeinab Shhadi, Froun, Lebanon, Resident (through interpreter):

Those young men protected us. If it weren't for them, Israel would have taken Froun and destroyed it like the other villages.

More than 200 homes in this village alone, including Zeinab's, were destroyed in Israeli strikes. This house belongs to one of her relatives.

My house was destroyed. During the 2006 war, it was also destroyed. We then bought another piece of land and built another house. We were so happy. Then this war came, and it was destroyed again.

So little is left of the village this war around that it's difficult to imagine how people can rebuild again. To many here, Lebanon's agreement with Israel feels not like a path to peace, but surrender.

For the "PBS News Hour," I'm Simona Foltyn in Southern Lebanon.
