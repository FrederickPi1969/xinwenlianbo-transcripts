## Exhibition reveals how summers by the sea shaped three great American painters

Now to a new exhibition that reveals how three great 20th century painters shaped the course of their own careers while on summer retreats together.

Correspondent Jared Bowen of GBH Boston takes us to Cape Ann in Massachusetts for our arts and culture series, Canvas.

They were three men escaping hot New York and off to chase summer at the seaside, the older seasoned painter and two young earnest ones, eager to learn from their mentor.

Oliver Barker, Director, Cape Ann Museum:

It's about the artistic process, and it's about a friendship that was so important to three titans of American art.

They were Milton Avery, Mark Rothko, and Adolph Gottlieb. For successive summers in the 1930s and '40s, they came here to Cape Ann on the North Shore of Massachusetts. In particular, they were drawn to Gloucester for its mix of natural beauty and the industrial grit of a fishing port. It was also an artistic rite of passage, says Oliver Barker, director of the Cape Ann Museum.

Artists colonies were established here and is still very vibrant today. 1932 was the similar year, when the Avery invited Gottlieb and Rothko to come here. By that point, Edward Hopper had come and gone through this city.

The museum's new show Avery, Gottlieb & Rothko: By the Sea is the first to examine the artists' time together here. While their New York kinship has been well-documented, this exhibition has been an excavation. Nearly half the works have never been shown publicly, and they're a revelation, says curator Eliza Rathbone.

Eliza Rathbone, Chief Curator Emerita, The Phillips Collection:

What's wonderful is, you can really feel them. You're with them. As Milton Avery's grandson said, this exhibition is as close to being there with them as we're going to get.

So here we meet the men and the men meeting each other.

I love these two sketches by Gottlieb, one of Rothko and one of Avery, because he absolutely captures them doing what they love to do. Rothko was a natural musician. Gottlieb has Milton sketching because Avery was always sketching.

Rothko described this as a golden time. There was no rivalry.

Rothko said something wonderful later on, and maybe towards the end of his life. He said, when we were young starting out, there were no galleries, there were no dealers, there were no collectors. He said, we had nothing to lose and a vision to gain.

Avery at 47 was 18 years older than the younger artists and had been coming to Cape Ann since the 1920s. A decade later, Rothko and Gottlieb literally followed in his footsteps. Here on holiday and far away from the grit of their New York lives and studios, they would regularly set out in the morning side by side to sketch or paint.

There was no imitation, but plenty of conversation.

We know they certainly got together every evening, pretty much, in the summertime. They all share the same subjects, but they treat them differently.

Including the wharves, the fishing, and of course, the beach, where Rathbone says Avery instilled in Rothko and Gottlieb the confidence to strip away the inessential. In his watercolors, Rothko went even further, his bathers, like Cezanne before him, bask on a more ethereal beach.

There's a kind of intimacy which is perhaps unexpected. And how does he do that? How does he express so much feeling in works that are so spare?

Whether Avery was painting the blazing beach sun or the Gloucester Harbor at night, reunited here for the first time with a study Rathbone discovered in Rothko's own collection, his artistic voice as the elder statesman remained consistent.

But the younger men evolved over the summers. Gottlieb tread into seaside surrealism.

We have a series of these paintings of boxes on the beach that Gottlieb did where he puts these unrelated objects into the sections of the box. And there's definitely a lot of surrealism in the ideas that lie behind these paintings.

By the 1940s, Rothko's works also changed. He began taking more wild, expressive turns, rooted in his appreciation for Greek mythology and the writings of Carl Jung and T.S. Eliot. As the younger men marched toward abstract expressionism, Avery's work settled into great studies of spareness.

The show ends in a gallery that doubles as a temple to these titans, a room filled with monumental late career work, the canvases that would solidify the legacy of each.

I'm just bowled over by the beauty of this room. It almost makes me want to weep. There's a peacefulness here and a kind of sense of fulfillment of all those very hardworking years that they spent, but years when they had nothing and came to the full realization of what they were about.

Years when time by the ocean offered a break from regular life, where the was joy, and where a mentor delighted in encouraging his younger friends to take up the mantle of American art.

There's a wonderful statement that Mark Rothko made as a eulogy of sorts at the passing of Milton Avery in 1965.

He speaks about Milton Avery as a true poet, a poet that has withstood many different transitions and changes in fashion. And, to me, there's a timelessness in that gallery.

Where we now see how many a day at the beach helped change the tide of artistic ascent.

For the "PBS News Hour," I'm Jared Bowen in Gloucester, Massachusetts.

Liz Landers has more on the efforts some advocates are taking to hold politicians accountable.

Cheyenne, thank you for joining the program.

## What is postpartum psychosis? The condition at the center of Lindsay Clancy's murder trial

The murder trial of Massachusetts mother Lindsay Clancy has brought renewed attention to mental health issues, and specifically a condition known as postpartum psychosis, a rare but severe mental health condition.

Lindsay Clancy is on trial for killing her three children in 2023. Her now ex-husband found the children strangled in the basement and Clancy injured from an apparent suicide attempt. All the children were under the age of 5. And Clancy is now paralyzed from the waist down.

Her defense lawyer says she was experiencing postpartum psychosis, which can cause delusions. But prosecutors are charging that she acted intentionally.

Shanan Buckingham, Massachusetts State's Attorney:

This was a woman who acted intentionally, rationally, and swiftly to accomplish a very specific goal, to kill.

Kevin Reddington, Attorney For Lindsay Clancy:

This is a woman that did not get the medication, did not get the medical treatment that she so deserved. This was a woman that was suffering from psychosis at the time that she went down to that basement.

Postpartum depression is not uncommon, but postpartum psychosis affects only about one in 1,000 women.

For more, we're joined now by Dr. Julia Riddle, a psychiatrist who focuses on reproductive mental health at the University of North Carolina Center for Women's Mood Disorders.

Dr. Riddle, thank you so much for joining the "News Hour."

How much do we know about postpartum psychosis and who it is most likely to afflict?

Dr. Julia Riddle, UNC Center for Women's Mood Disorders: Yes, thank you, Stephanie.

The truth is, we don't know enough. We need so much more research and so much more attention. But what we do know, which we have gotten from some registry studies, some Danish registry studies, as well as our own research, is that the people that are highest risk -- as you mentioned, so it's about one to two in 1,000 -- highest risk seems to be associated with bipolar disorder, because this is an affective disorder, which means a mood disorder.

So women with bipolar disorder or a history of postpartum psychosis are at the highest risk. But it's not everybody with bipolar disorder, and there are women who their first episode with any mental health condition will be in the postpartum.

So other indicators for that may be a difficult delivery, losing a lot of sleep, OK, or having family members, so family members that have had postpartum psychosis or a family history of bipolar disorder, going back to that being the highest risk. So we don't always know who they are, OK, and we don't always know exactly who to follow, but those are some of our indicators, which does make it a really difficult disease to prevent.

Describe the typical experience of a woman having an episode of postpartum psychosis.

So, first and foremost, right, people never read the textbook, so I will do my best to describe this. It is considered usually rapid, so very, very soon after delivery. So, a woman gives birth, and usually it's within the first few weeks, family will notice a change. They're acting odd. They don't seem with us. They're a little more detached, or they're more irritable and agitated.

So it often looks like confusion, an agitated state or a manic state, so extra energy, not sleeping being one of the most worrying signs. So you tell them to go lay down and they cannot sleep. You find them pacing. You find them moving things in an odd way. It doesn't quite make sense.

This often gets so normalized because, if it's the first child, who knows what it's supposed to be like? So, irritability, excitability, or it can look like a deep and detached depression. When it starts to get really scary, and we often talk about this in what we do, it's almost as if a vacancy comes over them.

And this is often when we see a loss of reality. So when there these delusions start, delusions that the world is not safe, delusions that they have to do something to change things for themselves, this is when we start to really worry about thoughts of suicide in particular, and sometimes when some of these infanticide thoughts can come up.

So it can progress, but usually it's this rapid change in the way the woman behaves. But often she's not sharing it. So people are kind of watching and wondering.

It sounds terrifying, but isn't it also true that most women who have this do not exhibit violence?

That is absolutely true. And I really want to put a point on that, OK?

In the media right now, the number that's getting quoted is 4 percent. So we're talking about one to two in 1,000 women after birth having postpartum psychosis and then 4 percent of that. That's actually a misattribution of a citation.

What it is looking at with that, what that paper was looking at was actually only women with a depressed version of psychosis, so they got depressed, so depressed that they became psychotic. And that was the group at 4 percent risk. So we're not even talking about that one to two out of 1,000. We're talking about a small, small, small group.

So it is rare. This is a treatable illness. When this gets recognized and treated, women get better, and the vast majority, the vast, vast majority are not violent.

During Lindsay Clancy's trial, a handwritten log she kept was presented, Dr. Riddle. She lists several psychiatric medications in this log and wrote at one point: "Awake all night with suicidal ideation."

She was apparently aware that she needed medical intervention. How challenging is it for patients to find a psychiatrist who can properly diagnose and treat postpartum psychosis?

This is not part of psychiatric training, N.P. training, P.A. training, psychology, or social work training. This is not something that is currently required as part of our education. So I sit on the board for the National Curriculum on Reproductive Psychiatry, because we know people are hungry for this education, but it is not required.

So it is hard for a patient to know when they're seeing someone that has the appropriate training. But it's also hard as a trainee or someone practicing to get these -- to get this training. So if we're not finding the right diagnosis, we're not providing the right treatment, and that's a real problem.

Thankfully, these tragedies are very rare. But what do you hope comes out of these discussions, not only on postpartum psychosis, but on postpartum mental health?

What I really hope for is better training for both -- for our professionals. I also hope for better recognition, reduction of stigma, and really more investment in our research. When we look at the research that goes into women's health, it is 6 percent of the investment.

Women deserve so much more, because, when we can get women and families better, right,we get mom better, we take care better of baby family, the whole family unit, and that has reverberations into the community and honestly into the economy. So these things matter tremendously.

What I also want people to know is that these are treatable illnesses, whether it's postpartum depression, postpartum anxiety and OCD, or postpartum psychosis. There are treatments and people do get better.

That is psychiatrist Julia Riddle with the University of North Carolina.
