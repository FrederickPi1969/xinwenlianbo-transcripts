## State lawmakers seek greater control over what universities can teach

Newly elected Democrats in Texas will head to Austin with the kind of power they haven't had in decades. One of the many questions facing Governor-Elect Gina Hinojosa is whether Democrats will try reversing big changes made at public universities in recent years by state Republicans.

Those laws, which match some of President Trump's demands and also predate them, have put restrictions on what can be taught and more. Texas is not alone. More than half of all college students are enrolled in a state with at least one law limiting what can be taught or how campuses can operate.

Stephanie Sy reports on the impact in Texas for our series Rethinking College.

Martin Peterson, Former Texas A&M University Professor:

When philosophy professor Martin Peterson arrived at Texas A&M in 2014, he says he approached his job the way he always had.

I have been teaching applied ethics for decades, and I was entirely free to design my own syllabus. After a couple of years, I started to teach another class, contemporary moral issues, and that's the class that eventually led to some trouble.

The trouble began as Peterson prepared to teach that class in the spring semester. According to the course syllabus, contemporary moral issues challenges students to understand articulate, and evaluate arguments from multiple points of view.

And among the assigned readings, an over-2,000-year-old text from the Greek philosopher Plato.

Plato is one of the most important philosophers of all time. He, in a sense, invented Western philosophy.

The reading from Plato's influential dialogue "Symposium" explores the nature of love and includes a speech by ancient playwright Aristophanes.

Aristophanes discusses same-sex relationships and also suggests that at some point in the past there might have been more than two genders, the myth of a split human. Whether this was Plato's own view is as always for Plato a little bit unclear, right?

## Newsom plans to commute all of California's death sentences

California Governor Gavin Newsom announced the largest mass commutation in the country's history, changing the state's entire death row population sentences to life in prison without parole. The decision could grant clemency to 548 people.

Newsom, who is considering a run for the presidency, wrote in a letter to constituents that -- quote -- "State-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California's values, undermines the foundations of our legal system, and diminishes each of us."

And Governor Newsom very publicly wrestled with this decision, taking the time to first consult with victims' family members and corrections officials, judges, lawyers, and many others. He also looked to the research and the evidence that revealed that the death penalty in California was a very costly, deeply flawed policy choice.

And I think all of that careful work and consultation led to today's decision.
