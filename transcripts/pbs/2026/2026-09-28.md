## Jessica Tarlov on navigating political differences and her new book, 'I Disagree'

Jessica Tarlov has made a career out of disagreeing, often while being outnumbered.

As the liberal voice on FOX News' "The Five," she spends her days debating people who see the world very differently than she does. And in her new book, "I Disagree: Winning Arguments Without Losing Friends," she argues that disagreement doesn't have to mean disdain, and that knowing how to talk to people across political divides is a skill we need now more than ever.

Jessica Tarlov, welcome to the "News Hour."

Jessica Tarlov, Author, "I Disagree: Winning Arguments Without Losing Friends": Life aspiration fulfilled now to be here.

So, look, on "The Five," you are the liberal voice at this table where you are outnumbered four to one. What has that taught you about the difference between winning an argument and persuading someone?

It's taught me a lot about patience, first and foremost and one of the big philosophies of the book, which is knowing when to listen and when to talk.

And being able to engage oftentimes silently as someone is doing their thing and putting some facts or perspective out into the ether that I vehemently disagree with and that I'm jonesing to pick apart has made it easier for me to be able to do this job, to understand people that think differently than me, and to be able to rebut it, I think, more effectively.

So, persuasion, very definition of what that means, for me, this job is about opening people's minds to the idea that there's an alternative way of looking at a certain subject. And the listening part of it is the crucial component.

What do you understand differently now about conservatives or a conservative viewpoint that you didn't fully grasp when you started at FOX?

I grew up in New York City, a very liberal family, school with all liberals. I didn't spend a lot of time with conservatives until I went to college, which I think is a lot of people's experiences who grow up in these kinds of enclaves.

And having exposure and working closely with people whose conservative values are rooted in the way they grew up, in their religion, traditions passed down across generations has been illuminating for me. It hits different if you're talking about something like reproductive health care and the person you're debating or even just chatting with is a pro-life person because of their religion.

And you also can't win that argument in the same way that you might be able to with someone else. Working with so many veterans, for instance. I have a new take on gun culture from being around people who grew up with guns, hunters, conservationists, all giving me a bigger perspective, I think, on the way that the country works. So that's one aspect of it.

And then the other is that -- and it's frustrating for liberals, but I have a front-row seat to the fact that they don't take politics as seriously as liberals. And I know that that is this massive land mine for people on the left, where you just say, how is this possible that you don't care, right, or what you're allowing to slide, and how can you just sit there and accept something like this?

If you're -- we're debating immigration, for instance, and we're talking about real tragedies like what happened to Alex Pretti or Renee Good, and somebody says, well, it the larger picture is what matters to me, and we're moving in the right direction. They shouldn't have been obstructing traffic. Or this is what happens when you leave the border open during the Biden administration.

And that's hugely annoying to me and frustrating. But they are not taking every bit of the story as seriously as the left is, and it is allowing them to focus on what they see as the bigger picture or the bigger project. And I didn't understand that before I started working for them. I thought that they were actually just deeply unserious about politics. They're just longer-term-minded.

You're fairly tough on Democrats in this book. You say that they too often don't really inhabit conservative spaces, that they can be scolding, that the party has lost some of the joy and cultural connection that politics requires.

What do you think Democrats misunderstand about the people they need to win back?

That those people are also not taking politics as seriously as they are, that they are consuming media that they are oftentimes not interested in engaging in. That was one of the main findings of every autopsy there was from the 2024 election.

And you see Democrats dabbling more showing up. A lot of them are coming on FOX News. And the way that I found that politics and the politics of persuasion works is when you are in a long-term relationship with the people that you're talking to and with their audiences, and when then they can really feel like you're trying to be part of their world.

And most of the time, they want you to show up and not actually be talking about politics, which we're still not great at. Democrats, we like football too, and all of the stuff that we get made fun of. But showing up on things like "College GameDay" and making sure that you're going to those events, you see the reception that like a Wes Moore or a Josh Shapiro gets when they go out and do those kinds of things.

And I think that is still missing from the recipe. I worry for the midterms, even though the polling looks great at this moment, and I know the country is in tremendous economic crisis, and folks, they know what it costs to fill up their tank, they know what groceries cost, but the results won't necessarily be an affirmation of the Democratic Party as a great option.

It might just be that the other side looked that much worse at this moment. And I wanted the book to emphasize a path forward for the party, which I have been thinking a lot about in terms of policies that will move us forward, but also what leadership looks like and joy.

You write about the emotional toll of being on the receiving end of attacks from President Trump. Is there a point at which engagement stops being worthwhile?

Well, engaging with him, I think, is not worthwhile. And that is definitely my policy. There's nothing really to say when the leader of the free world thinks that he should be watching "The Five", instead of overseeing the dropping of bunker-buster bombs on the Fordow nuclear enrichment complex, which is when he decided that he really needed to tell me how much he hated my jittery presentation.

But the big lesson that I took from the several times that he's come after me, or viewers, or when I get criticism even from people that I know in my real life is that oftentimes they're going to be focusing on the style of how you're doing something, like, I would have done it this way, or you're too much of this, you're not enough of that, you're unendurable voice, which was I think it must have been Steven Cheung who wrote that, because it doesn't sound like Trump.

But they focus on your style when they can't deal with your substance. And that has made me a lot stronger in my passion for what I do and the kind of advocacy that I feel like I'm putting out there to an audience that might not necessarily have a ton of access to someone who thinks like me.

And I wanted to make sure that that was an important thread of the book, because I especially want folks and younger women to understand the workplace can be quite rough-and-tumble. And people are going to criticize you and come after you. And if you can find a way to internally process that and unpack what their criticism actually means, you can succeed and just feel much better about yourself on the other end.

But it's also OK to cry, which I have in the book too.

The book is "I Disagree: Winning Arguments Without Losing Friends."

Jessica Tarlov, it's great to have you here.

## WFP official on addressing global hunger in face of wars and climate disasters

The United Nations recently announced a new executive director for the World Food Program, who is by tradition nominated by the U.S.

Luke Lindberg was a Trump administration Agriculture Department official, and he inherits one of the U.N.'s toughest jobs, made tougher seemingly every year by wars and climate change. Hundreds of millions around the world go hungry every day, and feeding as many as possible is a monumental task.

The man serving as the acting director of the WFP spoke recently from the U.N. General Assembly with our Nick Schifrin.

The World Food Program is the world's largest humanitarian agency, and it is facing challenges that are only increasing across the globe.

Some 266 million people face acute food insecurity because of conflict, weather and economic hardship. WFP is providing assistance to more than 100 million people across 83 countries, but faces serious funding shortfalls, just like much of the humanitarian world.

The WFP's acting director is Carl Skau. And he joins me now from New York.

Thanks very much. Welcome to the "News Hour."

Let me start where the world's largest humanitarian and food crisis is, which is in Sudan. Nearly 20 million people face acute hunger there. There's famine in some areas. We're, of course, in the fourth year of civil war in Sudan, but how much is the crisis still due to the difficulty of exporting out of the Strait of Hormuz because of the Iran war?

Carl Skau, Acting Executive Director, World Food Program:

Well, basically, everything we see playing out at the global level in terms of conflict, these disruptions to maritime trade and climate change, is playing out in Sudan.

I was just there before coming to New York with my frontline teams. I was in El Obeid in Kordofan, which is now the epicenter of the fighting, saw hundreds of women arriving with their children from villages that had been destroyed only a few days before. So the war rages on there, and displacements continue.

But on the markets in Khartoum and elsewhere, also prices are coming up because of the disruptions to maritime trade. I mean, Sudan is on the Red Sea, and now the escalation in Yemen is causing big issues for shipments to come in.

You mentioned you recently visited some of the communities that have been hardest hit, especially by violence committed by the Rapid Support Forces, the anti-government militia there. Tell us about some of the victims of that violence. Who did you meet? What did they tell you?

Well, I went to El Fasher, which is this city that last year was besieged for 18 months. The people there suffered through terrible atrocities, unspeakable atrocities. Famine was confirmed last year.

And when I visited, it's a completely destroyed city. You drove street after street was empty and destroyed homes, very few people, very little economic activity, a couple of stands, no markets. There was this exodus of hundreds of thousands of people towards the end of last year. They are now caught in that massive -- in the camp of displaced people, mostly women and children, now about 960,000 of them, living in this ocean of grass huts.

There are no basic services, no health care, no education, no water and sanitation. And so we failed them last year of not protecting them against these atrocities, and we are, frankly, failing them again by not being able to provide not even the basics that they need to survive.

We have seen funding crises all over the world for humanitarian agencies, not only in Sudan, not only in WFP. The executive director, Cindy McCain, who just left, told me earlier this year that the U.S. funding cuts could cause an additional 58 million people to face starvation.

Is that still the impact of the cuts to USAID some 18 months after they were made?

Well, it's not only the U.S. I mean, there are cuts across. WFP lost about 40 percent of its budget from '24 into '25. So it's fundamentally inadequate. We have about half of the money that we have asked for, but it's also very uneven.

Some contexts, like Afghanistan, like Somalia, it's severely underfunded. And there we're moving in Somalia's case towards a famine-like situation. In Afghanistan, we have probably the worst malnutrition crisis in the world.

One of the places where it seems like you are getting a more generous response from the international community is Nepal, which, of course, is still grappling with the devastating impacts of last month's floods.

You have said that this is a case where the international community response has been timely and generous. How so? And why is that the case in Nepal?

Well, there was a lot of attention. I mean, it was dramatic scenes, of course, and pictures that came out of Nepal. And so there was expression of solidarity coming from different sides.

And we were able to help the Nepalese government to respond. We have been providing these specialized trucks that can make it through even if their -- the roads have been destroyed. We have also supported with helicopter capacity. And we have now also deployed a new capacity with drones, where we are bringing food and other commodities.

I think it's an example of where WFP has stepped in, in support of government, providing that logistics capacity to be able to reach those who have been closed off by this crisis.

You visited Gaza earlier this year. And there are new findings that show modest, but very fragile improvements in Gaza in food security and child nutrition.

But 1.4 million people -- that's more than two-thirds or so of Gaza -- still face crisis hunger levels. What happens to Gaza if assistance isn't increased?

I think it's important to say that there has been progress on the food security, and -- but that is only thanks to the fact that WFP has been able to deliver to the entire population basically every month since the cease-fire started.

But, indeed, this is fragile. I mean, should we not have the funding to continue -- and, in October, actually, we are hitting a cliff, where we might need to have to cut to about half the population, 1 or 1.1 million people. And that is going to quickly then reverse these gains on food security.

But I think, even more importantly, other sectors, like water sanitation, shelter, health care, has not seen the same progress. And so life in Gaza is absolutely brutal still. We need to move forward with early recovery. Even if the political and security processes are stuck, we need to be able to provide for more dignified life.

I wonder what your message is to the world, frankly, that has not stepped up the way it needs to about why food security is so important.

Frankly, it's unacceptable that children go to bed hungry, and I think there is broad agreement about this.

But it's also about stability and security. Food security is national security. When people face hunger, they have three options, basically, starve, migrate or rebel. And so while WFP is engaged in this for the humanitarian cause, there is clearly also a case of self-interest.

Carl Skau, the acting director of the World Food Program, thank you very much.

## How Blanche is shattering DOJ's standard of political independence

Attorney General Todd Blanche is drawing scrutiny for his recent political appearances. with just five weeks left until the midterms, Blanche is blurring lines meant to keep the DOJ free of partisan bias.

Attorney General Todd Blanche has been introducing himself to some unusual crowds, like at the Republican Party's midterm convention.

To evil men and women in this country, know this. President Trump will hold you accountable.

An event in New York where he touted the Republican gubernatorial candidate, Bruce Blakeman.

We just heard from our great Nassau County executive, Bruce Blakeman, who is doing everything we need him to do to back the blue, to support law enforcement, and to make this county -- and to make this county a safe county that we can all raise our kids in.

And a North Carolina rally where he praised GOP Senate hopeful Michael Whatley.

I have been put in an impossible position. I have to follow the great leader you just heard from, Michael Whatley. But I am also the last speaker before you hear from the greatest president of our generation, President Donald J. Trump.

Ethics experts say this message delivered by this messenger shouldn't be happening.

Joseph Tirrell, Former Justice Department Official:

Having the senior law enforcement officer in the United States campaigning on behalf of candidates creates the appearance, if not worse, that the Department of Justice is not going to fairly enforce election law.

Joseph Tirrell was the DOJ's top ethics official until he was fired last year by Blanche's predecessor, Pam Bondi.

What would you be advising Attorney General Blanche to do if he were still working within the Department of Justice?

The same thing I used to advise former attorneys general before I left the Department of Justice, which is, it's inappropriate for you to make these kind of statements on behalf of any candidate.

Tirrell says Blanche may be violating the Hatch Act, a federal law limiting certain political activities of government employees. He wouldn't be the first to do so, nor are violations exclusive to one political party.

During the Biden administration, Massachusetts U.S. attorney Rachael Rollins was found to have engaged in among the most egregious transgressions of the act ever investigated, attending a political party fund-raiser in her official capacity, and then attempting to sabotage a political candidate by leaking that he was under DOJ investigation. She resigned as a result.

That same year, Attorney General Merrick Garland tightened the DOJ's restrictions on political activity, saying non-career appointees couldn't attend any partisan activity in any capacity. Bondi later loosened those rules.

Claire Finkelstein, University of Pennsylvania: It's very unlikely that any of these Hatch Act complaints will result in any sort of enforcement.

Claire Finkelstein is a law and ethics professor at the University of Pennsylvania.

The office that determines whether or not a Hatch Act violation has occurred is supposed to be independent. But with this administration, there are -- there's fewer and fewer truly independent agencies that can act without influence from the White House.

She says Blanche has also blurred the lines by briefing the press from the official White House podium recently, weighing in on issues of political importance to the president, like voting by mail...

There should be nothing surprising about what we're trying to do when it comes to mail-in voting.

Every Democrat senator suddenly races to a microphone to talk about AI after being eerily silent for months and months and months. So to talk about the timing of this as being nothing but an effort to influence the midterms, I shouldn't have to say that out loud.

Blanche has long teetered on the line between official and political. He began working with Trump as his defense lawyer after the president's reelection loss and has continued to vigorously defend him in office.

In a statement, the DOJ said: "Attorney General Blanche's appearances at political events are made in his personal capacity. His appearances are reviewed and cleared by the department's ethics officials." It also noted that he did not engage in political fund-raising.

Tirrell says attorneys general should be held to a higher standard.

Even with a disclaimer that you're speaking in your so-called personal capacity, the office of the attorney general is just too well-known and too important to create even the appearance of impartiality or impropriety, and attorneys generals should not be speaking on behalf of any candidate in any partisan election.

So I am the last speaker before the main event, President Trump.

## High mortgage rates compound housing affordability challenges

The average rate on a 30-year mortgage is back above 7 percent for the first time in 20 months. It's rough news for the housing market and for homebuyers already struggling with rising prices elsewhere.

And it comes as treasury yields, which impact borrowing costs across the economy, hit multidecade highs and after inflation fears forced the Federal Reserve to hike interest rates for the first time in more than three years.

To help us understand what all of this means, we're joined again by David Wessel. He's director of the Hutchins Center on Fiscal and Monetary Policy at the Brookings Institution.

Before we turn to the bond market, the average 30-year fixed-rate mortgage currently sits at 7.03 percent, according to Freddie Mac. What does the rate going above 7 percent mean for homebuyers and the housing market?

Well, actually today, according to Bankrate, which is the daily thing, it's up to 7.2 percent today. A year ago, it was 6.3 percent. So on a mortgage of $250,000, that's an extra $1,800 a year in payments. It's like having a 13th month of mortgage payments. So it's a big deal.

And what it means is that, A, houses will be more expensive. So, many people will be priced out of the market. You need to have more income to support that. And, secondly, it means -- and we have seen this from a number of the big homebuilders, Pulte, Lennar, D.R. Horton -- they are anticipating less demand so they're going to build fewer houses, which, of course, is not what we need now at a time when we know we need more houses.

Let me ask you about the bond market now. It's been getting a lot of attention. Help our viewers understand, what exactly is the bond market, why does it matter, and why so much anxiety around it in recent months?

Well, the -- when we talk about mortgage rates, why do mortgage rates move? They move because they're pegged to what happens in the bond market. And the bond market is a big financial market. There's $32 trillion worth of U.S. Treasury debt in the bond market.

That's about the same size as all the stocks on the New York Stock Exchange, and then, if you add in corporate borrowing or state and local governments, another $16 billion. So -- $16 trillion.

So, what this means is that people are buying and selling Treasury bonds. They're driving up the yield because there's so much more borrowing in the economy. ## What Trump's rollback of fuel economy standards means for drivers

The Trump administration is rolling back tougher federal fuel economy standards for new vehicles, giving automakers more flexibility over how fuel-efficient their cars and trucks must be.

The move reverses policies meant to increase fuel efficiency to encourage greater use of electric vehicles. According to the administration, the fleet-wide average will be 34.9 miles per gallon in 2031. Under the Biden era rules, the average would have been over 50 miles per gallon.
