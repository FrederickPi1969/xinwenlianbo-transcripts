## Investigation finds hundreds of U.S. citizens detained by ICE

Officials in California are calling for an investigation into the actions of federal immigration officers after they allegedly injured and detained a U.S. citizen this past weekend.

William Brangham has the latest on the growing number of Americans who are being caught up in the administration's enforcement action.

The incident in question took place on Saturday in Long Beach, California. Video shows the moment Ricardo Gonzalez, a U.S. citizen, is riding an e-bike and then is stopped alongside an unmarked ICE vehicle. It's unclear whether the vehicle hit him or not. He's later seen being held face down in handcuffs screaming.

My arm is broken. Help me. Help me. My arm is (EXPLETIVE DELETED) broken. My arm is (EXPLETIVE DELETED) broken. My arm is broken. It (EXPLETIVE DELETED) hurts!

Gonzalez was later taken to the hospital and treated for serious injuries. ICE says Gonzalez was arrested because he was rioting and damaged a government vehicle.

Details of Gonzalez's detainment come as a new investigation by ProPublica has found more than 500 U.S. citizens have been caught up in the ongoing immigration dragnet.

For more on this, we are joined by ProPublica's Nicole Foy, who was one of the reporters on this series.

Nicole, thank you so much for being here.

Is this incident that we saw reported in California this weekend, does this align with the kinds of cases that you document in your reporting?

It absolutely does, mostly because we found citizens getting detained by immigration officials for a variety of reasons around the country and sometimes in very similar situations like this, where maybe there was a protest nearby or maybe there was just an unexpected encounter between a U.S. citizen in their neighborhood where they lived with immigration officials, who have really expanded their dragnet and the number of people that they're trying to detain.

And, sometimes, those two people co collide, quite literally in this case. And there are many, many citizens that we have documented of the more than 500 that we found were detained so far who were also really seriously injured after their encounters as well.

I mean, DHS, as you reported in your series, repeatedly says that they do not detain or arrest U.S. citizens, despite what your reporting shows.

When you say that U.S. citizens are being detained, as you documented, who are the people that are being detained and what are the other circumstances around their cases?

Sometimes, it is folks that immigration officials have accused of assaulting or interfering with immigration operations. Sometimes, that seems to be the case. And, other times, there is witness -- witness testimony or footage that really contradicts that narrative.

But there's also been many, many people who have been detained because of questions about their citizenship, whether they were on a construction site and didn't speak English very well, or they were -- there were questions about whether they also were here in the United States without papers. We have seen people detained in a variety of ways.

What happens after these people are detained? I mean, are formal charges filed? It seems that if ICE then realizes you have an American citizen in custody, you would think that would be the end of it.

Yes, some people who have been charged with -- who have been arrested on allegations of assault were charged with assault, but we found many who still to this day have not been charged with assault, even if officials release statements accusing them of that.

We also found dozens of people who were held for several days, including at least 24 hours without being able to access their phone or their lawyer or to contact their family and let them know where they were. So it's -- some people really had a very brief detention, where it was maybe 15 minutes, 20 minutes where they're in handcuffs on the side of the road.

But other people disappeared into a system that was actually very difficult for their loved ones and their lawyers to find them, and at the same time they sometimes were seriously injured as well.

You shared a lot of your reporting, including the videos that you compiled, with former immigration officials to gauge their reaction. What was it that they told you about what you had uncovered?

They were really shocked by what they saw. These are folks who -- two particular officials that I'm thinking of retired fairly recently.

And they said that, first of all, they had never seen this many U.S. citizen encounters at all, let alone this level of detention, and that the -- they really characterized much of the use of force, the apparent reason or probable cause for stops, as really troubling, because it did not -- it did not seem that officers were following what they believe that they should have been trained to do.

Are there constitutional protections for Americans in these kinds of circumstances? And, if so, have there been any sort of enforcement actions against officers who might have been accused of or found committing wrongdoing?

Yes, I mean, everyone in the U.S. has constitutional protections, whether you are an immigrant or you are a U.S. citizen.

But what our reporting found was just, particularly when you're just looking at these detentions and arrests of U.S. citizens, really some very troubling examples of possible violations of folks' Fourth Amendment, the right to be free from unreasonable searches and seizures, whether that's accusations of racial profiling or accusations of really excessive force use during the arrest.

But we also found hundreds of people who were arrested because they were participating in a protest. We found many people who were also film -- were simply filming when they were arrested, and they believe that that is the reason why they are arrested. The government may disagree, but they believe their First Amendment rights were violated in that way as well.

Nicole Foy of ProPublica, thank you so much for sharing your reporting with us.

By Rebecca Santana, Philip Marcelo, Associated Press

## A look at the major state ballot measures voters will decide in the midterms

Voters will also weigh in on more than 140 other ballot measures next month in 40 states.

Our Liz Landers has more on the issues on the ballot across the country.

From abortion to voter I.D. to debates over transgender rights, the measures on this year's ballots touch nearly every corner of the political debate. Republicans are counting on some of them to energize their base in a tough midterm environment.

For more on the issues heading before voters, I'm joined now by Reid Wilson, editor and founder of Pluribus News, a site that covers politics and policy at the state level.

The midterm cycle historically does not go well for the party in the White House. So why the state level are Republicans looking to these ballot measures now? And what does the enthusiasm gap look like?

Well, Republicans hope that these ballot measures will gin up enthusiasm among voters who are not terribly excited to show up to vote. Democrats say that they're very excited to show up to vote this year. Fewer Republicans say that. So if Republicans stay home, that's bad for their party, obviously.

Now, these ballot measures are aimed at ginning up that turnout, just like ballot measures in 2004 that banned same-sex marriage were meant to get evangelical voters excited to vote for George W. Bush. Now, a lot of the academic research shows that didn't actually work. Bush's own campaign advisers say he won without those measures. So it's not terribly clear that the strategy is going to work.

As we reported, abortion is on the ballot in four states. Idaho, Nevada, and Virginia would protect abortion rights, while Missouri would roll back the protections that voters approved in 2024. Does abortion on the ballot help one party more than the other this year?

I think that abortion is an issue that we portray as polarizing, but I think the vast majority of Americans are in favor of abortion rights, based on the measures that have passed in just the last few years. I think voters really don't like it when rights are taken away from them, and so we have seen abortion protection measures pass in even some very red states like, as you mentioned, Missouri.

Six states have measures limiting voting to U.S. citizens and several others have voter I.D. measures. It's already a crime to vote in a federal election if you are not a U.S. citizen. Supporters call these election security measures, and critics call them redundant.

What are they actually going to change here?

Well, they wouldn't change much of anything, because, in the six states where these measures are up, Alaska, Arizona, Arkansas, Kansas, South Dakota, and West Virginia, there are no jurisdictions that allow noncitizens to vote.

There are a few jurisdictions in places like San Francisco, Oakland Washington, D.C., and some small towns in Maryland and Vermont, that allow noncitizens to vote in local elections, on school boards, city council races. But no noncitizen is allowed to vote in a federal election, and that's federal law.

These things would not change that. They would only stop local officials from allowing noncitizens to vote in local races, which, again, they can't in those states.

Four states will vote on restricting transgender girls from girls sports. Most of these are in Democratic-led states. Why is this going straight to voters, rather than the legislature?

So there are two ways that ballot initiatives can make it to the ballot. One is citizens petitioning, signing a petition, getting something on the ballot that way. Another is when a legislature refers an issue to the ballot.

These are blue states where the legislature is not going to pass a ban on trans women in girls' sports. Instead, supporters of these measures have gone to the people, gotten the signatures, and gotten on the ballot. This is a pretty common thing. We see this in progressive states where conservatives use the ballot measure to get something on the ballot that the legislature won't pass.

And on the other hand we see it in conservative states where progressives get things like the minimum wage or abortion protection measures on the ballot when the legislature wouldn't pass them.

Beyond social issues, taxes are also on the ballot, including adding a billionaire's tax in California that is backed by progressives like Bernie Sanders, and there's an effort to repeal a tax in Washington on households that make more than $1 million each year.

So, in California, this is a measure being pushed by SEIU, which is the one of the largest unions in the state. It would levy a 5 percent tax on billionaire assets. Interestingly enough, Governor Gavin Newsom is against this.

California relies so heavily on its capital gains tax that he worries that, if people with a billion dollars flee to another state, it could have real long-term impacts on the state's fiscal health. In Washington, on the other hand, Washington has never had an income tax at all.

And the notion of taxing incomes over a million dollars has been really controversial in a state that is used to not paying income taxes, even if very few people make more than a million dollars a year.

Reid Wilson, thank you so much for your expertise.

Because of Idaho's abortion law, Abby's doctor was not allowed to end her pregnancy.

There's like a piece of you that hopes that they can help you. Like, you're just like, no, my doctors can help me, because that's what doctors do. But you also have heard of other women who haven't received care, and you just are starting to, like, realize that that's you too.

## Democratic and GOP strategists on the deeply personal attack ad in Ohio

The midterm campaign is entering its final stretch, and the attacks are getting increasingly personal. In Ohio, Republican gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy's campaign is behind an extraordinary attack ad featuring the estranged mother of his Democratic opponent, Amy Acton, disputing Acton's account of her own childhood.

It's a striking example of just how far candidates are willing to go to win votes in this high-stakes election.

For analysis of that and more, we turn tonight to Faiz Shakir here, a Democratic strategist and senior adviser to Senator Bernie Sanders, and longtime Republican strategist Whit Ayres. Whit, we should note, is on the board of the "News Hour"'s parent organization, WETA.

Donna Arthur, Mother of Amy Acton: It's not easy for a mother to say this, that Amy has a serious problem. She is a liar. She lies about her past. She says she grew up living in a tent. None of that is true.

So this ad is sparking criticism from both sides of the aisle.

What is the political calculation behind an ad like this?

Geoff, there's a widespread assumption in the political consultant community that negative ads work. And a lot of them do if they're credible, if they're believable, and especially if they use the opponent's words against them.

But it's also possible to do negative ads that blow up your own campaign, those that are simply not believable, that are easily rebutted, or just make your skin crawl. And that Vivek ad probably qualifies on at least two of those criteria.

Is there evidence that attacks like these, these personal attack ads work?

Well, sometimes. And they're disgusting, so we all note that.
