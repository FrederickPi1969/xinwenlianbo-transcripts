## Riley Downing sings about the overlooked corners of American life in 'Landfill Poetry'

Riley Downing has spent his life collecting stories from the overlooked corners of American life, from his days working as a trash collector in rural Missouri to the people and places that shaped his small-town Midwestern upbringing.

As part of our arts and culture series, Canvas, Riley Downing joins us now here in studio.

So, as we mentioned before, music became your career. You worked as a garbage collector in rural Missouri. How did that experience and your upbringing shape the stories you choose to tell now in song?

I think just, creatively, lots of nostalgia for different things in life, and that's kind of what I think folk music -- that's one part of it.

You can mix it all together and just sing about life.

We were talking earlier about how you're set to debut at the Grand Ole Opry, the most celebrated stage in country music. What's that -- what's that feel like for you?

I grew up watching that with my grandparents on the television and going to a bunch of small opries. And after spending years in and out of Nashville, it's one of the highest honors you could have.

This new album, "Landfill Poetry," what's the title mean?

Just kind of a mix of old pieces of songs and ideas that put together -- like a piece of wood that's in your shed that you don't know what you're going to use it for, but then 10 years later you come back and you're ready to use it, you know exactly what you're going to use it for.

## National parks under strain from Trump's construction projects and political pressure

As the National Park Service celebrates its 110th anniversary this week, people continue to flock to America's national parks, more than 320 million last year alone. But that broad appeal is facing new political pressures, from orders to revise exhibits at parks to controversy surrounding the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool and the new White House ballroom.

Tiernan, welcome to the show. Thanks for being with us.

Have you ever seen this kind of thing before? Is this a new challenge for the Park Service?

Well, first, I should also join you in wishing the National Park Service a happy 110th birthday.

## Groundbreaking treatments and vaccines offer hope in fight against cancer

There have been several promising developments lately in cancer research and treatment, including a new drug approved by the FDA this past week for advanced pancreatic cancer.

Pancreatic cancer has long been one of the deadliest types of cancer. Its five-year overall survival rate is just 13 percent. But this new pill called daraxonrasib nearly doubled the survival time for patients and had fewer severe side effects than other treatments.

For more on this, we are joined by Dr. Robert Vonderheide. He directs the Abramson Center at Penn Medicine and is the president-elect of the American Association for Cancer Research.

Dr. Vonderheide, thank you so much for being here.

A very exciting week for cancer news. Specifically, on this pancreatic cancer drug, how successful do you think this treatment will be?

Dr. Robert Vonderheide, Abramson Cancer Center Director, Penn Medicine:

I think we have only seen the beginning. The FDA approved this drug for patients with advanced pancreatic cancer, doubling their survival and really offering incredible hope and excitement for our patients and their families.

Now the opportunity exists to bring this particular drug forward to patients in earlier stages or just after they have been diagnosed. I think we will see this type of medicine have a huge impact and hopefully reverse those statistics that you cited just a moment ago.

I understand this medication specifically targets the gene mutations that can spur pancreatic cancer cells to grow. Why has that intervention not been possible in the past?

Yes, you're talking about the RAS oncogene, which is the mutated engine that drives pancreatic cancer and many other tumor types as well, and it's been very difficult to drug and block and inhibit with conventional chemistry.

What's happened here is a brand-new type of chemistry, really unprecedented. We call it molecular glue that gums up this mutated engine room as it spins on a flywheel, just bringing it to a halt. It really took basic chemistry and basic research to find a new way forward, and that's what we see being delivered for our patients this week.

So the other big development is that there was this a new individualized vaccine that helps people with melanoma, which is a type of skin cancer, to live longer. Can you explain how that vaccine works?

So, this news was announced from the companies involved in building that mRNA vaccine, indicating that their clinical trial has a positive result. It's not yet FDA-approved.

What we're learning is these are patients with melanoma who are in remission, but at risk for relapse, and the vaccine given at that time lowered the chances of a patient relapsing, just an incredible breakthrough at the power of the immune system being generated against the very mutations that are found in the tumors themselves.

You mentioned that this is still in clinical trials and not been approved yet. Are there -- what else do you want to see from those results before you really cheer extremely loudly about this news?

Well, we're cheering pretty loudly because we have been following this for a long time, and some of the early results have been presented in detail.

But we soon will hear the full data report from the companies, we believe. And we're looking for two things. What is the magnitude effect? How much has survival been impacted and increased? What will this mean for the individual patient?

And, second, does it work as designed? Does it generate the T-cell immune response, as we think it does? And can that be mobilized to an even greater extent with improving the vaccine going forward? So, both clinical and scientific data, we look forward in the coming weeks.

I mean, I know this has been the dream of immunology to be able to get a vaccine to do this. Do you think we will see more types of these interventions for different kinds of cancers in the -- down the road?

A hundred percent. This is the first of what I think will be a wave of cancer vaccines, lowering the chance of relapse, recurrence, perhaps one day even preventing certain types of cancer from ever appearing at all.

This is really the power of the immune system, which over the last 10 years we have learned to harness in a safe way. It's a breakthrough because we don't have this type of modality yet. And so that's why the news this week was so exciting.

Well, mRNA vaccines were first developed for the COVID pandemic, as we all know. Hundreds of millions of individuals received that vaccine with really detailed safety data.

I think what we're looking at is a technology that is highly effective, not just against COVID, but also against influenza and now perhaps, as we just talked about, cancer. The power of mRNA as a medicine, whether as a vaccine or in other ways, is unprecedented. It's before us.

And I think we should all take huge advantage to what mRNA can do to improve the health of our patients.

All right, that is Dr. Robert Vonderheide at the Abramson Center at Penn Medicine.

## News Wrap: Friday marks 6 months since Iran war started

In the day's other headlines: Today marks six months since the U.S. and Israel first launched strikes on Iran in a conflict that President Trump said would last a matter of weeks.

Half-a-year on, the scars of the war's earliest days remain, with buildings across the country still in ruins. In the southern city of Minab, parents are still grieving more than 100 children killed when their school was bombed on the war's first day. One mother reflected on the loss of her son.

Khadijeh Ahmadizadeh, Bereaved Mother (through interpreter):

They have left a pain in the heart of Minab that will never go away. They think that now six months have passed that our hearts have grown cold, but they haven't at all. It's getting worse. This pain is so heavy. The children left with so many dreams.

Also in the region today, Israel says it carried out a rare airstrike in the occupied West Bank, killing a Hamas operative and two of his accomplices. The militant group confirmed that the target was a member and condemned the attack.

President Trump honored the crew of the Artemis II lunar mission today with the Congressional Space Medal of Honor.

Four intrepid space heroes whose sense of daring and adventure led them further from Earth than anyone has ever traveled before.

The three Americans and one Canadian received the nation's highest space honor in recognition of their flight around the moon earlier this year. They were the first astronauts to do so in more than half-a-century.

Also at the ceremony, the president signed an executive order creating the United States Space Academy, likening it to a West Point for the military space branch.

A.I. giant Anthropic says it remains focused on working productively with the Trump administration after a judge ruled that the Pentagon's measures against the company were -- quote -- "illegal and baseless."

A federal judge ruled Anthropic was denied due process after Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth labeled the company a national security supply chain risk earlier this year. That designation came after Anthropic refused to allow the military to use its Claude A.I. models for U.S. surveillance or autonomous weapons. The Trump administration is expected to fight the ruling.

In the nation's capital, thousands gathered on the National Mall today for what organizers described as a Defend the Vote March on Washington.

Martin Luther King III, Civil Rights Activist:

This is the season to reject the spirit of anxiety and fear by personally becoming and teaching our children to daily become engaged.

Martin Luther King III, son of the famous civil rights leader, helped organize the event 63 years after Dr. King's famous March on Washington. Senator Bernie Sanders and House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries also addressed the crowd. The march was intended to highlight threats to voting rights and other freedoms ahead of November's midterm elections.

British right-wing agitator Milo Yiannopoulos is in ICE custody and facing deportation tonight. The Department of Homeland Security posted this intake photo on social media, saying Yiannopoulos was arrested Thursday at an airport in Louisiana. The agency says he legally entered the U.S. in 2019, but overstayed and was ordered deported last month after failing to appear for an immigration hearing. DHS says Yiannopoulos will remain in custody pending his removal from the country.

In Tennessee, Governor Bill Lee and the Nashville International Airport announced plans today to rename the airport in honor of Dolly Parton. The idea has enjoyed strong support since the passing of the country music superstar and philanthropist earlier this week.

Today, over Nashville, a plane flew a banner calling on people to sign a petition in support of the renaming. More than 150,000 people have done so. But it's not a done deal yet. Under the airport authority's rules, at least two years must pass after a person's death before they can be honored. Officials will discuss today's proposal at a meeting next month.

On Wall Street today, stocks ended slightly lower to close out the week. The Dow Jones industrial average flipped less than 10 points, so basically flat. The Nasdaq fell about a half-a-percent. The S&P 500 also ended in negative territory.

And two royal deaths of note spanning generations and continents. Norway's prime minister says the whole nation is in mourning following the death of King Harald V at the age of 89. Europe's oldest monarch, Harald took the throne in 1991 and helped modernize the centuries-old institution.

His decision to marry a commoner before taking the throne initially shocked that nation, but eventually endeared him to his subjects. A direct descendant of Britain's Queen Victoria, Harald's ties to history ran deep. He fled the Nazis as a child during World War II and even attended Franklin Roosevelt's inauguration as a child.

And, in Uganda, King Oyo of Tooro has died at just 34 years old. Oyo became king at the age of 3 following his father's death in 1995, making him the world's youngest reigning monarch. Uganda is a republic, so Oyo held no formal political power, but he served for three decades as the cultural leader of the Tooro Kingdom in Western Uganda, home to more than two million people.

## Devastating scale of Himalayan floods comes into focus as rescuers arrive

Three days after a catastrophic wall of water, mud, and rock tore through Nepal's Himalayan valleys, the scale of the disaster is still coming into focus. Nepalese authorities say at least 579 people are now confirmed dead, and nearly 2,000 remain missing. The casualty situation in China is not yet clear.

And, tonight, the danger is not over. Authorities are closely watching lakes formed in the wake of that glacier collapse amid fears of further flooding.

In Nepal, the violent floods that raged through these valleys two days ago left haunting reminders, the banks of the Trishuli River now awash in gray mud, within these communities, cries of grief and trauma and warnings of more danger.

Police officers rode through the streets today shouting, "Stay away from the river," as residents, still shaken, ran to higher ground. Officials are monitoring a number of lakes formed by landslides overflowing. Crammed with debris, they present a high risk of more flooding.

Indira Adhikari, Evacuated Resident (through interpreter):

The flood is causing huge problems for us. Our house is now full of mud. We are young and still facing so much trouble walking and evacuating. Imagine how tough it is for old people in such a situation.

I have seen so many disasters, but this one, with the level of devastation, it's very difficult for me.

Suraj Sigdel is the Nepal country director for the aid organization Mercy Corps. He told the "News Hour" search operations were paused today after the new flood alerts went out.

My team was supposed to go to the ground zero this morning, and we were cautious about more flooding. And the lake is being formed at the Tibetan side of the border, and which might overflow or burst any time. We are in the monsoon season, so, like, the erratic rain is always expected, so it can rain anywhere, and it will have devastating impact if it rains heavily or the cloud burst in the flood-affected areas.

The dramatic images that have emerged from Wednesday's disaster speak for themselves, the river transformed from calm to crushing within seconds.

Construction workers at this hydroelectric dam ran for their lives as the wave of water and earth accelerated toward them.

Nepal's army says it has pulled 350 people to safety from this tunnel running underneath a hydropower plant. Officials believe more than 100 people are still trapped inside. China state broadcaster showed Premier Li Qiang today visiting the flood zone in Tibet, where emergency crews have relied on inflatable rafts.

Nepal's army has used helicopters to pluck people from their rooftops. Despite offers from international rescue teams, Nepalese officials say their in-country rescuers are sufficient for now.

Nepal's army is equipped, and they have the capacity to do so. And they don't want to, like, bring in many international people and then risk the coordination for it. So that is the logic of government.

But other aid may be coming, emergency supplies from the U.N., as well as India, and financial pledges from South Korea and the U.K. The U.S. State Department says it is sending Nepal $500,000 in assistance. The Red Cross says more than $30 million is needed for its response.

Based on satellite imagery and on-the-ground data, scientists now believe the bedrock underneath the Himalayan glacier collapsed. The massive rock fell more than half-a-mile down, shattering debris into the valley's rivers, which surged with deadly force into villages. Experts say climate change, if not a sole cause, may contribute to such phenomena.

We consider it as a climate-induced disaster. We are disappropriately impacted by this climate change, and we are being punished for something that we have not done. So, yes, we feel like it's not a justice.

As the casualties mount, stories of survival have surfaced as well. Maddie Tamong (ph) thought for sure she was facing the greatest loss of her life.

Maddie Tamong, Mother (through interpreter):

I had come to accept that both my children were gone. I went through the jungle full of leeches, crossed rivers, weeping and frantically searching for them. At last I have found both my children. They were injured, covered in scratches, but I found them.

She was reunited with them, scratches and all.
