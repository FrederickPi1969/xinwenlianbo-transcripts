## Sam Roberts and Geoff Bennett discuss the art of obituary writing on 'Settle In'

Sam Roberts has written some 1,600 obituaries as a reporter for The New York Times. His new book "Are They Dead Yet?" details the art of obituary writing, what his work has taught him about life.

Geoff Bennett spoke to Roberts for the latest episode of our PBS News podcast "Settle In."

You start the book with this wonderful contradiction. You have spent more than a decade writing about death, but you say you have been afraid of death for most of your life. So how does someone who has had this lifelong aversion to death end up on the obit desk?

Sam Roberts, Author, "Are They Dead Yet?: The Art of the Obit": Well, I wouldn't say afraid of death as much as coming from a family growing up in Brooklyn that -- where death was denied at all the time.

I remember, when my dad died, about six months later, his sister, my aunt, was asked by a neighbor how he was, and she at best could reply, so-so. That was the kind of culture I came from. And I wasn't particularly pleased when The Times asked me to write advanced obituaries to help reduce the backlog of people we should have been doing advances of.

But then I realized that obituaries are not about death. Death is mentioned in one sentence, and, in fact, they are about people's lives, what made them interesting, why they were transformational, what they were emblematic of in terms of transformation and the impact on society.

And they're mini-biographies, and that's what makes them so fascinating. Most of the people I have written about at The Times and mentioned in the book are people I had never heard of. Obviously, we do obituaries of celebrities and prominent people and politicians, but there's so many interesting, quirky people who you would just never know had existed. Those are the people I love writing obituaries about the most.

How long did it take you to realize and when did you realize that writing obituaries might be one of the greatest jobs in journalism?

Some people think of obituary writing as a dead end at the end of a career or something for neophytes at the beginning. I think it is one of the most coveted positions in journalism. We now have nine reporters at The New York Times writing obituaries, and they get on line to be hired for that department.

They are mini-biographies. They are stories about people's lives, what makes them interesting. And one of the things I discovered, Geoff, in doing this assignment is, there really are no ordinary lives. People have impact each in his or her own way.

After 9/11, when we did our series of portraits of grief of the victims of the World Trade Center attack, and during COVID, when we wrote about those we have lost, we did little obits on people who might ordinarily be considered routine, who we never would have done obituaries of.

And the impact they had on people's lives was substantial. As one obituarist said, who would you miss more, your mayor or your mailman? Well, we take a lot of people for granted, and these ordinary people really do have an impact on our own lives.

Thomas Carlyle, who is the originator of the Great Man theory of history, said himself, who is more important, Hannibal, who drove his elephants across the Alps, or some poor anonymous character who invented the spade? Well, I would go with the spade man, who probably had a much greater impact over centuries than Hannibal did.

So these are important things to take into consideration, that everyone is intrinsically interesting in his or her own way, and that the -- collectively, those obituaries reflect what our culture cares about, what we value in people.

You can watch that full episode wherever you get your podcasts.

## Andrew Bird takes eclectic compositions to symphony stages

From playing classical and jazz standards on his violin to singing folk and rock, Andrew Bird is constantly weaving together different styles and genres.

Special correspondent Mike Cerre caught up with Bird as he performed with the San Francisco Symphony. It's part of our arts and culture series, Canvas.

A classically trained violinist since he was 4, Andrew Bird has been improvising with his violin and his music ever since.

Andrew Bird, Singer-Songwriter and Violinist: Since the violin is generally one note at a time, I discovered this technology that could allow me to play pizzicato parts and then layer on both parts, and you're kind of creating this skeleton of a song.

Never intending to replace a band or orchestra he couldn't afford early in his career, his onstage recorded music loops with his violin, singing and whistling became his live performance signature, a one-man orchestra of the imagination.

The classical music is part of my roots, the -- but also all these other things. And it all just kind of steers in there and comes out as it does.

Tell me about over the years your kind of love-hate relationship with symphony orchestras. Why didn't you want to go that route with your classical musical training?

I quickly realized that I wasn't cut out for it. I was too -- I think too -- a little too individualistic.

This year, he's going full circle, playing with major orchestras like the San Francisco Symphony, making his millennial fan base and ever supportive and patient parents very happy.

No matter what I was doing, my dad was like, you sure you don't want to try to go play the Bruch Concerto with the Dubuque Symphony or something like that?

While growing up in the Chicago area and studying classical violin at Northwestern, he gravitated more to playing pickup sessions at Irish pubs, jazz clubs, and joining a punk ska band.

I was just really interested in absorbing, say, gypsy -- Hungarian gypsy music or early jazz or Irish music or anything, anything exotic or different.

His music culture immersion led to 18 very different albums over the years. His amalgam of different music genres still challenge the digital music algorithms, the Grammys, and even his fan base to categorize.

He's a very talented multi-instrumentalist, and I leave it at that.

He crosses a lot of boundaries. There's a little bit of electronic in there.

He's touched on jazz, a lot of that New Orleans sound.

I don't think it can be categorized, and I think that's the greatest thing about it. You can't. Every time, it's something different.

I learned early on that staying in one place too long in a genre, it just becomes a drag if you're ready to move on and you get stuck in that genre.

Some of his improvisations have been inspired by his focus on acoustics and his field recordings in what he calls echolocations, like Utah's Coyote Gulch Canyon and the aqueducts along the Los Angeles River.

So I would make these field recordings and then play the field recording and then start improvising and looping to that in the studio.

You have been nominated for an Emmy for your best original song for "The Pitt," very popular series.

They send me the scene, which is this very emotional scene where finally everyone stops and reflects on the passing of their beloved character. That, to me, is like an ideal use of what I have to offer. It's like taken me a whole lifetime to be able to write that song that took an hour to write.

Instead of a medley of his hits and soundtracks, Andrew Bird's symphony performances focus on the 20th anniversary of his first concept album, "The Mysterious Production of Eggs." He originally composed it while on a career reset in 2001 on his family's farm in Western Illinois.

I didn't bring any of my record collection with me, and I was just drywalling this barn trying to turn it into a studio. And I was extremely isolated, and I had this little looping pedal, and I just experimented all day long for weeks and weeks on end.

And I challenged myself to write songs that had only two chords or only three notes in the melody, like stripping away all that genre stuff. That's why it's kind of cool to come back all these years later and do this particular album, because there's so many parts and interweaving parts and details that I could never do solo.

San Francisco Davies Symphony Hall is a long ways from that Illinois barn and performing as a one-man orchestra.

Andrew Bird's "The Mysterious Production of Eggs" and his diverse body of work are on full display, with a symphony orchestra for a change, with the recorded sound loops not as essential.

## A look at Spain's plan to give undocumented migrants legal status

As most Western countries tighten their borders, Spain's left-wing Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez has enacted a plan aimed at giving legal status to undocumented immigrants. It's called regularization.

But late last month, when tens of thousands of migrants tried to illegally cross into the Spanish territory of Ceuta, which borders Morocco, that incident put Spain's immigration policy under the microscope.

Before most of Madrid wakes up, Carlos Aroca is already at work. For hours each morning, he collects garbage in one of Europe's busiest capitals. It's essential work done by an invisible worker.

Carlos Aroca, Ecuadorian Migrant (through interpreter):

We can work in jobs that many people no longer want to do, jobs in cleaning, fruit picking, farmwork, eldercare, and construction.

It wasn't his dream job either. Aroca came to Spain from Ecuador to pursue an MBA. After a year of study, he lost his legal status and was forced to find jobs willing to hire undocumented immigrants.

When it comes to working irregularly, it does hit us pretty hard since we don't have the benefits that the law provides a regular job. They're not going to pay us the same as someone who's on contract.

Carlos is among an estimated 600,000 undocumented migrants that now have the opportunity to come out of the shadows. The royal decree he approved in April provides temporary and renewable legal status to undocumented immigrants who have lived in Spain for the last five months and have no criminal record.

Vladimir Revelo, President, Asociacion Ruminahui (through interpreter):

These more than one million immigrants were working. But they were working in the informal sector. What this regularization has done is bring them into the formal system so they can now make contributions and have Social Security, access to public health care.

Vladimir Revelo is the president of a migrant advocacy group in Spain. The nonprofit has spent decades helping migrants navigate the immigration system. He's helped Aroca and hundreds of people like him, who are working, paying rent and raising families, but unable to fully participate in the country many now consider home.

My first reaction was one of doubt. I couldn't get my hopes up too much. Once the law was passed, of course, you could say it was a dream come true.

Today, foreign workers make up roughly one of every six workers in Spain. Revelo says the reforms are a pragmatic response to Spain's economic challenges, an aging population, low birth rates and persistent labor shortages.

Spain and the European Union will need around 60 million workers by 2050 because they need to collect Social Security contributions to maintain pensions for native citizens. Spanish businesses need this work force. Without this work force, the construction service and hospitality sectors, as well as the agricultural and parcel delivery sectors, would grind to a halt.

But not everyone sees the policy as an economic necessity. Opposition leader Alberto Nunez Feijoo argues Spain lacks the housing, public services and infrastructure to absorb more migrants.

Alberto Nunez Feijoo, President, Popular Party (through interpreter):

This regularization has harmful consequences for Spain and therefore for all of Europe, because it will put pressure on public services, further strain the housing market, and create problems in the community.

And, last month, the immigration debate exploded overnight. More than 350 miles south of Madrid, Spain's tiny North African territory of Ceuta was confronted with a wave of people. Tens of thousands of migrants attempted to cross into Ceuta from neighboring Morocco.

The chaotic scenes quickly spread across TV screens and social media, families sleeping on beaches, people washing clothes in the sea and lining up for aid. More than 100 people died, either drowning or crushed by the crowd.

For many who thought they'd made it, the crossing represented years of hope distilled into a single desperate attempt.

I have many dreams. And the first dreams to me, just I want to help my lost family. Why I said lost? Because I lost a lot of them, and I will fight for them. I will fight. I will do anything just to have them.

Twenty-nine-year-old Ahmed Haroun says he waited four years for the opportunity to escape Darfur, Sudan.

No, I'm not scared of this life. I believe everyone here just die one time. No one can die two times.

But most migrants who entered Ceuta have since been deported back to Morocco or returned on their own. What led to the mass attempt to cross into Spanish territory is unclear. But experts say it was likely not spontaneous.

Jeremy Konyndyk, President, Refugees International:

There was misinformation circulating on social media in Morocco, telling people that they could go to Spain and that they would have a chance to go there and stay.

Jeremy Konyndyk is the president of Refugees International. He said the role Morocco played in the chaos deserves scrutiny.

What we do know pretty definitively is that the Moroccan border guards who would normally have prevented something like this did not obstruct it. This was a breakdown, potentially a willful breakdown, by the Moroccan government in their border controls, perhaps to put some kind of pressure on Spain, because they knew that there would be this kind of hysteria in Europe over these sorts of images.

The images gave anti-immigration voices fuel to criticize Spain's immigration reforms, even though Spain has long taken a hard line on the kind of irregular migration that occurred in Ceuta.

Spain's far right party President Santiago Abascal reacted.

Santiago Abascal, Vox Party Leader (through interpreter):

We are facing an invasion, and it must be called that. I think that at this point no one can deny that the arrival of 65,000 people, according to what's being said at the moment, is an invasion. And we are facing an act of war promoted by Morocco and tolerated and allowed by Pedro Sanchez.

Twenty-two European Union member states led by Italy and Denmark warned that large-scale regularization programs risk drawing more migrants to Europe.

The far right immigration narrative in Europe has always been that Europe is going to be overrun by people from around the world if they open their doors and if they treat migrants humanely. And so, for them, they want to spin this as definitive proof of that.

Konyndyk says far right anti-migrant sentiment undermines the economic solution that he says more migration offers and that Europe needs.

They have created for themselves an impossible situation, where their economy desperately needs migration, and their politics will not allow for it.

What we see the Sanchez government doing here in Spain is trying for a more sane and rational migration policy that actually reflects the realities of their country and their economy.

Carlos Aroca says he's looking forward to having more personal stability and returning to his original dream of working in business.

Carlos, what is your message to people who don't agree with these immigration reforms?

We migrants in most cases leave our countries in search of a better life. What we come here to do is to contribute to the country that welcomes us. We want to be able to fit into an environment of coexistence through our work and our culture, respecting the traditions they have, but also contributing our own, which are also very valuable.
