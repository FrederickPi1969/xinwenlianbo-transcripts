## North Carolina Senate race emerges as Dems best chance to flip GOP seat

Well, the 2026 primary season is officially over. Voters in Delaware cast the final ballots yesterday. And, no surprises, they set up a U.S. Senate race between incumbent Democrat Chris Coons and Republican Michael Katz. Now the general election is finally under way. Mail-in ballots have gone out in several states.

And with just seven weeks left until Election Day, Lisa Desjardins takes a closer look at the increasingly competitive match for the U.S. Senate.

Democrats are looking to North Carolina as one of their best chances to flip a Republican Senate seat. And President Trump is taking a firsthand look. He's there today to try to help his handpicked candidate, former Republican Party Chairman Michael Whatley.

A recent Elon University poll shows the Democrat, former Governor Roy Cooper, with a double-digit lead over Whatley.

For an on-the-ground report, I'm joined by Colin Campbell of public media station WUNC.

Colin, just take us there. Where does this race stand?

Yes, so this is a race where we were expecting it to be a lot tighter than it has been, at least in the polls that we have seen. That lead that Governor Roy Cooper had hasn't really changed a whole lot over the last few months, and we sort of expected it to tighten up.

But this is the first we're seeing of President Trump and really any of the high-ranking members of his administration actually coming to North Carolina to campaign with Whatley. So we will certainly see if that helps boost him among Republicans. But the president's approval ratings in North Carolina are kind of the worst that they have been, and this is the case for many other states as well.

So whether this helps him with swing voters sort of remains to be seen, but certainly energizes the Republican base to have Trump here in North Carolina.

How is Whatley handling Trump specifically? Not every Republican candidate wants Trump to fly into their state or district right now.

Yes, and for a while we were wondering whether we would see the president in the state, and certainly this seems like a conscious choice on Whatley's part.

As a former chairman of the Republican National Committee, there's not a whole lot of daylight between Whatley and Trump. He really hasn't made much of an effort to distance himself from anything the president or his administration has been doing. He talks about Trump less on the campaign trail than he did earlier on, which seems to be a strategic choice to focus more on attacking his opponent than he is praising the president's administration.

But, certainly, you don't see a whole lot of difference between Whatley's campaign and his position on a lot of these national issues and the president's.

If you had to pick one or two key issues in this Senate race in North Carolina, what would they be?

I think affordability is probably the top one. I mean, the economy here is an issue. And Governor Roy -- former Governor Roy Cooper has been making that his signature issue. He does these Make Stuff Cost Less Tour events, he calls them, across the state, promoting his plans for that.

Whatley has been more focused on a crime -- tough-on-crime message, trying to tie Governor Cooper to people who've been let out of jail or certain criminal instances and policies that he's had. And then you have also got a lot of discussion about A.I. and data centers that have been popping up around the state, but less by the candidates in this race.

The candidates really have sort of picked their lanes, whether it be crime or affordability, and are sticking to that, and that seems to be the theme of most of the campaign events we have seen in this state so far.

Colin Campbell of WUNC, a state to watch. We appreciate your reporting.

## Cuban official on Trump's threats as tensions with U.S. rise

For months, the Trump administration has ramped up economic pressure and military threats against the island nation of Cuba. A de facto oil blockade means blackouts, food and water shortages for millions have worsened. Cuban officials say they're willing to talk, but, publicly, there's been no progress.

I spoke earlier today with Cuba's Deputy Foreign Minister Carlos Fernandez de Cossio as he visits the U.S.

Deputy Foreign Minister de Cossio, welcome to the "News Hour." Thank you for being here.

Carlos Fernandez De Cossio, Cuban Deputy Minister of Foreign Affairs: Thank you for having me.

So, this month, the foreign minister of Cuba said that there are no talks happening between the U.S. and Cuba, but that the Cuban government would continue reaching out to its contacts. Is that happening? Is that part of why you're here in Washington?

It's not the reason why I'm in Washington. I'm here because I'm attending the U.N. General Assembly. We have a major embassy in Washington, and it's normal for us, for senior officials to visit and engage with our diplomatic missions.

What our position on contacts with the U.S. is that first we have diplomatic relations. So we have official channels of communication. And we have had several contacts and meetings with the U.S., that what we claim is that they had no progress.

And we say that because you only have to look at the fact that practically every week or every 10 days or 15 days, there's a new announcement by the U.S. government of sanctions and coercive measures that punish the people of Cuba.

So you could not claim that there's progress in any dialogue or any type of communication if hostility continues to happen. And that's our main point. But we continue to be ready to engage with the United States when they are ready to try to solve our political differences, our bilateral political differences, which are many.

But we are firm believers that, through dialogue, through respect between the two governments, some common ground could be found.

When was the last time there were any direct talks between your two governments?

I prefer not to go into details. We try to treat this discreetly.

We truly reject the leaks that have been occurring. But I can assure you there's -- meetings have occurred, and we would be ready to engage again.

In other words, they are not closed, they're not suspended, they're not shut down. Simply, there's no progress to this moment.

And by your definition, progress would mean the U.S. would stop with additional sanctions?

That could be, but, in a broader sense, there will have to -- we would have to identify a common goal that we want to reach that implies progress in the bilateral relationship.

Up to now, if you look at the claims of the U.S. publicly, they want to take over Cuba. That is quite an ambitious goal. That certainly is unacceptable for Cuba. Cuba's a sovereign nation.

Do you take that as a real threat? Are you worried about a U.S. invasion?

We would be naive if we do not take that into account. It is constantly repeated.

U.S. senior officials are asked constantly if that is on the table, and they claim that nothing is off the table. So, a country like Cuba, looking what to what's happened in the world and our own history, we will be naive if we don't take that seriously.

Can I ask you about what's happening on the ground in Cuba right now? Because we have seen blackouts amid the oil blockade. We have seen life gotten so much worse for Cubans. Food is expensive. It spoils without electricity. We know there are water shortages as well.

What is your message to Cubans about what you and other leaders are doing to alleviate that pain?

First of all, it is very clear that the policy of the United States is to produce collective punishment, which is an international crime, to try to induce a humanitarian crisis in Cuba, meaning that is to use the pain of each and every Cuban to try to achieve a political goal.

That, again, is an international crime. The people of Cuba understand that. And when you see in the media normally the reports of what's happening in Cuba, they speak about the lack of energy, the blackouts and people being irritated, naturally, people being uncomfortable, naturally.

But that's only the surface. They don't take into account what it implies for a family that has a sick person at home and then doesn't know how to transport them to the hospital.

They don't take into account what implies for a person who needs an urgent surgery and has to wait in line because of, as a result of lack of energy, surgery has to be postponed, what it implies for a family that has a newborn whose life depends on a machine not stopping because of lack of power, what it implies for people having to take children to school, to do homework at night.

So it has a human face. We use statistics many times of what it implies, but this has a human face. And the main problem is that it's not collateral damage. It is the aim of the policy. So the people of Cuba understand that. And

the government has -- says, claims, and we're doing, trying to introduce in Cuba the transformations and to take the steps that allow us to cope with this situation. That does not depend on us.

I mean, as you say, these are the most significant privatization steps the nation has taken in over six decades. If this does lead towards an alleviation of suffering for Cubans, if opening up the economy does help, would you continue to do so?

Our aim is -- the main priority here is, how do we satisfy, how do we respond to the needs of the people of Cuba? And that is the driving goal here.

That is an enormous shift for a communist nation to say you will continue to open up your economy.

We will continue to go down this path, but we will retain the role of the state to respond to basic needs of the population and the economic capacity to do that.

I have to ask you about the allegations from U.S. officials as well about the gap between Cuban leaders and what they're experiencing and everyday Cubans.

They say the leaders aren't feeling the pain as evenly. I want to put to you what the U.S. ambassador to the U.N., Mike Waltz, said back in July. He said: "There's no food or fuel for hospitals. How is there fuel for the Castro private family jet? How does President Diaz-Canel afford his Hermes tie, his Rolex watch? How does the Castro family afford their 17 homes, their trips to Madrid and Moscow?"

First of all, this is claimed by the representative of one of the countries with the most inequality in the world today, an inequality that has grown dramatically in the past 20 years, and above all under the current government.

People here are not experiencing 20 hours a day of blackouts, though, in all due respect.

Secondly, there are no private jets within -- of the Castro family. It's totally untrue. There are no 17 homes of the Castro family. Totally untrue.

But he can claim whatever he wants when he has a microphone in front of him. All Cubans of any type -- senior officials in the government are suffering the same situation of Cubans. I'm not saying that all Cubans are on equal footing. Depends on the neighborhood where you live, the amount of hours it takes, the conditions of your home.

But the level of inequality, the level of differences in Cuba is dramatically different than the ones you have in the United States. The pain is on the Cuban population as a whole, in ways that no other country perhaps has suffered, unless you put the example of Gaza in Palestine.

It's warfare against a population of a whole country. And for an American official to claim that there are differences from the United States, a country where you have a minuscule elite in which a few people have more money than the whole population of the richest country in the world is quite hypocritical.

Mr. Minister, this survey says some 54 percent of people surveyed in Cuba say they ran out of food in the prior three months. There's reports of up to 20 hours a day without power.

Are you saying that Cuban officials are experiencing that same level of pain?

I can tell you that my colleagues and myself, we have two 2.5, three hours of power a day, that our refrigerator is closed, that you can't cool food, that we don't have running water. And I'm talking about Cuban officials in general.

And anyone who knows me can go and check or who knows any one of my colleagues. Now there are people which are not government officials, which are simple private citizens that might live across the street from a hospital. And they don't suffer blackouts because hospitals are protected. Clinics are protected.

And that's just, look, where they live. And some of us have to take our food and our products and ask them, can you refrigerate this for some time? That is normal life in Cuba for everybody. The claim in the United States that there's a difference between an elite and the people, it's totally, totally untrue, and they can't prove it.

The U.S. has claimed, this administration has claimed that Cuba is a national security threat. It's been deemed a state sponsor of terrorism. The White House has said that the Cuban regime aligns itself with nations hostile to the U.S., that Cuba maintains close ties to other state sponsors of terrorism.

Most of the countries that the U.S. claims Cuba is close to, the United States has a more comprehensive and broader relationship with those countries than Cuba does.

We're talking about nations like Russia, Iran.

Russia. The U.S. has a much broader relationship with China, at least, and with Russia than we do, with trade, with the exchange of different types, than we do.

It is an adversarial relationship with Russia, though. You would not call Russia an ally.

But they have more trade still today with Russia than we do, more contacts, more high-level meetings and visits than they have with Cuba, than Russia has with Cuba. With China, it's the same. They claim it's adversarial, but it's a claim.

But they have more relationship than we do.

Well, what does happen next then? How do you see this ending with the pressure, with the military threats, with additional U.S. sanctions? How does this end?

We will have to cope with ourselves. And to some extent, the international community perhaps would say, this is unacceptable for one superpower to be dictating everybody having to do.

But what cannot occur and what would be treason is for the Cuban government to tell our people, look, there's nothing else we can do. Let's ask the U.S. government what they want us to do and how they want to organize our country and how they want to organize the Cuban economy, the Cuban political system, Cuban property, and Cuban business. That would be treason by the Cuban government and the Cuban authorities.

Deputy Foreign Minister of Cuba Carlos Fernandez de Cossio, thank you very much for being here.

For more of that conversation with the deputy foreign minister, you can watch our full interview online on our PBS News YouTube page.

## How nearly 7 months of war have changed daily life in Iran

Late last night, for the third time, the U.S. House of Representatives voted to end the war with Iran. That war powers resolution would stop President Trump from continuing military action against Iran without congressional approval, but it still needs to pass the Senate, and it comes more than six months into a conflict that president said would last weeks.

For a view from inside Iran. I spoke earlier with "News Hour"'s Tehran-based special correspondent Reza Sayah on a rare trip home to the U.S.

Reza, welcome back to the "News Hour." It's good to see you here in person.

So you are there on the ground reporting for us and others.

With the war now approaching seven months, what has daily life become for people in Tehran and across Iran?

I mean, life in war is difficult. Initially, they had to deal with being bombed on a daily basis, then the economic pressure, the uncertainty, and now you have additional sanctions. So war is difficult, and the ones who feel it the most are the average, average people.

At the same time, you see a lot of pride in Iranians. You see a lot of confidence. You see in a lot of Iranians they know that this moment could be defining for the future of their sovereignty and independence. So you have all these emotions together. But definitely, when you're at war, it's difficult and painful.

Let's talk about the leadership and the sort of strength of the regime. As you have been covering, we know the U.S. and Israel have killed dozens of Iran's top military commanders and senior officials, ramping up the economic pressure as well, hit a number of Iran's military installations.

And yet the regime seems to still be standing, new supreme leader, of course, but the same ideological regime. Has that surprised you to see that strength of the regime? Do you know how weakened they have been by the U.S.?

Well, this was a defining moment for them. They took a very hard punch from the world's lone superpower, the United States, two nuclear powers.

And despite their senior political leaders, military leaders, and the supreme leader being assassinated, the leadership is still there. They have different names, different identities, but they are still there. And to many observers, this was an indication that Iran has very powerful, entrenched, well-rooted institutions that can take a punch like this and still survive.

And not only, in their view, have they survived. Now indications are that they're getting more aggressive, that they believe that the United States does not want a fair diplomatic solution, that they want to destabilize the United States. In their view, Israel wants Iran to be a failed state.

And they believe that diplomatic solution, negotiations has not worked. They haven't gotten anything tangible. So they're considering and carrying out measures that indicate that they're being more aggressive.

There were, as you well know, mass protests, millions of Iranians who took to the streets before the war. We saw the Iranian government respond with a brutal crackdown, which we have seen before, reports of at least dozens of the protesters who have been executed since then. Human rights groups say 100 protesters still on death row.

How would you describe the anti-government sentiment right now? Are people still willing to come out and protest?

Well, they haven't come out and protest. It's not -- Iran is 90 million people. There's certainly a significant, considerable segment of the population that at the very least want reform.

There's a segment of the population that wants regime change. And there's also a big, huge part of the population that doesn't want to be the targets of foreign attacks, foreign wars. And they see this moment as a moment of survival for Iran.

So the anti-government protesters that we saw in January have not reappeared in the streets. That doesn't mean that people don't want reform, that don't want change. But I think the population that has made its presence felt is the Iranian population that sees this as a war of survival and they're outraged that they have been bombed by the United States and Israel.

Reza, what is it like for you as a journalist reporting on the ground in Iran for Western outlets? Do you face repercussions or pressure, intervention from government officials?

Repeatedly, there's this narrative that American citizens are in danger there, that the Iran -- the Iranian government systematically goes after dual citizens and American -- and citizens in order to use them as pawns in negotiations.

There have been some arrests of dual citizens, and those have been unjustified. They have been crimes. But I have not seen any systematic effort by the Iranian government over these 20 years that I have reported there to go after dual citizens, and I'm an example.

I'm an American citizen. I have an American passport. I'm able to report freely over there. They have criticized many of my reports, especially in 2009 during the Green Movement. They have been -- they were very critical of my reports, where they didn't grant me press credentials.

But my experience with them has been, if my reports are fair and balanced, and if, in their view, I'm presenting both sides, they're willing to welcome international journalists, including American journalists.

Reza Sayah, special correspondent for us on the ground in Iran, it's good to have you here. Thank you very much.

## Interest rate hike a 'reassuring' sign Fed is acting independently, economist says

It's the first major move by Fed Chair Kevin Warsh to combat elevated inflation. President Trump has long pressured the Central Bank and Warsh's predecessor to lower interest rates.

But, Warsh, who is four months into the job after being handpicked by Mr. Trump to lead the Fed, explained why the committee voted unanimously to raise rates today.

The least well-off are the ones who have the most to gain from stable prices. The decision we made today was the right decision to deliver on the remit that Congress gave us to ensure stable prices.

Moreover, I would say, because of the underlying strength of the economy, because we are, as I mentioned, largely acting consistent with full employment, we can be focused on stable prices. Some months ago, I said we will deliver stable prices. Today's action is consistent with that.

And for more, we're joined now by Julia Coronado, an economist at the University of Texas at Austin, who runs her own firm called MacroPolicy Perspectives.

Julia Coronado, MacroPolicy Perspectives:

So what does this first decision by the Fed under Warsh's leadership tell us about Warsh as a Fed chair committed to fighting inflation and as a Fed chair who is willing to operate independently of the president who appointed him?

Yes, it's it's a pretty quick turnaround for an official who was nominated presumably to bring rates down to turn around and hike rates by September.

And I think that reflects the data he's facing and the committee he has to manage. So the committee is looking at the data, and as Warsh, Chair Warsh, described, unemployment is low, inflation is high. That's a pretty simple formula for Central Bankers.

There was a lot of support. It was a unanimous decision, and most officials think that it's not the last of the rate hikes. So more lie ahead.

So bring this down to the household level. What gets more expensive and how quickly?

Well, we've already seen longer-term interest rates back up.

Over the last month or so, we've seen a tremendous rise in borrowing costs, reflecting expectations that the Fed would need to raise rates, reflecting some of the geopolitical tensions we're seeing ripple through the commodities markets, and the inflation data itself just showing a lot of persistence.

So, in a sense, the borrowing costs consumers face have already increased. Mortgage rates are a lot higher. Auto loan rates are likely going to rise as well. So it's really -- I think you mentioned this is really about the Fed following through and the new Fed chair demonstrating that he is independent of politics and will do what the data, what the economy says is necessary.

And I think that's -- in a sense, that's reassuring that the Fed is acting independently.

On those borrowing costs that you mentioned, the 10-year Treasury has now passed 5 percent. How much does that complicate what the Fed is trying to do here?

Well, it actually is a reflection of what the Fed is trying to do, which is to slow the economy down, in a sense.

It's an unusual picture because the economy is being driven by this enormous A.I. build-out, rather than the typical hot labor market, hot consumer spending. We're in the middle of a huge CapEx boom, and that demand for capital is part of the reason interest rates are higher.

And so it sort of sows the seeds of its own slowdown. They -- there's a lot of borrowing being done by the tech companies, the hyperscalers. That's putting a lot of demand for money, and that's driving up the price of money, and that in turn could slow down that build-out.

Returning to what you said a minute ago, how most Fed policy watchers expect another rate hike, what would have to change for that not to happen?

I mean, one of the reasons that Chair Warsh cited for moving today was the resilience in the economy. The economy just has absorbed blow after blow from tariffs and wars and a lot of disruption, and the unemployment rate is at 4.1 percent, which is very low.

So I think what we would have to see are signs of wobbles in the economy, signs of vulnerability, a labor market where unemployment is on the rise again, or some pretty noticeable cooling off in geopolitical tensions and pipeline inflation pressures and in the inflation data itself.

And neither of those is the most likely scenario in the near future. So most of the officials who wrote down a forecast today, the vast majority think that we will see another rate hike before year end, likely at the December meeting.

And Warsh has promised a different approach to how the Fed communicates. What did you hear from him today in terms of his language, his tone, or what he chose not to say that tells us how he intends to run the Fed differently?

Yes, so there was a couple of differences.

One, he sort of noted that, while decisions have sometimes been made down to the last minute on high-frequency data, that the Fed is waiting and then they make a decision based on sort of one month of data, or that's the perception, that he is going to be stepping back and looking at underlying trends more. So that's one difference.

A second difference is that, in the past, when we the Fed thought it was embarking on maybe a sequence of rate hikes, they would kind of telegraph that at the press conference. Prior chairs would say that they think a sequence of moves is likely. Chair Warsh steered clear of that specific kind of guidance, although he did say -- it's very hard not to provide any forward guidance.

He did say that today's action is the start of the Fed demonstrating that they're going to make good on their inflation target. So the start of something suggests that, yes, more rate hikes probably lie ahead.

Julia Coronado, always great to speak with you. Thanks for making time for us.
