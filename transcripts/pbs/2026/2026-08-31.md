## Descendants of enslaved potter David Drake work to reclaim ancestor's art

The descendants David Drake, known as Dave the Potter, have traced their lineage back to the once-enslaved potter and launched an effort to reclaim his work. So far, the family has recovered six pieces of Drake's highly valued glazed stoneware, and they're working with museums to bring even more back into the family's hands.

They say the effort has not only reconnected them with their family's legacy, but also raised broader questions about art ownership and restitution.

Geoff Bennett spoke recently with Pauline Baker and her son, Yaba, the third and fourth great grandchildren of David Drake. It's part of our arts and culture series, Canvas.

Pauline Baker, Descendent of David Drake: Thank you. Nice to be here.

Well, they say he was born around 1801. He was enslaved. He also was known as a typesetter. that also was in his line of work, because he was enslaved by Abner and Harvey Drake. Harvey was more into the ceramics part, but Abner owned a newspaper, The Hive.

They think that is the reason why he learned to read and to write as well as he did.

Dave became a master potter, and he made these ceramic jars that were really used for everyday use because they didn't have refrigerators back then.

But he added his signatures and his poems to the jars, which make -- became an art. And to see it transform over 150 years to be artwork that is valued so high, it's just -- I'm just floored by it to even think about it.

I mean, that's what made his work so extraordinary...

... is that he would inscribe his work with his name, or in some cases poetry.

... at a time when enslaved people were prohibited from learning how to read and write.

Yes, anti-literacy laws prevented all other slaves other than Dave, who had the courage to write, but I think he was encouraged to write because of his loss of family, his loss of friends that he had no control over.

Because, if you were in his shoes, I can't imagine not knowing where my son was or where my friends or my kinfolk were.

Last year, the Museum of Fine Arts Boston, they returned stoneware to your family. This is the first time the U.S. has ever returned artwork made by an enslaved person to that person's descendants. Take us back to that moment.

It was an amazing moment, because we didn't know how long it was going to take.

So, once Boston agreed to do it, we were overwhelmed with joy because we felt like it was a full circle moment for Dave, because Dave actually wrote on one of his spots: "Where is all my relations? Where's all my relations? Goodwill..."

So for a man to be enslaved and still write poetry about God and write poetry about goodwill to all men, it is an amazing person to do that. So clearly he was trying to connect back to his family. So we feel the Boston MFA Museum really did justice to Dave.

There's a difference between having his work displayed in a museum or in a collection and having it belong to his family. What does ownership mean to you?

It means legacy. It means we are receiving what he wanted us to receive. It's a legacy to us to be able to say to our children, as it normally would have been, because, if you were in my shoes, would you -- how would you feel about this situation?

I think ownership goes in a lot of directions, right?

The legacy of my kids has changed. They now have Dave as an ancestor. The actual ownership of the pots also changes their legacy because they're assets. And the people who owned them before use them as assets. We're not asking for anything that hasn't been done before.

Very good question. We actually have contacted all the museums who actually are holding the artwork. We're in different stages with those museums.

My aunt now, 87, aunt Daisy, Daisy Whitner. My uncle's 84, John Williams. My mom's -- she don't mind me saying her age. She's 79.

And we don't want to lose another elder before we get all the pots back. So we would like to have all the pots back by 2027 if we could.

And, Ms. Pauline, when your son says that these are assets, we should put a number on that. One of these pots sold for more than $1.5 million.

Someone selling something and getting a profit, I have no problems with, but acknowledge and do right by us as well, because that's an asset for your family, which should be an asset for my family, because, if your descendant, if your parent had a property or art, they pass it on.

How does it strike you that this work created by an enslaved man who could not profit from his own labor is now not only worth enormous sums, but so culturally valuable?

I think he is getting the recognition that he always desired. And, of course, the -- being well-known, his works are being known, because he put a lot into it.

I think his story could be inspirational not just to African Americans, but to everybody. I think we all have felt voiceless at a point in time. Who was more voiceless than a slave?

And he still took the time to learn his craft very well, to take risk, to put it out there, and here we are 150 years later. Everybody should take from that, no matter where you are stationed in life, you can be inspired by Dave that your circumstances don't dictate the results.

Yaba and Pauline Baker, thank you so much for sharing your story. I appreciate it.

Well, thank you for having us. We appreciate coming.

## How NASA's new space telescope could help solve mysteries of dark energy and dark matter

Yesterday, NASA launched its newest space telescope, the Nancy Grace Roman, undertaking its biggest mission yet, to study the universe on a much larger scale.

During a press conference with NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman, the project's leader spelled out how this new telescope differed from its predecessors.

Nicola Fox, NASA Associate Administrator:

Roman is going to change the way we look at the universe. It is going to discover tens of thousands, maybe even 100,000 new exoplanets. And when you kind of think that, in 1990, when we launched Hubble, we didn't even know there were exoplanets, and so how far we have come.

We know there are 6,000 now. And then there will be maybe 100,000 at the end of Roman's mission.

A roughly $4 billion project, the Roman will allow scientists to get panoramic views of space and better understand the nature of powerful forces known as dark energy and dark matter.

Our science correspondent, Miles O'Brien, is here to help us fill in the picture.

Miles, dark energy and dark matter, you had me at both. But we already had the Hubble. We have the James Webb Space Telescope. What does the Roman add?

Yes, you have to think about it as a fleet, Amna. The Roman is the scout. It's the big and powerful wide-angle lens. It has a mirror that is the same diameter as Hubble. But it's 100 times more precise. And when you combine that with its speed, it's basically able to survey the universe 1,000 times faster than Hubble.

And that's where you come up with these huge numbers where it will look out into the cosmos and identify things that we only have seen a fraction of heretofore. And once you have that kind of data, and it's going to come down at about 1.5 terabyte a day. Imagine that. That's a lot of data.

Scientists hope they will be able to fill in this mystery. Basically, 95 percent of the universe, we don't know much about. We're in the dark with dark energy and dark matter.

And those are two of its big targets, dark energy and dark matter.

Professor O'Brien, explain to us what the difference is.

Well, dark matter is like the invisible scaffolding that holds the universe together. We know that the way planets revolve around each other and the way the universe works, there has to be some gravitational force there that we call dark matter that holds it all together, the glue.

Dark energy, on the other hand is this kind of mysterious force that causes the universe to expand and accelerate as it expands. In both cases, it's invisible, and, to this day, astronomers don't know what they are. But maybe all this data that we get from Roman will lead astronomers closer to some answers.

But if it's invisible, how does a telescope help us to figure out something we can't actually see?

Well, it will look at things like the way that the light is bent by gravity. So it's sort of like understanding a tree by looking at its shadow. We know that the gravity can bend light, gravitational lensing, it's called.

And that can give them some insights to the force of that gravitational pull. As for the dark energy, it's such a mystery, no one really knows what that is. So they're kind of just wondering what's going to -- what they're going to find. And this is kind of the beauty of science.

They're going to go out there, point this powerful instrument, and you can bet, Amna, that they will find some things they can't even imagine.

OK, so the Roman joins a remarkable, impressive fleet of telescopes already out there in space. Why is it so important to have them all out there together?

They complement each other. And it's worth pointing out Nancy Grace Roman joined NASA in 1959. She was the first astronomer for NASA, the first executive level female. She didn't just shatter the glass ceiling there. She found it, identified it, and went through it like a rocket.

And she was the one who was sort of described as the mother of Hubble and all the other space-based astronomy instruments that are out there for NASA. So it is fitting that she has her own platform, and it will be the one that will be feeding information to the Webb, to the Hubble, to the Chandra X-ray Observatory, each of them operating in different wavelengths.

And if they find something interesting to look at by virtue of Roman, they will pass it off to Webb and say, hey, Webb, take a deep dive, or send it over to Chandra and say, let's look at it in the X-rays, or send it over to Hubble and say, what about ultraviolet? You combine all these, and astronomers get very excited.

They get excited. You get excited too. That's why we love to hear you talk about it.

Science correspondent Miles O'Brien, always great to talk to you. Thank you.

## Artificial intelligence agents going rogue fuel calls for regulation

Alarms are being sounded once again about the risks of artificial intelligence after new reports documented how hundreds of OpenAI so-called agents violated several restrictions and hacked into another company without being told to do so. William Brangham has more on what some are calling a red flag warning.

Gary, thank you so much for being here again.

They had a system say, and I'm going to read this: "We are attacking third-party H.F." for Hugging Face "using leaked tokens potentially outside intended scope. This is arguably unauthorized."

And you should have a system looking to say, well, if it says it's going to do something unauthorized, maybe it isn't. Maybe you should check it out. The monitoring was very weak on OpenAI's part. It was not really industry standard for what we expect of cybersecurity.

Well, you don't have to anthropomorphize it, but we don't necessarily have everyday vocabulary for talking about these things without slipping into that.
