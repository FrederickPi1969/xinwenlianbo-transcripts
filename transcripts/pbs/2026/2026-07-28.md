## On 'Settle In,' Emilee Hackney and Amna Nawaz discuss Appalachian stereotypes

Appalachia is often stereotyped as a region defined by struggling coal mine towns and substance abuse.

Writer Emilee Hackney says those portrayals don't reflect her life growing up in Southwestern Virginia. Her debut memoir, "All That's Unseen," covers growing up inside that world and the political influences that came to shape it.

I spoke to her for the latest episode of our PBS News podcast "Settle In." Here's a clip of that conversation.

You talk about hearing folks back at home as they're learning about candidate Donald Trump, President Donald Trump, about him gaining popularity in Appalachia. They like that he told it like it is, things we have heard in a lot of communities where he has strong levels of support.

Did his strength and his support in the place that you call home, did all that make sense to you at the time?

Emilee Hackney, Author, "All That's Unseen: An Appalachian Memoir": Absolutely. I mean, most of the people I knew were Trump supporters right from the very beginning, because he comes to Appalachia and he says, I'm going to help you. He pays attention.

He makes us feel seen for the first time in decades. And when you are coming from a place that's really struggling, like Appalachia often does, or at least coalfield Appalachia, you have to trust in whoever offers you hope, because what else is there? What else do you hope in?

So, yes, at first, I was like, maybe this is the way. Maybe he's actually going to help. Maybe -- yes, maybe this is right. And I write about going to a rally that he held in Southwest Virginia at Radford University and feeling just the tension of that room didn't feel right to me.

And the way that he behaved, like seeing him in person kind of turned me off from the idea of him. But if I hadn't gone to that rally, I don't know. I don't know if -- I think it would have taken me a longer time to change my opinion of him.

And, even still, when I was at Harvard, I still had conflicted feelings about him because so many people I knew back home were still so supportive of him. And he was early in his first presidency, and we have seen him do a lot of things that now I'm like, oh, my gosh, how could I ever have supported him?

But at that point, it didn't -- it hadn't gotten to that point yet. And so I had these conflicted feelings about him. And once I started really looking into his policies, or lack thereof, in one of my writing classes, in my investigative journalism class, that was what finally I guess made me move -- it -- and started to move me in a different direction.

You have probably seen the comparisons being made now, right, between your book and the book written by Vice President Vance, "Hillbilly Elegy," about his time growing up in Appalachia and then in Ohio.

Do you buy those comparisons? Do you agree with them?

He makes a lot of generalizations about Appalachia based on his own experiences, his own anecdotal experiences. And I don't feel like those are fair characterizations to make.

I think that he probably encountered a lot of what I did. So I go up to Harvard. He goes to Yale,. And he writes about being an oddity to people at Yale, to New Englanders, and I felt that way too. And I encountered this big appetite for, what is Appalachia? Like, what are people like? Everybody has a stereotype in their head.

And he saw that people wanted to know more about Appalachia, but maybe not really. They just wanted confirmation of what they already thought about it. And I think he plays into those stereotypes by using his own stories from his childhood to say, like, all of Appalachia is addicted, like, addiction is endemic, it's such a problem, it's all over, poverty, it's just so widespread.

And I just feel like that's a flattened and non-nuanced characterization of Appalachia. He cannot speak for Appalachia as a whole, as he does in his book.

You feel like his anecdotes, his story don't directly relate to what you experienced growing up in a different part of Appalachia? Is that fair?

Yes, I don't think that his story could speak for anybody else's. So in my book, I'm very careful to say this is my experience in Appalachia. It's not going to be everybody's. And I don't think that he does the same thing. He's not careful that same way.

## Thomas Hampson celebrates U.S. musical history with 'Song of America'

His is a household name in the classical vocal traditions that have their roots in Europe. But for more than 25 years, Thomas Hampson has dedicated himself to reviving and celebrating American traditions in song.

Fred de Sam Lazaro went to see that on full display in Aspen, Colorado, for our arts and culture series, Canvas.

"My days have been so my days have been so wondrous free," which seemed like a fitting start to a concert held on Independence Day weekend, composed, after all, by one of the signers of the Declaration of Independence, Francis Hopkinson, drawing from the poetry of Thomas Parnell.

Thomas Hampson grew up in Spokane, Washington, and had a storybook path to stardom, attending prestigious music academies before his lyrical baritone was discovered. He's performed some 80 opera roles around the world and is a renowned recording artist as well in the solo vocal tradition of leader.

But along with the European greats from Schumann to Verdi, he's also loved the American tradition in song, like Charles Ives, or Harry Burleigh, the prolific 19th century African American composer. Hampson says nothing compares to the rich variety of song in America.

To me, it's the essential e pluribus unum statement.

No, it's exactly not homogeneity; e pluribus unum does not mean the deletion of the difference. In fact, it means listening to the difference.

I think in this very volatile time we're in that one of the great refuges is the arts and humanities and the stories of who we are, especially as America.

All of these composers and poets, they were asking us, what does America sound like when it remembers itself?

You know, people are very agitated today about the indoctrination of young people because of this or that path of education. You can either dam up the ocean or you can teach people to swim. And I prefer to teach people to swim.

## Board packed with Trump allies votes to weaken protections for historic sites

An advisory council packed with allies of President Trump voted Friday to weaken federal oversight of construction projects affecting historic sites.

Critics of the president's controversial projects in Washington are concerned that this move will open the floodgates to endangering preservation of historic and cultural places across the country.

Senior arts correspondent Jeffrey Brown looks at the bigger picture behind this latest move for our arts and culture series, Canvas.

President Trump's proposed arch on a traffic circle in Arlington, Virginia, Native American cultural sites in New Mexico's Chaco Canyon, two of the thousands of historic structure sites and more that could be impacted by a proposed change to rules governing construction on federal lands, plus a new executive directive to place signs outside the Smithsonian National Museum of American History calling into question the exhibitions inside.

Joining me to discuss both is Philip Kennicott, culture critic for The Washington Post.

Let's start with this proposed change to what's known as Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act of 1966. Tell us how it works now and what might change.

Well, at this point, if the federal government wants to develop a piece of property or make changes that would impact a federally historically protected site, they have to go through a process.

And that process is essentially one of consultation. They have to talk to the states and potentially to Native American tribes. And they also have to solicit input from the public. What's just happened is that the federally appointed body that controls this process has just issued a new set of rules that essentially gut the law.

It would minimize the number of properties that are eligible for review. It would minimize the kinds of impacts that are considered relevant. And, most important, it would effectively cut the public entirely out of the process if the government decides they don't want to solicit your opinion and they don't want to hear it.

And in terms of potential impact, now, I mentioned two that are well-known, the controversial arch in D.C. and Chaco Canyon in New Mexico. What other kind of sites are potentially impacted here?

This is essentially the big triumphal arch in Washington coming home to a site anywhere near you. It would impact historic sites all over the country, large and small.

It essentially would allow, theoretically, if a developer wanted to make changes, say, put a golf course next to a historic home or historic landscape,if they got the ear of the president or even the ear of somebody well placed within the relevant federal agency, they could get the public process basically shut down.

They could -- the federal government would decide, no, we don't want to hear from you. And that will greatly speed the development of that property, no matter the consequences.

Now, this change is being led by a little known agency. It's called the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation, all with appointees by President Trump. Do you expect legal challenges to come?

Oh, yes, I think those are already in the works.

And once these laws have gone through the process and been published, you will see a number of avenues that people pursue in legal cases. I think one of these is that the changes are not consistent with the procedures of the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation. And they're not consistent with the larger law, the 1960 law on historic preservation.

They also cut a lot of relevant parties, interested parties, like the states and tribes, out of this process. And so they will have an interest in contesting this as well.

All right, so let's turn to this other development that happened recently, President Trump's directive to put warning sides outside the Museum of American History.

We don't know what those signs will say, but this is clearly part of the continuing attack he's made against the Smithsonian at large. What do you see happening?

Well, yes, as you're right. We don't know what the actual particulars of the signs will say.

But they seem to be an effort to basically breach the trust of the public with the Smithsonian. The president has issued a report that really condemns the Smithsonian for divisiveness, for its focus on wokeness or DEI.

But these reports and these previous efforts to belittle and beleaguer the institution really haven't had much purchase yet. This seems to be a little bit of theater that's meant to sort of garner attention. The president has ordered the National Park Service, another greatly beloved part of the federal government that interacts with the people, to put these signs up.

So, effectively, what it's doing is pitting one beloved group against another, the congressionally chartered Smithsonian Institution.

So two different developments, two different areas in some ways. Do you see any connecting tissue here between the two things we have just talked about?

Well, I think it's about controlling how we tell history, whether that's at the material level of a building or when it comes to the narratives that are intertwined and unfolded in a place like the Smithsonian.

Authoritarian-minded regimes have always understood that there is both an material and an intellectual aspect of history. And if you can erase history in the material world, in the built world, you assert great power to actually erase narratives in places like the Smithsonian. And if you have that power, then, of course, you can control the present and the future.

There is an argument that these regulations take too long, that projects go on for years and years, so something needs to be changed in the way this is governed.

Well, they can cite a couple of cases perhaps where they have been burdensome and taken a long time to do, but we could cite, I think, many, many more cases in which a little bit of deliberation, a little bit of time, a little bit of thoughtfulness has preserved important historic and cultural places.

One has only to think about the speed with which the president demolished the East Wing of the White House to get a perspective on what could happen to historic sites all over the country if this supposedly burdensome process is made essentially effortless, so long as you can get the relevant agencies to follow your particular view of the situation.

All right, Philip Kennicott is culture critic of The Washington Post.

After graduation, the family moved to this rural Kentucky community in June 2022.

Her three boys know that their mom delivers babies. But Dr. Fields says practicing medicine here in Kentucky has changed since the state's near-total abortion ban went into effect almost four years ago.
