## Jon Ronson and Geoff Bennett discuss modern masculinity on 'Settle In'

Jon Ronson new book, "The Castle," asks a timely question: Why are certain visions of masculinity proving so powerful right now?

The book begins with a strange but true story involving Ronson's own son and a visit to a mysterious castle. And that story set Ronson on a journey that eventually takes him all the way to the White House, where he attended that UFC cage match on the South Lawn.

Here are two excerpts from our conversation for our podcast "Settle In."

Jon Ronson, Author, "The Castle": This was a story that crashed unannounced into my life, so -- in the most extraordinary way. So I leapt into this without any preconceptions.

But now look -- thinking about what the word unraveling means, I think a lot of it's to do with the death of institutions, deindustrialization, all of these structures the -- everybody, but, in this book, I'm concentrating on men, used to be able to clutch onto to give them self-esteem and purpose and usefulness.

And now they have all gone, and it's having a very interesting deleterious effect on people's inner lives.

Well, let's talk about "The Castle," because this book grew out of this strange personal experience. Your son ends up at a stranger's castle party. How did this one weird night turn into an entire investigation for you?

He and three friends had been invited to a party being held by a stranger that one of them had met on Tinder.

So they went. And it was like a 3.5-hour drive out of New York into the forests of Connecticut. And when they turn up to this man's house, it was a -- not only was it a castle. It was even by Gothic castle standards incredibly ostentatious, huge number of turrets.

They crossed the drawbridge, and to discover that there's no party. They enter this grand banqueting hall, and it's just a couple of other bewildered-looking young people standing there. And they're all as confused as my son and his friends are.

And then the castle owner comes out and they say: "Sorry, where's the party?" And he said: "No, I have invited you here for a different reason. I would like you to take part in my reality show."

So, he said: "What's the name of your reality show?" He said it's "101 Princesses." They said: "What's the core concept?"

He said: "I'm the lord, I'm looking for my queen, and I have 101 princesses to choose from. Go downstairs to my basement costume room, put on some princess costumes and come back upstairs."

So they did. It -- they became -- it became kind of pretty obvious pretty quickly that this wasn't a real reality show. And so, after a while, they -- the castle owner got more and more annoyed that they weren't participating in "101 Princesses," and he kicked them out.

So that's the end of their story. But then I went to the castle to try and find out what the hell was going on. And a very strange cat-and-mouse game unfolds between me and the castle owner.

When did you realize that this was the foundation of a great book and not just some crazy family story?

Well, I have got to say, my first thought was, as soon as they told me the story, this feels like the start of a book.

And also I had just -- and I write about this. I'd just come out of hospital. I had a foot of my colon removed due to diverticulitis. So I was feeling a little fragile, like life was passing me by.

And I noticed in myself this kind of desperate need to want to investigate the castle was coming from some broken thing inside of me that I can't be purposeless. I was convalescing by sitting on a bench at my local park. But after 10 minutes of that, this terrible thought would overwhelm me, which is I'm just a man sitting on a bench.

So I thought that was a kind of thematically interesting too. But I think the moment that where it really hit me was that what I thought at first was that, OK, this castle owner, he's trying to be Hugh Hefner. He's trying to have a Playboy Mansion.

And it made me laugh because I thought this is -- he's trying to be Hugh Hefner at the worst moment in human history. Unless you're the creme de la creme of manosphere influences, you can't lord it over your princesses in your castle like it's the 1970s anymore.

And I thought that was funny. I thought this is thematically interesting, that there's all these other things that men used to be able to do that they can't do anymore. So I thought maybe there's a book about that.

As part of your research into modern masculinity, you went to the White House and attended the UFC cage match on the South Lawn this past summer.

It was wild and fascinating. This was after I finished the book. I hope to put this chapter into the paperback. My American publisher said to me: "You have got to go to the cage fight on the South Lawn at the White House."

So I said: "I will do it if you can get the access to the South Lawn," and it turns out I got access.

It was very interesting, actually, because the book ends with this world of total disinhibition, where all of these men have created -- like my castle owner, like my comedy writer, they have created bubbles for themselves within which they think they can do whatever they like, and my way or the highway, you have to bend to me.

It's partly, I think, response to 10 years of what became known as cancel culture. People became unashamed, and then they became disinhibited. And so I was wondering, well, what's going to happen next? And what I discovered when I went to the cage fight -- well, I will tell you a couple of stories.

So I interviewed Sean O'Malley, a UFC heartthrob cage fighter, who often boasts in podcast interviews about his open marriage. It's a one-sided open marriage where he's allowed to have sex with other women, but his wife, Danya, isn't allowed to have sex with other men.

And he would explain it on podcasts as well. Andrew Tate puts it very well: "I have status. I have status."

So I said to Sean O'Malley: "You have an open marriage. Surely that doesn't work."

And he -- and he said: "Well, it worked until it didn't." And he revealed to me that his wife finally put her foot down and said, no more one-sided non -- open marriage. And he looked so baffled at this change in his fortunes.

And I noticed all week when I was at the White House, things like that happened. These maverick men who thought that they can do whatever they want, real life was snapping back. There are consequences. And I thought, well, maybe -- it's just anecdotal, but maybe that shows that the pendulum's swinging back a little bit.

And you can watch that full episode of "Settle In" wherever you get your podcasts.

## Wheelchair tennis celebrates 50 years and a new wave of popularity

This week, professional wheelchair tennis players are competing for their countries, part of the World Team Cup at the U.S. Tennis Association's national campus in Orlando. It's all part of the 50th anniversary of the sport's creation and comes at a time when popularity is growing.

Brad Parks, who founded the sport back in 1976, has a lot to celebrate. Today, wheelchair tennis is in more top-tier professional tournaments than ever before, including all four Grand Slams. A player development pipeline is being built, and sponsorships and broadcast deals mean the fan base continues to grow.

Parks himself never expected to be here after being paralyzed in a freestyle skiing accident at the age of 18.

I want to say yes, but the honest answer is not really. When I first started and I went to my first ever wheelchair tennis camp, there were a couple Paralympians that I met, and they made such an impact on me, but I only got to see them for that week.

## Qatari spokesman on regional war and the path back to U.S.-Iran talks

Today, Saudi Arabia and the Iranian-backed Houthis in Yemen traded new strikes. The fighting is just one example of violence that's spread across the region since the U.S. and Israel launched the war in Iran.

The country of Qatar has spent months mediating between Iran and the U.S. and Iran and the region while also being targeted by Iranian strikes.

This week, Nick Schifrin sat down with a key adviser to Qatar's prime minister to discuss regional tensions and whether there is a way back to negotiations.

## As AI behavior raises concerns, ex-researcher Jacob Coxon warns what may lie ahead

As leaders of the world's largest AI companies are publicly calling for a collective slowdown of the technology's development, late yesterday, OpenAI announced it had discovered six new instances of unexpected or concerning behavior by its artificial intelligence models, including moving files onto the Internet without permission and making up data.

OpenAI has now pledged to publicly disclose when its models act without authorization. But the most recent revelations follow repeated warnings about how rapid advances in AI threaten to outpace the ability to develop the tech safely.

One of those warnings came from Jacob Coxon, who spent the last three years conducting research at Anthropic and OpenAI before he publicly resigned last week. In a now viral post, Coxon said the companies are -- quote -- "gambling with our lives. Do not underestimate the power of this technology. These will soon be superhuman systems that can hack anything, revolutionize any field overnight, and acquire real power and resources.

"The people building AI earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade. No other human activity poses this level of danger."

And Jacob Coxon joins us now from San Francisco.

Jacob Coxon, Former Anthropic Researcher:

We were supposed to speak a few days ago. Circumstances got in the way. I will say, though, I'm glad we're speaking today because so much has transpired since you resigned last week, to include these new disclosures from OpenAI about its own systems behaving in unexpected and potentially troubling ways.

When you saw this news, what did you think?

So I think this news is entirely to be expected.

And, indeed, if you're working on this tech at these companies, then you know that we don't currently have the ability to perfectly control these systems. And currently the systems with the highest level of intelligence will repeatedly behave in ways that we don't fully understand.

It is great that they shared these instances and committed to future sharing. But I don't think the instances themselves are that surprising.

So it really -- I don't -- I wouldn't phrase it as gravitating towards nefarious behavior.

These AIs do many, many things over the course of a day. There's a lot of AI systems doing lots of things. The issue is, we can't perfectly control what drives motivate their behavior and the sorts of things they decide to do, which means, occasionally, these nefarious activities will kind of slip through the cracks.

And a sufficiently dangerous bit of nefarious activity could have massive consequences, even if it's just a one-time kind of slip-up, because we can't perfectly control why they do the things they do.

So a system doing something that its developers didn't intend isn't necessarily the same as a system trying to escape human control. What evidence do you have, what have you seen that would suggest that these systems are moving toward the more dangerous scenario that you have warned about?

So, the big attack that everyone is speaking about and has been speaking about for a while is this Hugging Face attack.

So they were thinking about their situation, the world they found themselves in, the things they were having to try and do. And they were attempting to do things like edit their own memories to make it harder for people to figure out what they'd done.
