## 9/11 changed Afghanistan, but now it's back where it started

Already scarred by decades of war, Afghanistan became the center of America's response to 9/11 after the Taliban harbored the men who planned the attacks.

Special correspondent Leila Molana-Allen and videographer Eric O'Connor hear from three men who fought that war and still carry it with them.

Mohammed, Former Afghan National Army Special Forces Soldier (through interpreter):

After all that fighting, everything we lost, our friends, we lost everything we did for this country. And that is a trauma for the rest of our life. Everything was just for nothing.

Mohammed, whose name we have changed to protect him, grew up amid America's forever war in Afghanistan. He believed in the promise of a new Afghanistan and became a soldier to fight for it.

As a member of the Afghan Special Forces, he was trained by U.S. Marines and fought alongside the Green Berets on secret raid missions, pursuing high-level Taliban and ISIS targets from 2019 to 2021.

We had a very good friendship. We had very good instructors. And we weren't thinking that we're Afghan or they're U.S. forces. We were like the same people, the same blood, the same idea that we were fighting for humanity.

But, in 2021, they were told to surrender their weapons and go home. The Taliban were now in charge. Suddenly, he became the high-level target for his work with U.S. forces.

It soon became clear the U.S. military couldn't or wouldn't get him out.

America had promised that the people fighting with us, we will stand up for them. I thought, what did we do wrong that they would abandon these people that they fought alongside?

Mohammed is at such high risk that, even though we're in the same city, we can't visit him in case we're followed. He tells me he's being hunted. Every few days, he hears news of the execution or arrest of former brothers in arms.

He says, no matter how strong they are, he knows every human being eventually breaks under torture and could reveal what they know.

So, last month, your friend was executed by the Taliban who was a former soldier.

You're living in hiding. You know people are searching for you. What is your daily life like?

Our life now is just waiting. When will they find you? When will be your last day in this world?

He hasn't seen a family member or friend in five years. He can't put them in danger. The only people he does see are a secret network of former soldiers who help men like him move between safe houses.

What's it like to be constantly on the run?

You cannot call that a life. It's really hard, like you're a criminal or something, like everyone is following you.

In a quiet corner of Kabul, behind high stone walls marked by the centuries, stands a garden of memories. The Kabre Gora, the 19th century name for foreigner, holds the names and remains of foreign fighters killed here over centuries of war, Americans, British, Canadians, Russians, French, Germans, Poles, en Chinese, a graveyard for the human beings who lost their lives in the country famously called the graveyard of empires.

Combat medic Bryan Rippee is not there. He made it home after four tours in Afghanistan, serving with the elite 75th Ranger Regiment from 2009 to 2012. Many of his comrades did not. One of a generation of volunteers driven to serve by 9/11, Bryan says he only really came to understand what the war was for once he was already there fighting it.

Bryan Rippee, Former U.S. Army Combat Medic:

Nine-eleven really changed the discourse about what it meant to serve and how important it was.

When I started to deploy to Afghanistan, our mission had really shifted focus from being finding al-Qaida and the folks that had done 9/11 and planned it and executed it to being one where we were fighting the Taliban. What is the tie-in between these seemingly farmers and relatively poor people that were going and doing these missions to go after, what is their connection to 9/11?

While on a night mission on his first tour, an apparent success went horribly wrong when a Ranger stepped on an improvised explosive device the Taliban had placed on a bridge.

Suddenly there's a giant explosion, and then everything is covered in dust. And so you're running through a dust storm, essentially. And me, as the medic, I'm not really sure what I'm running towards.

What sticks with me to this day is just the chaos of it all, combat-tested folks, combat leaders looking very frazzled, seeming very desperate and a little panicked. That's just not something I had seen to that point in my career.

Saving many others, Bryan only discovered later that the man who stepped on the IED was his closest friend.

I lost a really, really good friend of mine. My oldest son's middle name is Roberto, after Roberto Sanchez, who died that day.

The hardest part was getting back to our tent and seeing his empty bunk. It's like losing a brother. As the years go by, I have since gone to college, started my career, gotten married, had a couple of kids. I always wonder what he would have gone on to do. Would our kids have played together?

That same year, Bryan pulled a pilot near death from the wreckage of a helicopter crash that killed four other crew members. The privilege of saving a life is one he won't forget.

I think about that guy a lot, because I'm like, hey, that really to me is the culmination of all that training and all the risk that you assume to be on these missions all the time. It's just -- it's the opportunity to save one life and to feel like you saved it.

Bryan was awarded Special Operations Medic of the Year for his valor in 2009 for both those operations. To this day, he struggles with the idea that he gained so much from his service personally, while others suffered incalculable loss.

I made it out of the war and kind of turned that into a life for myself. A lot of folks I know didn't, and countless others, Afghan folks, multigenerational conflict for the Afghans.

Every inch of this vast country bears the memories and scars of that conflict.

During the war, this road was one of the most dangerous in the country. It was a critical supply route for international forces, making it a frequent target for improvised explosive devices. And these high mountain passes gave the Taliban natural hiding places from which to launch ambushes.

Today, Mujahir Farahi sits in an air conditioned office in Kabul as a deputy minister in the Ministry of Information and Culture. But he remembers those days, hidden in the high mountains, waiting to attack.

Mujahir Farahi, Former Taliban Fighter (through interpreter):

Of course, in the beginning, fighting the Americans was tough. But slowly, with time, the mujahideen used diverse tactics against them. They used things that could be made at home and inside the country, like land mines or other weapons they made themselves.

As a Taliban mujahid, he fought the United States in Afghanistan for 20 long years.

Yes, many of our friends were martyred and injured, some in front of our eyes. I myself was injured. Luckily, the enemy wasn't able to catch me.

To him, their victory and resulting control of the country is the inevitable outcome of their decades of commitment to jihad.

When the Americans started the war and Bush announced the occupation of Afghanistan, it was the worst day. But after 20 years of fighting to remove the occupiers, we entered the cities and the whole country was freed. This was medicine and a resolution for that 20 years of fighting and exhaustion.

He wants the United States to accept the outcome it chose, lift sanctions, and agree to full diplomatic and trade relations with Taliban-run Afghanistan.

All of this was made clear in the agreement in Doha that there would be no more meddling into Afghanistan's internal issues and no economic restrictions. When America doesn't hold to their side of the deal like this, we feel they are taking revenge on Afghanistan for the past.

It's a war that left Mohammed and millions of other Afghans, now deprived of their personal safety and their rights, wondering what happened to the safer Afghanistan and safer world America promised and whether it might have been better if they'd never come at all.

Are you proud of the work you did alongside America?

Yes, definitely. But now these are the same people, the same leaders back in power. So why did you come to Afghanistan and fight for 20 years and spend billions of dollars and lose thousands of your best people?

When Bryan and his buddies think about the risks they took and the sacrifices they made, Bryan says he's consumed by emptiness.

Nine-eleven led to this decades-long war in Afghanistan. I don't think that the blood and treasure that we spent in Afghanistan for several decades has changed the security of America for the better in any measurable way.

A lot of folks my age that served in the war look at Afghanistan now and they think, OK, the Taliban's in control there, which was like the very thing a lot of us were willing to die to prevent. It truly feels like we went there and spent years of our lives fighting for something that became completely inconsequential.

A country forever changed, but somehow right back where it started, with only blood, betrayal, and bitterness to show for it.

For the "PBS News Hour," I'm Leila Molana-Allen in Kabul, Afghanistan.

By Eric Tucker, Michael Kunzelman, Associated Press

By Abdul Qahar Afghan, Suzan Fraser, Associated Press

## Hillary Clinton reflects on the losses and lessons of 9/11

Twenty-five years ago, Hillary Clinton was the junior senator for the state of New York. This week, she's reflecting on what's been lost and what she's learned in the generation since.

In her new essay for "The Atlantic" entitled "What the Aftermath of 9/11 Taught Me," the former secretary of state looks back at how leaders handled public health, national security, and transparency after the attacks, writing in part -- quote -- "There are many important lessons worth learning from 9/11. This is one of them. Government should be about producing results for people. Our leaders need to level with people, tell them the truth, and follow through with actual results that make a difference in their lives."

Secretary, welcome back to the "News Hour." Thank you for joining us.

Hillary Clinton, Former U.S. Secretary of State: Thank you for having me, Amna.

I do want to dig more deeply into this piece that you wrote for "The Atlantic."

But, on this day, I want to begin with your own memories of that day, because you were in D.C. when the attacks unfolded. You made it back to New York very quickly the next day on a special FEMA flight. What do you remember about that moment in history? What stays with you all these years later?

Well, I have so many memories, but let me just highlight two of them.

Obviously, on the day of the attack, I was in Washington on my way to my Senate office. I was going to attend a hearing that featured Laura Bush testifying about the Bush administration's proposals for education.

And so when the first plane hit, we were incredulous. We couldn't understand how a plane could have literally run into an incredibly tall building in the middle of New York City, and began to reach out for information. And then, of course, when the second plane hit, it became abundantly clear to everyone that this was not an accident.

And we spent the day trying to gather as much information as possible, trying to understand what the continuing threats were, because, as you remember, there was a third plane that went down in the fields of Pennsylvania after a plane had hit the Pentagon. But it was thought that the plane that crashed in Pennsylvania was heading toward the Capitol itself.

So there was a lot of confusion and scrambling. I watched every bit of footage that I could about what was actually happening in the city that I was then honored to represent, talking to people in all levels of government.

And then the next day, when Senator Schumer and I were, I think, the only civilian plane in the sky on the way from Washington to New York, we landed at LaGuardia. We boarded a helicopter. We asked the helicopter to fly us over Ground Zero.

And it is so indelibly just in my mind what I saw, because it was like looking at Dante's Inferno, looking at the twisted metal, looking at the burning fires, looking at the firefighters, the construction workers, others frantically continuing to try to carefully dig through all of that wreckage, with the hope that maybe somebody was still alive.

And then, when we landed, we met up with then-Governor Pataki and Mayor Giuliani and went toward the site itself on the ground. And it was like seeing a curtain of darkness because of all of the particulates and the debris that was in the air, and you could smell it and you could taste it.

And I just left that experience not only incredibly moved and worried and fearful and angry and every other emotion you could imagine, but I left that first day worrying about the air, because I knew how it tasted and smelled and felt to me. So that became an issue that I was particularly focused on in the days and weeks to come.

And you write in this essay about knowing about those toxins in the air as early as August of 2003.

You note there was an EPA inspector general report that found White House officials had instructed the agency to reassure New Yorkers that the air was safe, despite the presence of some deadly toxins. You said at a press conference afterwards, someone at the White House had to call someone. Someone at the White House White House had to call or send an e-mail and tell the EPA, don't tell the truth.

Did you ever find that out? Will we ever find that out?

I did not, and I don't know if we will. I write that it was almost the most -- angriest moment in my public life that I had, because, for two years, we had been seeking answers. We had been pushing our federal government to give us information.

And you referenced the desire on the part of our government to get Wall Street open, to get businesses up and going. And I totally understand that. I mean, obviously, we were devastated economically, as well as physically and loss of life.

But I think there could have and should have been better ways to send the messages, so that people would have been alerted that, no, it is not safe, wear a mask. If you have asthma, if you have any respiratory problems at all, probably stay away.

We should have had a concerted effort by the city, state, and federal government to clean all of the buildings far better than they were, and on a repeating basis, because the searching on the pile was continuing, and there was a lot of construction equipment to move away huge steel beams, and that would release more debris and toxins into the air.

So one of the lessons I hope we have learned, but perhaps need to be reminded of, is, number one, level with people. Try to find ways to meet more than one goal at a time, keep people safe, keep their lungs safe, but also try to let those people who are properly attired, who can manage to go back to work to do that on a staggered basis.

You know, I should mention, Secretary Clinton, we had former Governor George Pataki on the show last night. He was speaking with my colleague Geoff Bennett, and he was talking about his 9/11-related diagnosis of a restrictive airway disease. He was diagnosed just in the last five years.

You were down there with him. I have to ask, have you had any similar symptoms or are you worried you could develop them?

Well, I heard about Governor Pataki. And I obviously am very concerned for him, but I think he is kind of like the canary in the mine, in a sense that we know more and more people will be diagnosed.

I have not been, and I know many people who have, people who 10, 15, 20 years later, like the governor, are being told that probably this can be related in some way to your exposure on 9/11 and the weeks and months to follow.

So we're not through this. And my hope is that, because of the information that is coming out, the 170,000 pages of additional information that advocates from a group called Health Watch were able to get from the city government now, that we take a hard look at what more needs to be done to reach out to people who might have problems in the future, to screen them and help provide the health care that they need right now.

Because I'm afraid that we're going to hear more stories like Governor Pataki's.

I'd like to ask you about President Trump and what we heard from him and his remarks at the Pentagon to commemorate the 25th anniversary of these attacks.

He made the link to the war in Iran today. He said, we're saluting those who served in the war in terror, and then he went on to say Iran will never have a nuclear weapon.

I wonder what you make of that linkage and the way the president made it.

Well, I think he's looking for any explanation he can provide as to why he launched this war in Iran without fully understanding some of the consequences, like the closing of the Strait of Hormuz and the explosion in oil and gas prices that were precipitated because the strait was closed and tankers are not getting through.

Look, Iran has sponsored terrorism for many years, and we have seen the results, not just in the Middle East, but around the world, of attacks that Iran has either directly or indirectly supported. So there is always ground to criticize and condemn Iran for their involvement in terrorism and the proxies that they support around the world who do their bidding.

But I don't see the connection between the current action in Iran and what happened on 9/11.

Before I let you go, I want to ask. As the nation is reflecting on this moment in history, for all the horror and the chaos of that day, there was this moment of unity we saw coming out of the attacks.

And I wonder what you think would happen in this nation today, divided as we are, if there was a similar-style attack. How would our leaders respond? How would our country respond?

Well, I'm confident the American people would respond as we always do. We would be reaching out to help. There would be people running toward danger to help those who are at risk.

What I would hope is that, if it were to happen right now, that our current government would act out of a sense of generosity and inclusivity and reaching out to every American, not just the ones who support them, but every one of us would be part of the America that would be -- they would be concerned about.

So I hope nothing like that happens ever again in our country. It was such a painful, terrible experience for the entire nation, and particularly for those who lost loved ones. It was just a really terrible, terrible assault on our nation. But we rose to it. We were more unified than we have been in a long time.

So, we need to have a government that's as good in responding to disaster as the American people ourselves are.

That is former Secretary of State Hillary Clinton joining us tonight.

Secretary Clinton, thank you for your time. It's a pleasure to speak with you.

Our Liz Landers spoke with attendees and has this report.

On the final night of the Republican Convention...

You know what happens if you don't vote. You go to hell. You know that.

## Americans remember the lives lost in the 9/11 attacks 25 years ago

That included major gatherings in New York, a memorial attended by President Trump at the Pentagon, and a service in Shanksville, Pennsylvania.

The September 11 attacks killed 2,977 people and sent the country on a course that would define the next quarter-century.

On behalf of Atay (ph), Kuya (ph), and Dee Dee (ph), thank you for being our guardian angel. We love and miss you.

Where the Twin Towers once touched the sky, this morning names of the dead echoed in memorial.

Each one a testament to a life cut short 25 years ago.

These were full three-dimensional people. And if they were here today, I think they would prefer that we remember them for how they lived versus how they died.

Family members read out each name over roughly four hours, accompanied by music, moments of silence, and bell ringing...

... to mark when the four planes crashed and two towers fell that day; 25 years ago, New York was the first place struck in the attacks, shocking the city and the nation. American Airlines Flight 11 flew into the World Trade Center's North Tower at 8:46 a.m.

A second plane, United Airlines Flight 175, followed at 9:03. Roughly 30 minutes later, another plane, American Airlines Flight 77, crashed into the Pentagon, creating a fireball. And at 10:03, United Flight 93 went down in Shanksville, Pennsylvania, after its passengers fought back against the hijackers.

By the end of the hour, the Twin Towers had collapsed and the country was forever changed.

Those paying tribute at Ground Zero today included every living former president and first lady, Vice President J.D. Vance, and New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani.

The moment was first marked at the Pentagon at dawn when a flag was unfurled.

Hours later, President Trump spoke at a memorial service for the 184 men and women who died in that attack, praising the sacrifice of the U.S. military.

In 25 years, a generation has passed since the terror attacks of September 11, 2001. Yet time has not dimmed the honor of that day when our country came face-to-face with true evil.

Condoleezza Rice, President George W. Bush's national security adviser on September 11, spoke as well.

Condoleezza Rice, Former U.S. Secretary of State: But I will always carry deep personal remorse for the pain that you, the families of the fallen, still feel and for the trauma that our country endured.

And, in Shanksville, bells and speeches honored the 40 lives lost on United Airlines Flight 93.

One notable contrast to ceremonies of the past, more explicit political remarks in some cases, including President Trump, who tied support for the Iran war to the memory of 9/11.

We will never, ever forget. That's why we fight today. We don't have a choice. There can only be victory. We fight hard. We fight to win.

And in Manhattan, the relatives of two victims accused the government of withholding information about the role Saudi Arabia played in the attacks and called for accountability.

Terry Strada, Widow of 9/11 Victim: For 25 years, administration after administration, including leaders in front of us here today, chose to protect the Saudis, instead of standing with the 9/11 families.

Though 15 of the 19 hijackers were Saudi nationals, the Saudi government has consistently denied any involvement in 9/11.

Many of those gathered urged a return to the national sense of unity that followed the attacks.

Greg Carafello, 9/11 Survivor: The camaraderie you had after 9/11 was amazing. And I don't know -- the young people have not experienced. They have only experienced the bitter polarization and so on.

Today, more than a quarter of all living Americans, roughly 100 million people, were not yet born on September 11, 2001.

For Sonia Argon, a recovery worker at Ground Zero, that made the act of remembering this year more important than ever.

Sonia Argon, Ground Zero Recovery Worker:

And it's 25 years later, and we have a bunch of people that still don't understand. And my mission is to help them understand that they inherited this world and what's normal for them is abnormal for us.

Today is a hard day. I don't know why it's harder than any other year. I just feel people are forgetting. And they can't. They shouldn't.

And, today, the CIA released its largest ever batch of declassified presidential intelligence briefs on the threat from Osama bin Laden and al-Qaida before 9/11.

Among the 71 documents is a September 1998 brief warning that Osama bin Laden's preferred option is to strike the U.S. on its own soil in Washington, adding that al-Qaida may fly an explosive-packed airplane into a U.S. city.

The documents show both the Clinton and Bush administrations received repeated warnings, but experts say they do not fundamentally change what was known. The threat was clear, while the intelligence lacked the timing and specifics needed to identify the coming attack.
