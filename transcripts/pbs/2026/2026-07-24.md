Well, prosthetics are typically designed to blend in as replacements for missing body parts, but, increasingly, they're becoming tools for creative expression, especially in the niche community of ocular prosthetics, or artificial eyes.

Our senior arts correspondent, Jeffrey Brown, meets a Portland eye maker whose designs are helping clients see themselves in a new light. It's part of our ongoing coverage of the intersection of arts and health for our Canvas series.

You accessorize. That's how you think of the -- all the eyes you have now?

Yes. I'm lucky enough that I get to accessorize with something that is a medical necessity.

Yee lost your left eye to cancer at 18 months old and spent years wearing a prosthetic designed to mirror her natural eye.

I knew I was different. I never felt like I was missing something. It wasn't until I was older that I got a little more self-conscious about it. I felt like a prosthetic in general wasn't to make me feel good about myself, but it was to make others feel comfortable looking at me.

Everything changed in 2021 when she started wearing a new eye featuring a shimmering gold iris that was meant to grab attention, not deflect it.

Standing out to me is more important, in fact, than just hiding who I am to make others around feel comfortable.

In an office in nearby Portland, Oregon, where decorative eyes watch from nearly every corner, Christina Leitzel, board-certified as an ocularist, crafts the bull designs that have now become her trademark, so-called fun eyes.

Christina Leitzel, Center for Ocular Prosthetics: I think there's something in me that I have always wanted to help people. I originally wanted to make prosthetic limbs.

As an art student in Philadelphia, she was drawn to artists like Sophie de Oliveira Barata, who turned prosthetics into powerful art pieces.

I just thought it was such a beautiful art form to blend both art and science and then give someone back something that they lost.

Most eye prosthetics have two parts, a custom acrylic shell and a surgically implanted sphere that restores volume to the eye socket. The implant is often made from a porous material that allows tissue to grow into it, helping the prosthetic move with the wearer's natural eye.

Half of Leitzel's work is still creating prosthetic eyes that seamlessly match the real thing. But it's the fun eyes where her artistry comes alive, each one telling a story.

They could be anything from a cat eye, where it's a pet that had just passed.

I asked Christina to make me an eye so I can remember Noodles more, and just kind of have her with me all the time.

Or a portrait of a wife who died. The message that I got was, can you paint the portrait of my wife? Because I would like to have her see everything I'm seeing.

On social media, her creations range from glittery glow-in-the-dark and color-shifting eyes to American flags, sparkling night skies, and soccer balls.

When you have an ocularist, most people, like, they confide, they share their stories.

Very, very personal. And it's kind of scary to be so vulnerable and share that part of you. So I think it's special in that way to be a part of someone's life and change them.
