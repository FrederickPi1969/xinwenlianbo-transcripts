## Group urges Congress to adopt proportional representation to end redistricting battles

We turn now to our series "On Democracy," where we hear a range of perspectives on how government should function, what's led to this moment in American history, and where the country goes next.

With fewer than 100 days to go until the midterm elections, high-profile battles over redistricting have become a fixture in political headlines. A number of Republican-led states under pressure from President Trump have redrawn their congressional maps to gain an election edge. Some Democratic-led states have followed suit.

Those battles have already this year led to the elimination of majority-Black districts in Louisiana, Alabama, and Tennessee. One group of scholars is sounding the alarm on the consequences of gerrymandering; 500 of the nation's top democracy, voting rights, and legal experts have signed an open letter to Congress urging lawmakers to consider one solution, proportional representation.

These scholars argue that single-member districts are -- quote -- "failing to deliver fair and representative outcomes" and are the result of outdated electoral rules that worsen gerrymandering. A proportional system, they say, is a practical remedy.

Here with us to unpack this is one of the signatories. That's Pamela Karlan, a law professor from Stanford University.

Pam, welcome to the show. Thanks for joining us.

So let's just start with why you decided to sign on to this letter and why now.

Well, our system of electing people to Congress is broken. Over the last several years, what we have seen is the Supreme Court has given a total green light to gerrymandering, partisan gerrymandering, and it's gutted the Voting Rights Act.

And the upshot of this is that instead of voters being able to elect the candidates of their choice, politicians are picking the voters of their choice. And that's not healthy for a democracy.

There was a similar letter that went out four years ago that had some 200 backers for it. The fact that you're now up to 500 people arguing for this system, does that tell us something about consensus around this or this particular moment?

I mean, one of the things that we have seen over the last year-and-a-half is that states used to just redraw their congressional districts after the census came out, and now they're redrawing them again and again in an effort to squeeze out the last bit of partisan advantage.

And, as I said, nobody can look at that and think that that's a good system.

So you argue in the letter that gerrymandering's gone too far, and that, in your words, the way to end redistricting wars is to make redistricting matter far less. So just explain for us how proportional representation would do that.

Well, instead of electing members of Congress from single-member districts, so we have 435 districts now, instead, what states that elect multiple members would do is, they would create districts that elect more than one member, and in which who gets elected depends on the proportion of votes that each party gets.

So, for example, in a state like Louisiana, which has six congressional seats, you might have two districts, each of which elect three people. And what that would do is, it would enable numerical minorities to elect some candidates of their choice.

So it wouldn't be as winner-take-all as the system now is.

Pam, even as the system could seek to solve one problem, could it create new ones? I mean, one of the chief frustrations we hear from voters is gridlock within the systems. Could a proportional system mean more parties or third independent parties are now empowered, they have to work towards coalitions?

Does it potentially create more gridlock?

I don't think it's likely in the American system, because we're not a parliamentary system. We are a presidential system.

And even if we were electing people from multiple districts, most of those districts would have three or four representatives, and that would mean that people would still have an incentive to vote for one of the major parties, as long as they're actually satisfied with what the major parties are doing.

Just for some context here, we know the U.S. is somewhat unique, in that the majority of nations around the world that have elected representation use proportional representation or mixed systems in some way to elect their legislators, or a little over 60 percent of those countries.

Why hasn't proportional representation been seriously considered in the U.S. before?

Well, one thing is that our system for electing members of Congress has been fixed since 1842.

And there's actually a really interesting speech that one of the senators gave in 1867, and he said, if we had known about proportional representation at the time, we might have gone for proportional representation, rather than districting.

Once you have a system in place, especially in a country like the United States, it's very sticky, because the members of Congress, they know they can get elected from single-member districts, and they don't know necessarily what would happen if we had a proportional system.

And so it's hard unless there actually turns out to be a political movement behind it to change the way elections are conducted.

Well, that leads to my next question, which -- how realistic is it to implement this kind of system right now, especially given that the people who are navigating the system and benefiting from it would be loath to try to change that system at the same time?

In the short run, it's not going to change. I mean, you really have to build a political movement behind this.

And part of what I hope we're going to start doing is having a real discussion about why our current system of electing members of Congress just doesn't work anymore. And it's creating a system in which, although Congress is divided between the Democratic and Republican Parties, individual states don't have representation that's fair to people on both sides of the aisle in those states.

If the system doesn't change, if there's no serious election reform, what do you see happening in the years ahead?

Well, what I see happening is people becoming increasingly disillusioned with the system, people believing less and less that our democracy works, and that's not a healthy world in which to live.

Pamela Karlan, law professor from Stanford University, thank you so much for your time. Really appreciate it.

It all comes after the Federal Reserve voted yesterday to keep interest rates unchanged, despite three dissents, all arguing in favor of a rate increase.

To help make sense of it all, we're joined now by Greg Ip. He is chief economics commentator at The Wall Street Journal.

So, Greg, I'm going to ask you to just pick up on what we heard from Ian Ralby there when it comes to the impact of the war in Iran on the U.S. economy. If we're moving from a two-choke point crisis to a three-choke point crisis, more disruptions to oil supplies and global trade, broadly speaking, what's ahead for the U.S. economy?

Well, what is surprising is that, despite all the turmoil that the war has created for the U.S. and global economy, including very high gasoline prices and interest rates, is that the American consumer seems to be shrugging it off.

Now, we did hear that growth in the second quarter came in low at 1.5 percent. But that actually -- if you look under the hood, things were a lot stronger, very, very strong growth in consumer spending, especially on durable goods. The reason growth came in low was that a lot of that spending was on imports and/or came out of inventories. So it didn't lead to higher domestic production.

So that tells us, at least thus far, the impact of higher energy prices has been pretty mild for the consumer. Now, that might be for a couple of reasons. One is that there were a lot of tax refunds in the second quarter. That probably helped. The other is that we have this A.I. boom that's still going on, and that has lifted the stock market. So people who are wealthy and have a lot of stock market wealth are still spending a lot.

Looking ahead, I do think that the pressure of those higher interest rates, the end of tax refunds, and possibly some flattening of the stock market means that the consumer could struggle a bit more.

In terms of how people can look at this, because there is some confusion, let's just revisit some of those highlights from the GDP report today.

We did say there that the U.S. GDP grew at 1.5 annually in the second quarter, lower than expected, down from 2.1 percent annual growth in the first quarter. At the same time, consumer spending, as you mentioned, ticked up. That's now 3.2 percent annually. That's up from just 0.5 percent in the first quarter.

There seem to be a lot of mixed signals here. So what do you take away from all of that?

So these numbers can be very confusing, partly because of the fact that, in any given quarter, you can have people spending money, and they might be spending it on stuff that we make here at home, or they may be spending it on stuff that we import or stuff that was already in inventory.

And it looks like, in the second quarter, a lot of that spending was on stuff that we imported or was already in inventory. That's why the GDP number seemed low. The production number seemed low, but spending was very strong.

I would expect that some of that spending would start to flow through to domestic production in coming months. So the underlying picture here is still of a resilient economy and a pretty healthy consumer. But, again, we have to look at the fact that going forward with energy prices and interest rates still pretty high, that may not last.

Well, tell me more about that, because when every time we talk about resilience, we hear from folks who say everyday Americans are experiencing this economy in a different way.

You look at some of the big picture numbers here, and 30-year mortgages have climbed back up to their highest rate in a year. Gas is now above $4 a gallon at the national average again. Grocery costs are up 33 percent since 2019. What does all that mean for the average American and how they're experiencing this economy?

Well, the average American is certainly still not able to get over the fact that prices, as we're saying, are up 25 to 30 percent from four or five years ago. So you have that sort of like level of unhappiness with the inflation that's been there honestly for quite a few years now, perhaps disappointment that President Trump, who campaigned on a promise to bring prices down, has not succeeded and has in fact presided over a big increase in some key prices, such as for beef and gasoline, some of it his doing, some of it not his doing.

And then there's the fact that you the averages conceal what's going on beneath the surface. As I was saying, the fact that the stock market has done so well means that people who are very affluent have been able to spend a lot, even though overall incomes aren't growing up a lot.

But that's not true of most of the typical family who doesn't have that biggest stock portfolio. So those healthy averages are hiding what I think are probably significant disparities between people at the top and people at the bottom. And the message we're hearing is that the median consumer is not doing nearly as well as some of those average figures, which are being basically buoyed by stock market wealth, are telling us.

You mentioned inflation. That brings me to the Federal Reserve and, as we reported earlier, the decision to hold rates steady that was announced yesterday under their new chair, Kevin Warsh, who also we should remind folks came into this role promising a new regime at the Fed, a new way of communicating as well.

Here's part of what he had to say yesterday. Take a listen.

Market participants are learning to play the ball, not the referee. And market prices will continue to respond in the direction and magnitude they see fit. This is, in my view, a change for the better. I understand the desire for rolling forecasts and commentary from this committee.

Greg, help us understand what Warsh is saying here and what all this might mean for the next Fed meeting and their decision ahead.

What Warsh is basically saying is that he believes that, in the past, Fed officials have commented too much on the data and what it might mean for the change in interest rates in coming months.

He believes that interferes with getting a pure, unfiltered view of what the markets think about the data. So when he says play the ball, not the referee, he's saying have the markets respond just to the data, not try and predict what he, the Fed chairman, is going to do.

But there's a problem with that analogy, which is that he may think that the Fed is a neutral umpire here, but it's not. It's a player in the game. It's the most important player. The Fed sets the short-term interest rate. That is an absolutely central factor in market pricing and in the economy.

Bond investors are always trying to figure out not just what the data means, but what does it mean for what the Fed is going to do to short-term interest rates? That's why they hope and expect the Fed will give them its own view on what some of this data means. If Warsh is going to stay out of that business and never explain to the market what any of this data means, it's going to be confusing.

So, for example, this week, he said once again, the Fed is absolutely dedicated to getting inflation down, which is right now too high. But he didn't raise interest rates. And so the markets and investors and the public want to know, well, why? If you're serious about that, why didn't you raise interest rates? And he didn't answer that question.

And so they're jumping to conclusions. They're saying he talks a big talk, but he's really not that serious about that. And you can see in the markets questions beginning to arise about the credibility of this Fed chair to really get inflation down.

Greg Ip of The Wall Street Journal, great to speak with you, as always. Thank you.
