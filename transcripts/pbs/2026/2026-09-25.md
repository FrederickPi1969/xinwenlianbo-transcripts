## Health care costs a major concern for voters ahead of midterms

The midterm elections are closing in, and once again, health care costs, including loss of insurance coverage through the Affordable Care Act, are at the center of a political debate.

According to national data, enrollment in the marketplaces created by the ACA dropped by roughly three million people this year. That's mainly due to the expiration of enhanced federal tax credits last year. And it's leading to an average premium payment surge of 114 percent for many enrollees.

This week, Vice President J.D. Vance announced plans to cancel coverage for nearly 760,000 more people who get insurance through Obamacare, citing claims of widespread fraud.

J.D. Vance, Vice President of the United States: We expect that most of these people are fraudulently enrolled. But we're going to do some additional verification on about 419,000 people. We're going to make sure that they're, first of all, legal residents of the United States of America.

And, second of all, we're going to make sure that they actually meet the income threshold requirements in order to receive these Obamacare benefits.

For more on what this could mean for those relying on Obamacare for coverage, we're joined now by Larry Levitt, executive vice president for health policy at KFF. That's a nonprofit health policy research organization.

Larry Levitt, Executive Vice President For Health Policy, KFF:

So when Vice President J.D. Vance says this move will save taxpayers $2.2 billion, and it's all aimed at rooting out abuse, what evidence is there that thousands of people were improperly enrolled?

Well, I think the reality is that there is fraud in the Affordable Care Act or Obamacare marketplaces that is mainly perpetrated by rogue insurance brokers who are either signing people up against their will or counseling people to mislead about their income or circumstances.

And I think the administration's efforts to root out this fraud are legitimate. But at the same time, as you said, millions of people have lost coverage due to the expiration of the enhanced premium tax credits at the end of last year. So there is fraud, and the administration is taking efforts to eliminate that.

At the same time, millions of people, real people, are also losing their health insurance.

Is there an appeals process for people who have been dropped from their ACA coverage? How does that work?

I mean, these are people who lacked a Social Security number or proof of their immigration status on their applications or on the federal data systems. And they were given 30 days to -- after being notified by their health insurer to confirm their details.

Now, we all know we all let mail pile up. We don't necessarily follow through on things. So these people got dropped, but they will have an opportunity to get back on if they legitimately are eligible for health insurance coverage.

We know that health care costs are a major concern ahead of the midterm elections. And according to KFF polling this summer, it found that 51 percent of voters say it's extremely important for candidates to address this issue.

What is putting the most pressure on household budgets right now? Is it premiums, deductibles, prescription costs, all of the above?

Well, if you even back up a little bit, I mean, we have asked people, when you say the cost of living or economic concerns are your biggest worry, and no doubt that is what is on voters' minds. Health care and gas prices are the two household expenses that people are most worried about.

So it's not surprising that people want candidates to be talking about health care costs. And it really is all of the above. And it's not just people getting coverage through the Affordable Care Act. It's people in employer plans. That's over 160 million people. They're seeing their premiums go up. They're seeing their deductibles go up.

And that that's putting a crunch on household budgets. I mean, people are going into medical debt. There's over $200 billion in medical debt that Americans are holding, or they're cutting back on other household expenses.

What's making employer-sponsored coverage more expensive? What's driving that?

Well, people blame their insurance companies, and there is certainly plenty of blame that could be put on insurers for the problems people have in the health care system.

But the reality is, insurance companies are not the only problem here, and, in fact, they're not the biggest problem. The vast majority of the reason that health insurance premiums are going up is that health care expenses are going up. Health care prices are going up. And that is mainly hospitals.

I mean, hospitals have driven 40 percent of the increase in health care costs in recent years. I mean, the hospitals are merging, they're getting bigger, they're raising prices, and that's a big reason health care costs are going up.

Yes, that's what elections are for, right...

... to see what the solutions are going to be.

And the parties are far apart. I mean, we started talking about this health care fraud that the administration has been emphasizing. In many ways, Republicans and Democrats are talking past each other. I mean, Republicans are talking about fraud.

That certainly can increase federal government costs, but rooting out this fraud is not going to help people struggling to meet their household expenses, and including health care costs. Democrats are putting forward a wide range of proposals. Progressive Democrats are arguing for Medicare for all, a government system that would cover everyone.

Moderate Democrats are talking about more incremental steps, restoring those premium subsidies that expired at the end of last year, restoring the over $900 billion in Medicaid cuts that Republicans passed last year, capping hospital prices.

And if Democrats win the house or even win the Senate as well, President Trump will still be in the White House. They may be able to get some things done, but they're not going to be able to implement their entire health care agenda.

Larry Levitt, executive vice president for health policy at KFF, thanks again for your time this evening. We appreciate it.

## Mexico's foreign secretary on Trump, cartel violence and U.S. trade talks

Talks slated for Monday to review the U.S.-Mexico-Canada trade agreement, or USMCA, were postponed. That's a deal that President Trump struck in his first term and threw out in his second.

While the trade war with Canada unfolds, Mexican leaders are taking a different approach, even as Trump had harsh words for America's southern neighbor this week.

I spoke earlier today with Mexico's foreign minister, Roberto Velasco Alvarez, who joined us from his New York mission.

Mr. Secretary, welcome to the "PBS News Hour." Thank you so much for joining us.

Roberto Velasco Alvarez, Mexican Foreign Minister:

So you and I are speaking as the United Nations General Assembly is coming to a close. As you saw, that included some searing comments from President Trump about Mexico. He called Mexico the epicenter of cartel violence. What's your response to those remarks from President Trump?

Well, what I can tell you is that in the past two years of the Sheinbaum administration, we have seen a decrease of over 50 percent of the homicide rate in Mexico.

All the different crimes that we have in the country are also in a downward trend. So we're seeing excellent progress. This is historic progress. And it's the most important progress that we're seeing against crime in the whole region of Latin America.

At the same time, we should underscore there is still significant security challenges in Mexico. I know there's warring factions of the Sinaloa cartel in particular. The murder rate for women there is at the highest rate in 14 years.

And, as you know, the Trump administration has designated eight Mexican cartels as foreign terrorist organizations. They're using that framework to justify and threaten potential military action. What do you believe? Do you think that this president might actually make good on that promise for military intervention in Mexico?

Well, what I can tell you is that we have a very important security cooperation with the United States.

But this cooperation is based in the principles of paying respect to our sovereignty and, of course, also to our territorial integrity, and naturally also to what we call cooperation without subordination.

I hear you saying territorial integrity. Do I take that to mean that President Sheinbaum has made it clear to President Trump that U.S. military action would not be welcome in Mexico? Is that right?

Naturally, there are activities in our country that only correspond to Mexican authorities to have -- this is previewed in our Constitution, and we have to respect our own laws and our own legal framework.

I'd like to ask you about the trade talks as well between the two nations, because that ongoing review of the USMCA, or the U.S.-Mexico-Canada agreement, those talks were supposed to begin next week. They were postponed. It's been reported likely in October.

Can you tell us why they were postponed and whether or not they have been rescheduled?

There is nothing to worry about. We continue to work very closely with the United States trade representative. We continue to have conversations both in the USMCA review and about the tariffs that the U.S. has implemented. We have made progress.

We don't have an agreement yet, but we continue to engage constructively on all these issues.

We have also seen Prime Minister Carney consider now becoming an associate member of the E.U. as a trading block. You recently convened a number of Latin American countries with your Southeast Asian counterparts to discuss trade.

Is it fair to assume that Mexico is looking for other trading partners? You feel you may not be able to rely on the U.S.?

Well, Mexico is one of the most open countries in the world for trade. We have free trade agreements covering over 60 countries in the world. It is a normal thing for us to continue to communicate with this and other partners.

But that does not mean that we will not continue to have a close and respectful relation with the United States, and particularly on trade, given that we are each other's number one trading partner.

I do want to ask you finally about the immigration crackdown in the U.S., because your government has said it's going to take legal action in relation to the deaths of 17 Mexican nationals in ICE custody or raids under this administration.

I know you filed complaints with state prosecutors. You have sent cease-and-desist letters to detention centers. Has there been any response on the U.S. side?

Well, this is an issue that, of course, worries us very importantly. The human rights of our citizens in all of the world are one of our primary responsibilities. And, of course, we're always looking for ways to protect them.

This is a very important part of our dialogue with the United States. And in the case of the Mexican citizens that have died under is custody or during ICE operations, we have, as you mentioned, taken a series of measures. We have received an indication that there are ongoing investigations.

Of course, we expect to have more information on that. And we will continue to make the case that it is extremely important to make sure that the conditions that led to these deaths do not continue to exist.

Mr. Secretary, 17 Mexican nationals have died in ICE custody or raids. Do you believe that the U.S. is committing human rights violations?

What I can tell you is that each case is different, and there needs to be investigations on each of these. That's what we're looking for, and obviously to have justice when there was any case that -- where there was any legal violation.

That is the foreign secretary of Mexico, Roberto Velasco Alvarez, joining us tonight.

Mr. Secretary, thank you so much for your time. It's good to speak with you.

By Darlene Superville, Jill Lawless, Associated Press

## What the Supreme Court's voter-citizenship database decision means for the midterms

The Supreme Court revived a controversial voter citizenship database today just two months ahead of the midterm elections.

In the ruling, the court sided with the Trump administration as it looks to use the Department of Homeland Security's so-called SAVE database to review state voter rolls for potential noncitizens.

Joining us now is Jessica Huseman, editor in chief of Votebeat, which has been covering this issue.

Jessica, it's always great to speak with you.

So walk us through exactly what the Supreme Court did today. What can the Trump administration and states do today that they couldn't do yesterday?

Jessica Huseman, Editorial Director, Votebeat:

That's a really complicated question to answer because of the timing of the November election.

So the Supreme Court stayed a lower courts of June ruling that blocked the 2025 modifications of the SAVE database that allowed states to use them to mass-check people for citizenship status on their voter rolls. And so states can begin to do that.

The problem for the Trump administration is that we are in a 90-day period required by the NVRA, which is a federal voting law, that prevents people from being removed from the rolls ahead of a federal election. So while this is really going to be a sea change for election administrators going forward, I don't think voters will see any real effect right now for this election, given that -- given that parameter.

And this database, this SAVE database, it wasn't originally designed to police voter rolls. How has the Trump administration transformed it? And why has this become a central part of its election strategy?

Right, absolutely. I think that people are really confused about what the SAVE database is, what's in it, what it was created for.

And really this is a database to track noncitizens or to sort of have an accounting of noncitizens in the country that DHS knows about, right? And that's a pretty clear distinction between I think what people think that it is, which is a magical database with every noncitizen who happens to be in the country living on it, right?

That is not the list that exists. And even the list that they're using have significant problems. We are seeing pretty bad mismatch problems. We saw that repeatedly in Texas, for example. So it's not a perfect database. And Trump has allowed states to use this to batch search people who are on the voter rolls.

Previously, states could only search one person at a time. Now they can use it much more broadly. And the database is tied into several other databases that DHS maintains. So, theoretically, it's a much more powerful tool than it used to be. The problem is that it's still returning a lot of errors.

Well, on that point, Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, she warned in her dissent that states might cancel the voter registrations of at least some recently naturalized citizens. So how does an eligible voter get wrongly flagged by this system? And has that actually happened before?

It has happened and it's happened frequently. We've reported about it in Texas. So I think that people assume that government databases all talk to each other and they automatically track you through your citizenship process. It does not, right?

This is a database that is largely maintained by human beings who have to enter the information into the database on time and when required of them and as you expect them to. And we know from history that that doesn't often happen.

I think that it's important for people to know that this isn't the last check, right? If your county chooses to use this process, if your state chooses to use this process, your county election administrator is still the one that is ultimately responsible. for removing you from the rolls.

And Votebeat knows from our reporting that these county clerks tend to be much more careful with these matches than the federal government or even many state governments are. And they are doing those second-level checks to make sure that people who are flagged are not unnecessarily removed in most cases.

Jessica Huseman, you make the confusing crystal clear. Thanks for helping us make sense of all this. We appreciate it.
