## How local officials are testing voting machines to ensure election integrity

Midterm election season culminates on one important Election Day, but, in many states, voting is already under way.

For the local officials who run elections, the work started even earlier.

Liz Landers has a closer look at how they prepare for voting and why they say you should trust the process.

Brian McKenzie, Davis County, Utah, Clerk:

We're testing the overvote function of the machine to make sure it accurately identifies those.

Before a single real ballot is counted in Davis County, Utah, workers run stacks of pre-marked test ballots through the machines. Some are marked wrong on purpose. It's called logic and accuracy testing, and it confirms the machines count ballots correctly and catch errors like a voter picking too many candidates.

Utah is one of a handful of states that votes mostly by mail.

County clerk Brian McKenzie explains the path a mail ballot takes.

This is Mickey Mouse and Donald Duck, that their envelopes have come in, right?

Every envelope carries its own bar code. If a voter needs a replacement ballot, the old code is canceled, a check McKenzie says keeps anyone from getting more than one ballot. Return ballots are picked up by at least two election workers, logged and brought here under around-the-clock video surveillance.

Each envelope is scanned, date-stamped, and photographed. Workers compare the signature to as many as five on file, all that before a machine even opens the envelope.

That machine creates that separation from your personal identifiers and anything that you voted, maintaining your constitutional right to a secret ballot.

Ballots move through the process in batches of 200, counted at every step. Vote tallies are loaded onto encrypted memory sticks.

Nothing is wireless. Nothing is Web-based. It's all physical, manual process to transfer that data from one place to another.

McKenzie's first election in 2004 used punch cards. Now he's the county's clerk elected in 2022. Over that time, the job has gotten a lot more public.

Early years, it was a successful election if you never made the front-page news, right? But there is a lot more focus on it. There's a lot more engagement to it.

This year, that focus included a fight in Washington over voting by mail, which President Trump has tried to curtail. At his direction, the United States Postal Service announced new rules to regulate mail ballots before the Supreme Court stepped in last month to block it.

The uncertainty had McKenzie's team drawing up backup plans. He says the big risk is always change that comes too close to an election.

I have found that, with enough time and information that, yes, our voters do adapt and they do adjust. The concern is when it's short. Short notice, significant changes, that creates disruption. That creates question. And when you have questions like that creates doubt. When people have doubt, they disengage.

Joseph Kirk, Election Supervisor, Bartow County, Georgia:

Well, crews come in, pick up everything, deliver it to the polling place.

He says the bigger danger may not be someone changing votes.

There are so many ways to cast doubt on the election through that process, and I think that's the true danger. I don't know that anybody is going to try to, like, say, hack the voting system and change the votes.
