## Inside the AP's process for calling election races

The 2026 campaign has been the most expensive in U.S. history, and it's all leading to tomorrow night, when the votes are counted.

Deema Zein has a look at what happens next.

Millions of votes have been cast in this midterm election. Traditional battlegrounds have expanded in the fight to control Congress. It all comes down to one question: Who won?

This year, the "News Hour" is once again relying on the Associated Press to call winners in critical races.

For a closer look at how the AP makes those race calls, I'm joined by David Scott, V.P. of election services at the Associated Press.

So I want to jump right in. People watching results on election night want to know the winner. So tell us, how do you know when the race is over and which candidate won?

So the thing that we're looking at primarily is the vote count. We are reflecting the choices that Americans have made at the ballot box as they make their choice about who they want to lead the country, who they want to represent them in Congress or in the statehouse.

And so we start there. And, really, the question we're asking ourselves is, are the trailing candidates or a trailing candidate able to catch the leader?

And when we have determined, based on all of the evidence, primarily the vote count, but other things that we take into account, what happened in the advance vote, what's the history of a state, what our poll shows about what took place and what voters were thinking about on election night, but really the vote count, when we see and we are certain that the trailing candidate can't catch up, that's when we declare a winner.

We have got a big team at the Associated Press that makes up our decision team. And they're all journalists. They are people who cover politics. They are data analysts. They are experts in political science.

They are folks who study this year-round full-time. We have folks who focused on turnout and what the expected vote it is. What's the denominator? How many votes are going to be cast in this election? And then, of course, the numerator, what are each candidate getting?

And, again, we're trying to answer that question. When we look at all the votes that have been counted so far, what do we see about what's outstanding? And has a candidate reach the point where none of the others are able to catch them and they will be the winner? And that's when we declare a winner.

Not until that moment. Our certainty is our standard. We're not trying to be first. We're not trying to race to a decision. We are looking at what's coming in. But we also feel an obligation to let an the voters know, to let America know, this is who you have chosen to lead.

And so we're being swift, but always focused on accuracy first. Our standard is certainty. We are certain when we declare a winner that the trailing candidates can't catch up.

Do bad calls actually happen? And, basically, why should Americans trust AP to get it right?

We have been doing this literally since the founding of the Associated Press; 1848 was the first time that we counted the vote and declared winners two years after the founding of a cooperative. It's part of who we are as a news organization.

We think that the choices that Americans have made is a news story. It's really important. In this year's primaries, we declared winners in more than 11,000 races. And we made three mistakes. And one was because of bad information from election officials, for example. And another one was because of a technical problem. So they do happen, but they're very rare.

David Scott, vice president of election services at Associated Press, thank you so much.

In Washington, D.C., a federal judge dismissed the charges today against two former FBI officials, saying the case against them can only be described as weaponization. Nikki Flores and Bradley Benavides had been accused of lying to Congress in 2023 about their knowledge of efforts to investigate Hunter Biden's laptop.

Last week, the same judge criticized prosecutors for trying to add additional charges without going through the proper procedures. Those two were dropped. The Justice Department could still pursue a grand jury indictment against the two former officials.

In Massachusetts, a federal judge dismissed the Trump administration's lawsuit against Harvard University today over whether it uses race as a factor in its admissions. The DOJ had sued the Ivy League school earlier this year for access to years of admissions data. The administration alleged that Harvard was not complying with the 2023 Supreme Court decision that prohibits race-based admissions programs.

But the judge sided with Harvard, whose lawyers said the case was intended to punish the school for refusing other administration demands.

Turning overseas now, supermarkets across Europe reported a new wave of cyber attacks targeting their refrigeration systems today. At least one store said hackers remotely raise temperatures in refrigerators and freezers to as high as 122 degrees Fahrenheit, forcing employees to throw out spoiled food.

Incidents were reported in several countries, including Belgium, Ireland, the Netherlands, and Sweden.

Some organization hacked into the temperature monitoring system. It's insane. I don't see what they stand to gain from it. It's a form of terrorism really.

The FDA has approved a generic version of the world's only twice-yearly injection to prevent HIV. The medication is called lenacapavir, and it's expected to cost around $40 per person each year. That makes it comparable in price to daily medications, but experts point out the convenience of only needing two doses a year. It's a major step toward making the drug more available in around 120 low- and middle-income countries.

The Kennedy Center has announced its latest group of honorees as the ceremony prepares for the first time ever to be held off-site. The Kennedy Center itself has been closed for months amid a legal battle over President Trump's plans to renovate the building and add his name to it, forcing the December 7 ceremony to move to Washington's Capital One Arena.

Karl Bushby embraced his mother as he returned home to Hull -- that's in Northern England -- after his extraordinary journey. The former paratrooper set out from the southern tip of Chile in November 1998 at just 29 years old, inspired by an idea from his father, who was an Army veteran.
