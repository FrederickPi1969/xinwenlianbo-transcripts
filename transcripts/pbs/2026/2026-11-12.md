## A Brief But Spectacular take on finding purpose after military service

After 24 years in the army, Quinton McNair returned home to Chicago and found himself struggling to adjust to civilian life. But an unexpected hobby helped him discover a new sense of mission. He shares his brief but spectacular take on finding purpose after the military.

I joined the army for all the wrong reasons. I wasn't joining to be patriotic. I wasn't joining to serve my country. I was joining to run away from my problems.

We all know Chicago gets a bad name, and I lived in some of the areas where that bad name comes from. There was a lot of gang activity going on there. I was one of the few people who were not in the gang. Walking to school every day without protection from either side. I used to get jumped.

That all affected my grades and growing up in the area. And it was a real hard time for me. Basically, I joined the army to get away from the city and probably pretty much to save my life. 24 years in the army, I did just about everything and went everywhere. Between Kosovo and Bosnia and then Iraq and Afghanistan, I think my military career, I deployed between nine and 11 times. I get out of the military in 2019. I retire. I come home to the city that I ran from.

Everywhere I looked, something had changed. I didn't have the close friends support of the people that I built in the military. I felt like a stranger in my home city. Diagnosed with PTSD and depression. Now that I have all this free time, I'm sitting at home. I'm looking for a job. And now all of the intrusive thoughts that I had pushed down while I were in the military were coming up. I found a job as a HR manager. I wanted to do something where I dressed up or I became, you know, something different than I had been for the past 24 years. Not knowing that, you know, that transition was going to be hard for me. Baking became like the therapy that I needed to get through that very hard time in my life.

So, when I first started posting pictures of what I was creating online, I would hashtag at struggle beard bakery because of the struggle with my mental health to making fun of myself with my beard and the products really looked like they were on a struggle bus. But then I got very very good at it. It went from being this therapy to this hobby to something that I wanted to share with other people. So, I decided to turn Struggle Berry Bakery into an actual business. To all my battle buddies who are still in the military, you're kind of anxious about what's going to happen on the other end. Couple of things that is going to help you out immensely. Get that help.

Don't let your PTSD, don't let your trauma just sit. Don't try to mask it. Actively go through the stages of getting some help. Also, you are highly skilled. The time management that you learn in the military highly, highly useful and highly valuable in the civilian world. A soldier will try to figure something out with what little resources that they have. That is invaluable in a workplace. Your people management skills. You've learned how to manage people in some of the hardest situations and some of the craziest places.

An office building should be nothing to you after you manage a squad in Iraq or in Afghanistan. You already have the training. You already have the knowhow. You just have to show it to the world. My name is Quincy McNair and this is my brief but spectacular take on how to find your purpose after the military.

## Scott Colom's historic victory marks 'new moment' for Black voters, Bryan Stevenson says

Scott Colom's historic victory in Mississippi marks a political milestone in a state central to the struggle for civil rights and voting rights in America. To help us understand the significance of this moment, we're joined by Bryan Stevenson, founder and executive director of the Equal Justice Initiative. Bryan, welcome back to the NewsHour.

Scott Colom's election is historic regardless of where one stands on his politics. We should remind folks that Mississippi is where Medgar Evers fought and died, where Fannie Lou Hamer challenged the political establishment, where James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, Michael Schwerner were murdered while trying to register Black voters. How do you connect Scott Colom's victory to the sacrifices of the people who helped make this moment possible?

Well, there's no state in America that has more violently resisted full political participation by Black people than the state of Mississippi. The people you identified along with so many others, Vernon Dahmer, Herbert Lee, Lamar Smith, all of these people had to lose their lives in the effort to create greater political participation. And so I think this is really an extraordinary moment in the history of this country.

People don't realize that Mississippi was a majority Black state for much of its history. From 1860 until 1940, there were more Black people in Mississippi than white people. The only reason why we haven't had dozens of Black senators from Mississippi is because of violent voter suppression and abusive resistance to the rule of law.

In 1940, became a majority white state in part because our government tolerated decades of terror, violence, and lynching that drove hundreds of thousands of Black people from the state. So now for Scott Colom to win when it is no longer a majority Black state, to win by not being about this history, but inviting others to challenge the kind of bigotry that is made Mississippi such a dangerous place for Black politicians. I think it's really remarkable and I think it inspires me and others in this region to believe that the spirit of the civil rights movement, which really changed America that decade between 55 and 65 is a decade that resulted in our country for the first time becoming a more functional democracy. And I think that spirit has been awakened in Mississippi and many parts across the South and it's really energizing to see.

And fast forward to the present moment after the Supreme Court gutted key protections in the Voting Rights Act, Mississippi saw new battles over voting restrictions and redistricting, gerrymandering. At the same time though, you had organizers who were working to mobilize voters and to expand political participation. What does that suggest about the power of grassroots organizing to overcome barriers, intentional barriers to the ballot?

I think what happened this summer was a wake-up call to many African Americans and to many people who just believe in democracy that the court would gut the single piece of legislation, which was the crowning achievement of the Voting Rights Act, that has actually facilitated meaningful political participation for the first time in our nation's history; that they would gut that act and then that states would immediately exploit that, states like Tennessee and Alabama, to undermine Black political participation. I think it energized Black people all over the South. And I think Scott's win in Mississippi, and the win in Texas, win in Georgia, and the win in Alabama, and the win in South Carolina is evidence that this is going to be a new moment in the politics of America. Well, I think the fact that, you know, Scott Colom won this race, uh not just with the Black vote, there aren't enough Black voters for him to have won a statewide race. He persuaded a much higher percentage of white voters to vote for him in a moment when they had been told that they should avoid things that are echoes of our history of civil rights activism and racial injustice. The same thing happened in Georgia, the same thing happened in South Carolina, the same thing happened in Texas. And I think that's what is inspiring. I think every American should be proud of what the civil rights movement did for this country, that it allowed us to become this more perfect unit. That should make all of us proud. And when we have people who are trying to undermine that progress, who are trying to return us to the politics of division and racial exclusion that's defined so many parts of this country for too long, I think you're going to see lots of people saying, "No, we don't want that.
