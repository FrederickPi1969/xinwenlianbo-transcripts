## HBCUs enter new fight for equal funding

A new lawsuit in Missouri could have sweeping implications for the nation's historically Black colleges and universities and the billions of dollars they say they have been denied over decades.

Current and former students at Lincoln University are suing the state for nearly $549 million, alleging that Missouri systematically underfunded the school compared with the University of Missouri. The case could become a model for similar legal challenges across the country.

Joining us now is Don Calloway, a former Missouri state lawmaker and attorney who founded The 1890 Project to challenge longstanding funding disparities at historically Black land grant universities. He's also leading this lawsuit against Missouri.

This conversation, we should say, is part of our series on higher education called Rethinking College.

So we know for decades HBCUs have been receiving less funding than their predominantly white counterparts. But you're arguing that what happened at Lincoln University in Missouri, you're saying that this wasn't simply unfair, that it was illegal.

What exactly did the state do wrong and what makes this a violation of federal law?

So, from 1987 to 2020 -- really to the present, but through 2020 on paper, we see for the first time, because the federal government started tracking -- based upon the states' -- individual states' own reporting, the federal government started tracking and then publishing exactly what each state allocated to its land grant institutions.

So it's an important context here. Every state in the country has a flagship university, your University of Missouris, your Auburn Universities. That is an 1862 land grant institution, which the government gave thousands of acres to place a school and give -- then gives millions of dollars a year to each school to fund.

After slavery ended, in 1890, there came a series of those land grant institutions for the freed slaves to educate them concurrently. And what we have found is that, from the inception, every state has not met its obligation to fund the 1890 equitably with the 1862.

As a result, we didn't see that data published until 2023. It goes back to starting in 1987, which was the first year that the feds began to aggregate that data. And so our lawsuit is based upon the notion that the state of Missouri underfunded Lincoln University as compared to the University of Missouri, to the tune of about $361 million, since we have begun tracking the data.

So we're talking about hundreds of millions of dollars. Those numbers can feel abstract, but what has the loss of funding actually meant for generations of students at Lincoln University?

Well, a couple of things. I'm glad you asked that. First of all, it's not just Lincoln, but there is an aggregate across the 1890s of about $12.6 billion of the 16 states who did not properly fund their 1890, as compared to their 1862.

But there are real, tangible damages here. You take career earnings of a FAMU graduate compared to a Florida State graduate, a Fort Valley graduate in Georgia, as compared to the University of Georgia graduate. When you attend an institution that has been hampered by demonstrable underfunding, the facilities are in disrepair.

That has a definite effect on the quality of life on campus. The programmings, access to advisory facilities, access to all of the things that one believes goes into the standard of life that one expects from a reasonably competitive college are diminished.

And we see the results of that as you enter the professional sector, whether it's private or public, through end of life.

So you know that states can say, look, we make funding decisions based on enrollment, institutional needs, performance, budgetary issues.

Where is the legal line between a legitimate funding decision and unconstitutional discrimination? And how can you prove that Missouri crossed that line?

Well, one of the ways that Missouri underfunded Lincoln is, it created a five-metric rubric. And so it has -- gets a certain amount of money from the federal government. The state of Missouri is supposed to match that. Well, what it did in Lincoln's case is created a five-metric rubric that apportioned 20 percent of its state match, which was mandatory, upon Lincoln hitting these certain metrics.

They didn't apply that metric to the University of Missouri. So that is facially unconstitutional, inasmuch as, number one, the state had no precedent to create this thing. But, secondly, they didn't apply it equally to the University of Missouri to determine its match.

So that, in and of itself, whether or not we ever achieve the remedial fund of the corpus of $361 million, that funding metric can be destroyed immediately. And that provides us the prospective relief. But that's just one of the ways that we make a very clear legal argument that this was fundamentally and facially unconstitutional.

The $549 million number, how did you arrive at that?

That's a figure with nominal interest. The 548 is salacious. It makes it a bigger headline, but that's with nominal interest calculated.

I hope that we don't have to get that because we'd be glad to settle for the objectively indisputable 361 that was withheld from Lincoln.

As you were putting this case together, did you happen upon the reason why, why whoever it was put these hurdles in place for Lincoln to get the money that they were owed...

... and those same hurdles were not in place for the University of Missouri?

Well, I don't think it's any different in Missouri than it is in Arkansas, Pine Bluff, as related to U.A., or Florida, again, FAMU as related to Florida State. It's just a vestige of a segregated higher education system.

And so I'm not looking at any individual legislator or governor or attorney general across the country. You can't look at anybody and say, hey, you're wrong, you're racist. Most of us, and I'm talking to me because I was a legislator at the time, most of us just inherited the system we were given.

But when the data was published and made plain on September 18, 2023, you have an obligation to do better once you know better, right? And so that is the very objective case we're bringing, and we're asking legislators of good faith to make a very clear decision to right a historical wrong, but, more importantly, fix the funding metrics going forward so that these institutions are strengthened for the future.

They're not -- Lincoln is not a majority-Black institution anymore.

It is -- the current student body is more white. You could say the same thing for West Virginia State, also a historically Black institution. And so what you're doing is harming an entire region of people who have played by the rules, gone to college, stayed out of trouble, and they're going into a work force with diminished economic prospects.

And I think every legislator, regardless of party or political affiliation, has an interest in fixing that.

If you prevail in Missouri, this could potentially open the door to billions of dollars of claims nationwide.

Yes, we will absolutely prevail in Missouri, first of all.

But, once we do so, it does open the door and it already has, because your historically Black land grant colleges have the same case across the board. Every state's underfunding might have been done in a little bit different ways and different nuanced ways, but the objective violations of the 14th Amendment Equal Protection Clause are there across every state.

So, we have already heard from legislators in Oklahoma, Virginia, Texas, all of whom are interested in helping us bring their case. So we're very excited about the potential national ramifications, which could fix this historical wrong going forward.

Attorney Don Calloway, good to speak with you.

## Tightening sanctions on Russia is 'the way to end this war,' says David Kramer

For perspective, we turn now to David Kramer, who held a number of senior positions at the State Department during the George W. Bush administration. He's now executive director of the George W. Bush Institute in Dallas, Texas.

David Kramer, Former U.S. Assistant Secretary of State: Thank you.

Let's start with this apparent reset, because, just days ago, as you well know, President Trump, President Zelensky appeared to be at a breaking point. Today, Zelenskyy says the critical U.S. intelligence sharing has been restored.

You have European leaders talking about getting diplomacy back on track. Is this a genuine reset or simply a temporary reprieve?

It's hard to say. I certainly hope it is more than temporary, because Ukraine has been suffering as a result of some of the disruptions in provision of intelligence, the Starlink and Starshield that Nick referred to, and Ukrainians have been losing their lives as a result.

The war, let's not forget, was started by Putin initially in 2014, on a full-scale level in 2022. He is the one who has refused to negotiate and bring about an end to the war that he started. Ukraine and Zelenskyy have demonstrated repeatedly a willingness and flexibility to end the war and to end the suffering that the Ukrainian people have been enduring.

And we have seen this cycle before, a confrontation between Trump and Zelenskyy, followed by efforts to repair that relationship.

What does that recurring pattern suggest about the fundamental challenge in the U.S.-Ukraine relationship right now?

It's a strained relationship. We go back in this current administration to the February 28, 2025, infamous White House blowup, where the president and President Zelenskyy were essentially shouting at each other.

And then the relationship got better, on a better track, and the United States imposed some sanctions on Lukoil and Rosneft, the two largest Russian energy companies. But, more recently, we have seen rhetoric coming out of Washington that suggests that the war is Ukraine's fault.

Ukraine has been the victim of Russian aggression. That Zelenskyy and Ukraine refused to end the war. Putin is the one who refuses to end the war. And so there's been this seesaw that I think has been both exhausting and unnerving for the Ukrainians, but also deadly, because Russia is taking advantage of this and continues its strikes against Ukrainian civilian targets.

What do you believe Putin understands about President Trump that allows Putin to really shape President Trump's approach to this war?

I think, on the Russian side, they have been dangling the prospect of improved U.S.-Russian relations, whether it's for business deals, nuclear negotiations. Of course, President Putin has offered the possibility of a summit meeting in Beijing next month, when President Trump will travel there for a major international conference.

And the idea of Putin, Xi, the Chinese leader, and President Trump meeting to discuss Ukraine without Ukraine is something the Ukrainians would have great difficulty accepting.

What is in Russia's interest? What is Russia's incentive to end this war? Putin has suffered enormous battlefield losses. Russia's economy has come under considerable strain, and yet he continues fighting. Why?

Putin doesn't view Ukraine as a truly independent state? He said this as early as 2008 to President Bush when they met in Sochi.

He wants to put Ukraine under Russia's thumb. He wants to destroy Ukraine's identity. He wants to remove the duly elected government in Ukraine, President Zelenskyy, whose term has gone beyond its mandate, but that's because Ukraine had to declare a martial law because of Russia's invasion.

And Putin wants to deny Ukraine the ability to choose its own future, to determine whether it wants to be a member of NATO or the European Union, and it wants to reduce its military. Putin essentially wants to eviscerate Ukrainian identity, sovereignty, territorial integrity and independence. And that's obviously something that Ukrainians find wholly unacceptable.

Would additional American pressure change Putin's calculations at this point?

I think it is the way to end this war. I think it involves tightening sanctions, going after the shadow fleet that Russia depends on to bring in funding for its war effort. It includes going after Russian banks and more companies and individuals.

It also includes resuming military assistance, whether it's through the system that the Europeans purchase American weapons and then provide those to the Ukrainians or direct military assistance. We have to remember that the U.S. Congress passed the Lindsey Graham legislation calling for more sanctions by 86-11 in the Senate and a sizable margin in the House.

Americans, surveys show, support Ukraine. And so I hope that we will understand that pressure on Putin, not on Ukraine or Zelenskyy, pressure on Putin is the way to try to bring about an end to the war that the president has been talking about since he returned to office.

And you, of course, served in a Republican administration that viewed American leadership, support for Democratic allies, resistance to Russian aggression as fundamental national security interests.

How do you explain, how do you understand the transformation of Republican foreign policy under President Trump?

Well, there has been a major change. And America first places the priority on different issues.

But I think there should be consistency throughout in recognizing that Ukraine is the country we should be supporting. Zelenskyy is the one who offered us assistance when Iranian Shahed drones were hitting American targets in the Middle East after the war started there. The Ukrainians have been the ones who are fighting for their freedom, but they're also fighting for our freedom, given what Russia has been doing to the rest of Europe, even in the United States.

And so supporting Ukraine, I think, serves U.S. national interests and is something that every Republican and every Democrat, for that matter, should get behind.

David Kramer, executive director of the George W. Bush Institute, speaking to us from Dallas, Texas, this evening, thank you for your insights. Great to speak with you.
