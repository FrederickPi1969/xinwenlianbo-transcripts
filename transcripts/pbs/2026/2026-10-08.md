## How GLP-1s are reshaping cultural views on body image

Roughly 12 percent of Americans are now taking the blockbuster set of weight loss medications known as GLP-1s. And while their popularity continues to skyrocket, new concerns have emerged around the societal impacts, especially around body image.

Correspondent Stephanie Sy and producers Mike Fritz and Sam Lane have that story for the last installment of our series GLP-1s: Reshaping America.

When Gabby (ph) Coro became a mom in 2021, the joy of having her son, Charlie, was soon overshadowed by what came next.

It was very hard to shed the pounds. I had gone through pretty severe postpartum depression that lasted roughly three years, and nothing was really working. So some of the side effects include weight gain or, like, really hard to lose weight.

The 35-year-old college professor lives in Miami with her husband, Raul. She says, despite repeated efforts, she struggled to lose about 30 pounds from her pregnancy.

I just didn't like how I would look in pictures, I didn't want any pictures taken of me during this time. My husband can attest he had a family member ask him if we were separated or divorced. He's like, I don't see Gabby in any of our -- the pictures with you and your son.

And that's how I was just like, don't take a picture of me. I don't want to be seen.

But, late last year, she was prescribed a GLP-1.

And I took my first injection after Christmas and my birthday, so I could have birthday cake and enjoy myself.

But for Gabby Coro, who after just three months on a GLP-1 lost nearly all her post-pregnancy weight, the decision to take them felt fraught.

We really saw people embracing body diversity, body acceptance, and enjoying seeing themselves in the images around them. And then weight loss medications came on the scene, and there was less diversity.

There's also research that larger-body people who use GLP-1s to lose weight are facing new stigmas. A study published earlier this year found they're viewed negatively for taking a shortcut.

## Botched execution reignites debate surrounding death penalty

Christa Pike, the Tennessee woman who survived a botched execution, has reportedly taken a few steps in her hospital room six days after she received two lethal injections. Pike was convicted of the 1995 murder of Colleen Slemmer, and it's now unclear how that case will proceed.

In your research, you have found that lethal injection fails more often than any other method in American history, including hanging and the electric chair. Why is that?

Well, no method of execution is foolproof. Every method of execution that we have used in the United States, whether it's hanging, the electric chair, the gas chamber, now lethal injection, has run into problems.

Lethal injection runs into more problems because, paradoxically, it's a very complicated way to try to put someone to death. You have to find a usable vein. You have to secure an I.V. You have to make sure that the drugs that you are using are up to date and potent.

So it's not surprising that a complicated procedure like lethal injection would run into problems of the kind that it has.
