## 'A down payment on change': Rahm Emanuel on what Democrats should do after midterms

Senator Tillis mentioned what he sees as the striking parallels between this year's midterm elections and the Democratic wave of 2006.

Rahm Emanuel helped engineer that victory 20 years ago. Now, as his party looks to regain power, what lessons does he see for Democrats today?

We're joined now from Chicago by Democrat Rahm Emanuel, the former Chicago mayor, congressman, White House chief of staff, and U.S. ambassador to Japan.

Rahm Emanuel (D), Former Mayor of Chicago, Illinois: Thanks, Geoff. Thanks very much for having me.

So, before we turn to domestic politics, I want to start with Israel's election, because when we spoke on this program this past summer, you said the U.S.-Israel relationship was at a tipping point, and you called for a fundamental change in direction.

Now, as a result of this election, neither major bloc has won a governing majority. And the question for you is, in your view, can anything fundamentally change as long as Netanyahu remains in power, even in a weakened coalition?

The short answer is no, both because of both the person and the policies.

He would have to have a fundamental difference in calculation. This has all been said by the top echelons of the military, people that used to work for him. Everything runs through the calculation of what's in his interest, not as what is in the nation's interest.

He has refused to have it because the judgment would be unambiguous about the role he played in one of the worst military and strategic catastrophes in Israel's history since its founding in '48.

Well, two things. I mean, it was very -- the similarities are here, unpopular president, unpopular president, corruption, corruption, unpopular war, unpopular war, spiking energy prices, spiking energy prices.

## Trump should 'take stock' and 'own' election outcome, Sen. Tillis says

Republican Senator Thom Tillis of North Carolina is sounding the alarm about the future of his own party.

In his new book, "How to Lose Friends and Antagonize Presidents, Tillis warns that Republican lawmakers are afraid to challenge President Trump, that Congress is surrendering its power, and some of the president's closest advisers are leading him toward failure.

And Senator Tillis joins us now from North Carolina.

And you write in this book that, unless more Republicans are willing to voice their concerns about the White House, that the country could be headed into what you call perilous waters. What concerns you the most about the direction of this administration right now?

The lack of a feedback loop with Congress, really dating back to when I wrote a letter to my colleagues in the Senate Republican Conference back in April of 2025.

I tried to warn that, if we were not active participants in formulation, execution, and timing of policy, that I was afraid we were going to make the same mistakes that Obama did. And we're seeing the same thing. We did not have that feedback loop. We got a little bit of ahead of our blockers.

The voters can only absorb so much change over a period of time, and I think we exceeded their capacity, and that's a part of what's driving people to the polls.

In your book, you estimate that at least 30 Republican senators privately share your concerns about President Trump. And that's an enormous amount of political power, especially when the president's approval ratings are in the low 30s.

If those senators stood together, wouldn't they have more leverage over the president than he has over any one of them? Why aren't they using it?

That was what I was trying to get at last year, last April. And I also said in my communication to my colleagues that we will know everything that we need to know about the environment by early first -- fourth quarter last year.

And we did. We could see that things were not moving quite in the direction that we should have. And that was really our last chance, in my opinion, to recalibrate before we were going to have -- the only thing we'd have there is lessen the maybe negative impact of the election cycle.

The point that I was trying to make is, if we didn't have that separation, then it was also going to expose the president. And I communicated that to the president. I told him, when we were moving too quickly on the so-called Big Beautiful Bill, that, if we weren't careful, he was going to have two of the most miserable years of his life.

It was the timing and how they went about it that became a problem for the voters.

In your view, what's the clearest example of something that the Republican-controlled Congress allowed President Trump to do that they should have stopped?

I think the Big Beautiful Bill is probably the best example, because that's an example of where, if we had just passed the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act that basically make permanent what we passed in 2017, we would have had a huge win that we would be running on right now.

But we -- but we passed health care bills. You're already seeing the narrative that the health care portion of the bill was the problematic portion that I believe was going to cause some headwinds in this cycle, even more in the 2028 cycle. That was probably the best example.

But we needed an after-action, and we should have pressed on either the war powers resolution or very clear communication to Congress sooner. And the president should have communicated to the American people why it was important for him to do what he did. That's another one where we have lost the narrative. And all people think about is the fact that it's cost them $20, $30 more to fill up their gas tank.

Regarding Secretary Hegseth, whom you mentioned, you have called your vote for him one of the biggest regrets of your Senate career. You had serious reservations about his qualifications and judgment before the vote. What caused you to set those concerns aside?

A unanimous vote from every person on the Senate Armed Services Committee, up to and including one member who had expressed some concerns.

I served on the Senate Armed Services Committee for eight years. And I decided with the -- with any of the nominations that came out of committees that I wasn't on, on the committee of jurisdiction, that I would put a lot of weight on a unanimous vote, even if it was a party-line vote.
