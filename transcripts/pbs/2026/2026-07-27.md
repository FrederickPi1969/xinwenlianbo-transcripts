## Documentary explores why U.S. mothers lack support systems found in other countries

A new documentary explores the realities of motherhood in America, from the high cost of child care to the latest culture wars over parenting and raising a family.

Amna Nawaz recently spoke with the film's creators for our arts and culture series Canvas.

The debate over a modern motherhood is often framed as a choice between career and family. A new documentary film called "No Country For Mothers" dives deep into the so-called mommy wars, unpacking the systems that exploit and perpetuate those divides and what it would take to end them.

You have working moms. You have stay-at-home moms, and then you have work-from-home, stay-at-home moms.

Everything that structurally is set up is set up to make motherhood so damned hard.

They join me now. Welcome to you both. Thank you so much for joining us.

Reshma, I'll start with you because you meet with a lot of different moms in the film. You share your own story as a working mom too. What did you find as you dug into this? Were the struggles of these moms universal?

Reshma Saujani, Executive Producer, "No Country For Mothers": Yes, I mean, look, this film is an investigation into the lies we tell about American motherhood to distract and divide us from getting the things we need to make motherhood tenable in America, which is paid leave and child care.

And what I found as I went across the country is we all want the same thing. ## Cyclospora and salmonella outbreaks raise concerns about U.S. food safety

Public health concerns are growing, as cyclosporiasis cases surge, and now a salmonella outbreak across 17 states has many Americans raising concerns about U.S. food safety.

Geoff, these back-to-back cases where people are getting sick from food have put many people on edge and raised questions about the nation's food supply.

With nearly 4,200 confirmed cyclospora cases nationwide across nine states, and more than 7,400 additional unconfirmed cases, plus a salmonella outbreak linked to eggs that has sickened almost 100 people so far, consumers are wondering if enough is being done to keep unsafe food out of our stores.

For more on these concerns, we are joined again by Caitlin Rivers. She's an epidemiologist at Johns Hopkins University.

Caitlin, so nice to have you back on the program.

Let's start off with this troubling pattern. We have got this very, very big cyclospora outbreak happening, really concentrated in Michigan, but we're also seeing the salmonella outbreak. Is this just a particularly bad year or is something else going on here?

Dr. Caitlin Rivers, Epidemiologist, Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security: I think both are true here. There is no question that this is a very large outbreak of cyclosporiasis. To my knowledge, it's the largest on record. And so that is absolutely consequential.

The addition of the salmonella outbreak in eggs is unsettling, but actually not uncommon. There are one to two large outbreaks of something, some sort of foodborne illness each month. And so currently it's salmonella and shelled eggs, but the existence of a second sizable outbreak is not in itself notable.

Is there a connection between these two? I mean, I know cyclospora, we have seen that linked to contaminated water being sprayed onto lettuce and in some cases berries, but we're still searching for the root cause of that, or the root causes, salmonella, as you mentioned, linked to eggs.

Is there any linkage between these two things, or are they just separate outbreaks?

They're just separate outbreaks. They're caused by different pathogens, different food items. Different states are affected.

What they have in common is that our food system is very interconnected. There are very large food producers that distribute to many, many states, reach often millions of consumers. And so when we see outbreaks like this, it's because we largely share a food supply with our neighbors.

There have also been a series of cuts made by the Trump administration to some specific surveillance monitoring programs, as well as much, much broader cuts that have trickled down to the public health world writ large.

Is it fair to say that those cuts are somewhat complicit in this or not?

It's hard to draw a straight line because we don't necessarily see the inner workings of the people who used to work there, but are now RIFed, or, that is to say, laid off, or the direct cut line to budget cuts.

But I can say with confidence that, when you lose a quarter or a third of your public health work force, when you are under strain for coming up on six-plus years coming out of the pandemic, the consequences of that do matter. They do manifest.

And we like to say in public health that, when we do our jobs right, nothing happens, and we're seeing now the opposite of that, where things are happening. And I think we need to recognize that there are -- there are things we can do, which is to say, investments and support, that can help to reverse that trend.

Cyclospora in particular is hammering Michigan. Do you have any sense as to why it is so bad in that state?

I think there are two probable reasons. One is that they received the contaminated product, right? They had the shredded lettuce, the iceberg lettuce from Taylor Farms.

But I also think Michigan has done a very good job alerting the public to the existence of this outbreak and educating providers on the need to identify and test cases. And so although you may see higher numbers and think, oh, dear, that's worse, in some ways, it's better because it means that public health officials have more information to be able to identify the source and take the necessary actions to keep people safe.

Alongside those two outbreaks, we have also seen another virus reemerge, and that is measles. And we have got this record number of cases this year. I believe it's 2,300, which is, I think, the worst in 35 years, I believe it is.

This was a virus that we had once basically eradicated in the U.S. Why are we seeing a return of that virus?

It's true. This is already a record year for measles. We have surpassed last year's total already in July, and this is the biggest year since the virus was declared eliminated in 2001.

We have a safe and very effective vaccine against measles. And so the reason that we're seeing these increased cases is because that vaccine coverage is falling. The good news here is that the trend is reversible. If we are able to increase vaccine coverage, then we will be able to return to a state where we can say measles no longer circulates.

But, unfortunately, today that's not the case.

And some of what you're talking about is, I think, driven by some messaging that we have been hearing coming out of HHS and the health secretary, Mr. Kennedy.

Do you think, though, that state and local public health departments are able to step in when you're talking about salmonella and cyclosporiasis system and measles and the messaging involved in that? Are they up to that task?

State and local public health officials are actually the front lines. Many people don't know that public health in the United States is federated, which means it's the state and local officials who have the power and the ability to act, whereas federal officials, at least on the CDC side, it's different for FDA, but they act in a more advisory, technical capacity.

And so I have absolute confidence in our ability, our state and local officials' ability to combat these threats, but they do need resourcing and support. They can't do it in the absence of money and people. And so that really comes back to the need to continue to invest in public health and give our systems what they need to thrive.

Caitlin Rivers of Johns Hopkins University, always great to hear from you. Thank you so much.

## India's youth-led 'cockroach' protests expose cracks in Modi's government

They call themselves the Cockroaches after a top official compared India's jobless youth to the pests.

Now, days after their protests toppled the education minister, students say police are coming for them. What started as outrage over leaked medical entrance exams has turned into one of India's largest youth-led protest movements, challenging India's prime minister.

"News Hour" producer Zeba Warsi reports now on how India's Gen Z took on one of the world's most powerful leaders and what it means for the country's future.

The world's largest youth population just made their powerful government buckle under pressure. Beneath the celebration was something remarkable, India's youth discovering the power of their own voice.

The government has messed with the wrong Gen Z people.

It's a victory for the Constitution of India. It's a victory for the people of India. And it's a defeat for the dictator.

That's Abhijeet Dipke, a graduate student at Boston University who sparked a movement from an insult after India's chief justice compared unemployed young people to cockroaches. Dipke launched the satirical Cockroach Janta Party, or CJP, a play on Prime Minister Narendra Modi's ruling BJP.

Today, its Instagram page has grown to over 25 million followers, more than twice that of the BJP. It all began with a leak of a national medical school entrance exam taken by two million aspiring medical students across the country.

It's an exam they can prepare to take for years with private tutors and offers a way out of poverty for those who do well. The decision to invalidate the test results was unbearable for many students, like Pradeep Kumar (ph). His mother, Sushila Devi, says her son was so distressed that he took his own life.

Sushila Devi, Mother of Student Who Died By Suicide (through interpreter): He felt that if the exam was held again, then he may or may not be able to do so well. There was already so much death burden on his father, he just decided to die.

More than 20 other young students made the same tragic decision. Their unfulfilled dreams have become a generation's grief. That turned to anger. Tens of thousands of students took to the streets, calling themselves Cockroaches, demanding the dismissal of the education minister.

Last week, they marched toward the Parliament. The government responded with a violent crackdown, beating unarmed students with batons, using tear gas and even pellet guns. Students, journalists, and witnesses gathered video to document and verify the attacks on a digital platform that became known as The Protest Record.

International human rights organizations have called out the Indian government for using excessive force. And if the government's goal was to empty the streets, it had the opposite effect. The protest started in New Delhi and quickly spread across the country. Indian politicians who opposed Prime Minister Modi, including opposition leader Rahul Gandhi, backed the students, demanding accountability for what they called a brutal clampdown.

For weeks, the revolution was not televised, as most mainstream media in India support the government. But news spread on social media, with tens of thousands of Instagram Reels watched by millions. Students can be seen mocking the police force that beat them.

And the posts turned protesters into Instagram celebrities. Analysts say India has witnessed widespread protests before, but this moment feels different.

India's Gen Z are blaming him for the police violence and criticizing his divisive politics.

It took seven weeks of protests, but Modi finally relented and promised to get to the bottom of the medical exam leaks. This weekend, the education minister resigned.

Umang Gupta, Former Modi Supporter (through interpreter):

He fears losing power. They know, if this protest goes on for a long time, the government can fall.

Raised in a family of devoted Modi supporters, 22-year-old Umang Gupta once led her university's chapter of Modi's BJP Party. But its crackdown on the Cockroach protesters changed everything for her.

I have joined the protest against my parents' wishes. So many kids have stood up to their parents and they are getting beaten up. They are being called terrorists.

She said India's youth is now determined to take action against what they see as democratic backsliding.

When there is a fire within, we can stay hungry too. When there is courage within, we are unstoppable. That is the power of every youth. The youth is the future.

The Cockroach Movement may be over for now, but, today, its leaders said dozens of students are still being targeted by police across the country, particularly from minority groups. Students say protests will resume unless those cases are dropped.

Neha Bora, National President, All India Student Association:

Nobody believes that this victory is because of the benevolence of the government. People are very, very, very frustrated even now.

Student leader Neha Bora spent 23 days on a hunger strike and emerged as one of the movement's leading voices.

This anger, this deep, bone-deep frustration with the way teens were, all of that being handled with brute force, they're just tired of the same old script.

Bora said Prime Minister Modi may have figured a way to slip out of this crisis, but the students will continue to hold him to account.

It's a good thing that he accepted one demand of ours, because the next demand is his resignation.

India's youth have made themselves impossible to ignore. And they have shown that even the most powerful governments can be forced to bend to the will of the people.
