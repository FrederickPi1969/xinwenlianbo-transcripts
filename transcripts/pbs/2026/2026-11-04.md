## How the Latino shift to Democrats helped turn Texas blue

As we have heard tonight, a major factor in last night's victories were the number of Latino voters who headed to the polls and supported Democrats.

For perspective on this massive swing, we turn now to Maria Teresa Kumar. She's CEO of Voto Latino. That's an organization that helps register Hispanic and Latino voters.

Maria Teresa Kumar, Founder and Executive Director, Voto Latino: Thanks for having us.

So Amy mentioned earlier, of course, that there was the shift we saw from 2024 to 2026. We should also just say, to be clear, Latinos are not a monolith. But how do you look at that and what's behind the people who backed Trump in '24 and went Democrat -- Democratic in '26?

So it's -- folks are absolutely right. Latinos are not a monolith until you come for our families. And that's basically what we saw last night.

It was a repudiation of the ICE raids that have been happening. I can tell you that I can go around the country and there's not a Latino, whether they work in Wall Street or they work in the fields, that don't know someone that's been caught in the dragnet. You marry that with the fact that financially they're not doing well; 36 percent of Latinas that we surveyed in April were skipping meals.

All of a sudden, you realize that there is an opportunity for the Democrats to have a real conversation with them that is not talking about who is and isn't an American. What Amy was talking about, I think, is really important. I think it's something that both the Republicans and the Democrats need to take note, is that the Latino vote, because they are so young -- they're about a generation-and-a-half younger than the rest of the American voter.

They are oftentimes considered very much independent, but the swingiest of swing voters because they don't have a history of voting even among their families. And so they are very issue-driven. And if you promise that you're going to basically provide for them to be able to actually stretch that dollar, then they will listen to you.

But at the same time, if you start criminalizing them, then you're going to see what happened last night.

I want to talk to you about Texas in particular, basically a 30-year grip by Republicans broken last night. You have the first Latino governor in the state in Gina Hinojosa. You have the Democrat, James Talarico, winning the Senate race there. How do you look at Latino participation in those races?

And generationally also, what are the trends you're seeing in Texas?

So, Voto, I tell you, we started investing in Texas almost three cycles ago because we recognized that 54 percent of the kids in classrooms were young Latinos.

And when you go down to the Rio Grande Valley, it is one of the poorest zip codes of people who are incredibly resilient. And when they flipped from left to right, it was because of the flood of immigrants coming across the border that was creating real chaos in their communities, and they were having a hard time stretching that dollar.

So they took a chance on Trump. And what Trump did was overstep. I spent a lot of time with in the Rio Grande Valley, and I was talking to folks that were -- that voted very heavily for Trump and said, look, I'm a builder. It used to cost me $300 to do a square foot. Now it cost me $630 for a square foot because they're deporting my men.

And because of the tariffs, we can't actually be able -- we can't actually try to figure out how to plan for our job, for the work that needs to happen. But I do think that the headline is, is that young Latinos showed up. And, again, when we talk about this idea of affordability versus immigration, for young Latinos, this is where we're going to see a generational shift.

For young Latinos, it was absolutely to protect their parents.

When you look at this broader picture, this is the question we're asking again and again, do you feel like these votes were solely against President Trump? Or was there something also going for Democrats here?

The candidate quality in Texas specifically was through the roof.

Gina Hinojosa is from the Valley. Something that I -- when -- during the 2024 campaign that I often reminded the Harris campaign, neither Harris nor Biden went down to the border. Vance and Trump were there all the time. They did the retail politics so well this last week that their closing arguments were all along the Valley.

If you look at who swung the most, who in Texas, when you look at the map of all the blue, it was all the southern border of Texas. And it proves the point. This was a community that has been long overlooked, but once you recognize them and you gave them something to vote for, they came out in force.

So what do you think Democrats have to do now? They have two years to try to make good on some of the promises that they made. What can they do? What should they prioritize for these voters that they have won back to make sure they don't lose them again?

So, Amna, in August, we actually asked this very question, and we came up with a pothole agenda. Latino voters are looking for accountability on ICE, they're demanding for a pathway to citizenship and for the Democrats to say that out loud, and they're trying to figure out economic relief.

Talk about SNAP benefits. Talk about raising the minimum wage. Talk about how you actually can identify their taxes, and, believe it or not, how do you actually create term limits for the Supreme Court? That's all top of mind.

Maria Teresa Kumar, CEO of Voto Latino, thank you so much for being here. Always great to talk to you.

## Republican mayor says election wipeout is 'rejection of Trumpism'

President Trump has sought to deflect blame in last night's sweeping Republican losses. But within the party, some are beginning to ask whether they have been too deferential to the president and whether the GOP needs to rethink its identity.

To discuss what comes next for Republicans, we're joined by David Holt. He's the Republican mayor of Oklahoma City.

Mayor Holt, welcome back to the "News Hour." Thanks for joining us.

So it was a tough night for your party. You posted on X last night this message, writing: "A political party that's morally and intellectually bankrupt deserves the rebuke of the American people."

It's very strong language. So what do you think Republicans could or should have done to avoid that rebuke, to mitigate some of those losses?

We 10 years ago as a party -- I wouldn't say I was a part of this decision and that -- or that I have gone along with it -- that we as a party ultimately ceded our decision-making and our intellectual capacity to one person and really became a cult of personality, instead of what we had been, at least for the remainder of my adult life, which was a party of ideas, a conventional American political party trying to bring together enough people to win an election and build a coalition.

It changed 10 years ago. And so I don't think this was a -- like something we could have messaged better in the last two weeks. I mean, I think we made a fundamental decision to really not be the Republican Party anymore, but to be this banner carrier for Trumpism, for a specific individual and his whims and desires, and to be the MAGA party.

And what happened last night was such a total rejection of Trumpism and MAGA that really that party now no longer exists as a functional political movement.

We should point out your state of Oklahoma was one of the few bright spots for Republicans, right? You still control the governorship, both Senate seats, all five congressional seats. So if this was a referendum on the president, what do those results tell you about where Oklahoma is?

You're correct. I mean, the Republican nominee for governor did win in Oklahoma last night, but certainly the writing was on the wall that something was shifting even in Oklahoma, maybe not enough to shift all the way over to exchange control.

We did in the '90s and in the 2000s, and so anyone who doesn't take pretty serious lesson from what happened last night in Texas in my neck of the woods is just living on borrowed time, because that's going to happen in Oklahoma.

Oklahomans, Texans, Americans, they're ready to move into a different kind of conversation. I mean, I don't think this is going to be like some quick turnaround. I think this is going to be a multiyear conversation, but it finally began last night, and it needed to. And this country needs two real parties. And I think we're finally going to -- we're finally going to have that conversation that we have needed to have really for the better part of a decade.

That is the Republican mayor of Oklahoma City, David Holt, joining us tonight.

Mayor Holt, thank you for your time. It's good to speak with you.

## New Mexico's Deb Haaland on making history as first Native American woman elected governor

As have been reporting, yesterday's election brought a number of historic firsts. In New Hampshire, Chris Pappas became the first openly gay man elected to the U.S. Senate. In Colorado, Melat Kiros became the first Generation Z woman elected to Congress. And, in Georgia, Keisha Lance Bottoms became the first Black woman elected governor.

Among those making history is Deb Haaland, who will become the first Native American woman to serve as governor of any U.S. state. Haaland previously served as interior secretary under President Biden and was one of the first Native American women elected to Congress.

Congratulations on your victory and welcome back to the "News Hour."

You made history, of course, but voters are also expecting results. So how will you balance the symbolism of this victory with the responsibility now of governing a state that has faced persistent challenges? So many people are dealing with the high cost of living, persistent challenges with poverty, education, access to health care.

How are you planning to deliver tangible results in New Mexico?

Absolutely. Thank you so much for the question and for having me.

And, of course, very proud to be the first Native American woman governor in the history of our country. That wasn't why I was running, though, of course. I have so much experience, having served at the highest level of government as the secretary of the interior, where I managed a work force of 70,000, an $18 billion annual budget.

And, yes, like the rest of the country, our state is facing steep Medicaid cuts; 89,000 people can be kicked off the Medicaid rolls come the beginning of the year, people losing their SNAP benefits. This is all the result of the Trump bill that passed earlier this year.

And so, of course, we want to make sure that we're stepping up, the legislature and myself continuing to fill in the gaps that our governor now has worked to fill in. We know that prices are getting higher due to so many of the tariffs and the war in Iran making gas prices higher. New Mexicans are paying higher costs for everything.

And so we want to put more money in the pockets of New Mexicans, enhancing the child tax credit, the working families tax credit, continuing to move our clean energy forward, which we know will lower utility costs. So there's a lot of work to do, but I'm confident that we can get there.

Democrats had a strong election night across the country. How much of your victory, in your view, was a rejection of President Trump and his approach, and how much of it was an endorsement of your own vision for New Mexico?

Well, we currently have a Democratic governor here in New Mexico. Generally, every eight years -- our governor is termed out the end of this year. Every eight years, the seat flips back to Republican.

And New Mexicans realized that yes, we needed to keep going on the trajectory that we are going on. We have universal childcare. We have early childhood education for every child in New Mexico. And those are things that New Mexicans feel have made their lives better.

And so I want to ensure that we can enhance those programs, expand them across the state, so that every New Mexico family has an opportunity to participate. Universal childcare, it's an economic driver all over New Mexico. It creates jobs and helps parents to know their kids are safe while they're at work.

So it's a win-win for our state and the people of New Mexico, and we will continue down that path.

I wonder, what have you learned from your time in Washington, both in Congress and in the Cabinet, about what government gets right and what it gets wrong, and how does all of that shape your approach to governing?

Well, I will tell you, I feel like I always do the right thing when I listen to stakeholders, when I have the people at the table whose lives would be affected by the decisions that I make.

I did that when I was at the Interior Department, and I will do it again. The most successful projects that we got across the finish line, transmission lines, offshore wind projects, protecting millions of acres of public lands across the country, that was all done with the idea that we need to bring people to the table early, that we should act with full transparency.

So I intend to be transparent as the governor of New Mexico. I intend to bring stakeholders together to tell us what they need and what is best for their communities. New Mexico is different anywhere you go. None of our communities are the same, the northeast, the northwest, the north, the south, Central New Mexico.

There's so many different communities with different needs. And so we're going to make sure that we're listening and that we can do what's best for our state.

Yes. I hear you when you say that you did not run for the sole goal of making history. But you have broken barriers before, as we mentioned, the first Native American woman -- among the first Native American women elected to Congress, the first Native American Cabinet secretary.

Does this victory feel different, serving as governor?

Well, I mean, look, this is my first successful statewide campaign. New Mexico's the fifth largest state, hundreds and hundreds, thousands of miles of rural roads that we drove on all throughout this campaign. So I feel like I worked really, really hard for this victory.

We raised the funds to hire organizers. We had them on the ground all over the state. We worked very hard and didn't take anything for granted. So, I feel that hard work still means something in our state and in our country, and I was really proud of the success that we had.

New Mexico governor-elect Deb Haaland, thanks again for speaking with us. We appreciate it. And congratulations.

By Scott Bauer, Geoff Mulvihill, Associated Press

## The issues that drove voters' decisions in the midterms

And for a deeper dive into the data and why people voted the way they did, Amy Walter of The Cook Political Report With Amy Walter is back with us.

Hello, Geoff Bennett. I feel like I never left.

So, look, President Trump urged voters to treat his -- this election as a referendum on him, and in many ways that's what they did.

The economy was a top issue for voters, as we know, with gas prices soaring amid the war in Iran. How much did voters hold the president and his party, the Republican Party, personally responsible for the economic pain they're feeling?

Yes, I mean, that's the real takeaway from this election. It's not just that people thought that the economy was their most salient issue. It's that they believed that it was Trump's policies that had caused the higher prices.

In The Voter Poll, the exit poll, 63 percent of Americans who voted said Trump's policies are the cause of higher prices. Only 37 percent said it was for some other reason. And here's really -- the next piece of this is the priority piece. It's not just that they were frustrated about the war and its impact on gas prices and on inflation.

The president has mentioned that today, as you noted, the vice president saying it's really the Iran war that's the big problem. But what voters also felt was a real sense that the president was focused much more on his priorities than he was focused on their priorities. Just 36 percent of voters said they believed that Trump has the right priorities; 64 percent said no.

And when asked, well, what does he actually prioritize, 62 percent again said his priorities. Just 38 percent said the country's interests. And this, I think, was really typified, Geoff, by the fact that he ended his campaign -- we talked about this a lot going into the election -- ended it at the Reflecting Pool talking about the work he was going to do there, and then the day after the campaign, he's standing in the still-not-completed ballroom.

Voters looked at that and said, I just don't understand how he could be more focused on this than on bringing down my costs. And I think the issue of betrayal overall -- it's not -- again, I don't think it's just that people are upset that their prices went up.
