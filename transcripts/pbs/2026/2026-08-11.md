## What to know as your driver's license gets a digital upgrade

Your old plastic driver's license could soon be getting a digital upgrade.

Digital reporter Tim McPhillips walks us through three things you should know, including some of the benefits and some of the concerns.

Ian Grossman, President and CEO, American Association of Motor Vehicle Administrators: It's essentially what it sounds like, which is taking your driver's license and making it a digital credential that you can carry on your smartphone, your watch, your tablet.

That's Ian Grossman, president and CEO of the American Association of Motor Vehicle Administrators. They're a trade organization that provides DMVs with best practice guidance, as well as software and tech infrastructure.

Twenty states and Puerto Rico offer a form of a digital driver's license. Some allow you to add it directly to your mobile wallet, like your credit card. Others have a state-created mobile app that stores your I.D.

Two, what are the benefits? Well, the idea is, when you need to show your I.D., you could essentially tap your phone on a reader, like at an airport or a doctor's office, or while getting a speeding ticket.

And that could help to limit the information you share. Take, for example, a liquor store.

They don't need to know your name. They don't need to know your address.

And if, like me, you have lost your wallet and/or left it in a TSA bin, it's more difficult for someone to get into your phone than it is to just pick up your plastic I.D.

What you have time to do is get to any browser anywhere, log into your online DMV account and say, I have lost that device, pull that credential off of that phone.

But there are concerns around this new technology to prove who we are. And that brings us to three, government data collection.

Here's American Civil Liberties Union senior policy analyst Jay Stanley.

Jay Stanley, Senior Policy Analyst, American Civil Liberties Union:

One problem is that the standard for digital I.D. that a lot of the states, although not all of them, are adopting would actually let the government track you.

So what does that mean? Well, when you tap your mobile I.D. on a reader, information about you is sent from point A to point B. That simple transaction involves major decisions. Like, does verification happen locally on your phone or on a server?

And could a third entity like a state or federal government access data on where you used your I.D.? Stanley and the ACLU have rallied against that last scenario called phone home or server retrieval over potential abuses.

For AAMVA's part, Grossman says states that might allow server retrieval would be blocked from using AAMVA's digital I.D. systems.

We have prohibited the ability to what we would say call back to the agency.

All this is pressuring states not to build systems that phone home. But because it is possible to do with the technology and laws in place today, the responsibility falls on our elected officials not to flip the switch. But:

We have seen a couple states that have put in place some very good protections in their laws.

In March, Utah put in place the nation's first digital identity bill of rights that bans phone home architecture. Stanley agrees that states need to be proactive in codifying a phone home ban into law.

The states are sleepwalking into building a version that's not privacy-protective, and we're going to get stuck with that as a standard for potentially generations.

As of now, some state governments on both sides of the aisle have allowed mobile driver's licenses, but others have rejected them, like Texas and Minnesota. But Grossman says widespread adoption is coming.

And Stanley argues that too should be codified in law.

You should have a right to continue to use your plastic license.

But it has a real potential that we're going to wake up one day, we're all going to be forced to get these things. They're going to become like a virtual ankle bracelet.

## The effectiveness and cost of the National Guard deployment in D.C.

It has now been one year since President Trump declared a crime emergency in the nation's capital and deployed thousands of members of the National Guard to its streets.

As Lisa Desjardins reports, questions continue to mount over the mission's cost, its legality, and its effectiveness in fighting crime.

From Metro stations to monuments, these days, soldiers in D.C. are almost as common as sightseers.

Nearly 5,000 troops from more than 15 states currently patrol the capital, a controversial symbol of the president's authority in this overwhelmingly Democratic city.

I'm announcing a historic action to rescue our nation's capital from crime, bloodshed, bedlam, and squalor.

When President Trump announced the deployment last August, combined with a surge of immigration enforcement, it immediately sparked protests and drew criticism from experts, who said the data showed violent crime in D.C. was already at a 30-year low.

Thomas Abt, Senior Fellow, Council on Criminal Justice: D.C., it's no secret, has had above-average levels of crime for many, many years. But crime at the time of the announcement was falling quite rapidly. So there really was no urgent situation that needed to be addressed.

Total crime in Washington has continued to drop over the last year, down another 20 percent, according to the D.C. Police Department. The White House credits the Guard and the Make D.C. Safe and Beautiful Task Force, which it says includes 20 federal and local law enforcement agencies and has made 16,000 arrests in the past year.

But there is debate over the root causes of the decline, particularly if the Guard impacts violent crime at all. Yesterday, on TRUTH Social, the president threatened to sue the author of a study from the liberal Center for American Progress, calling the research a radical left scam.

In July, Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth said critics of the operation were blinded by ideology.

There's nothing political about this exercise. Law and order is something all Americans deserve, black, white, rich, poor, man or woman from D.C. or far-flung places in this country.

Rodney Miller, 50-Year D.C. Resident: It's really unnecessary. It's a kind of a horse-and-pony show.

Polls have shown a majority of D.C. residents oppose the deployment, and the Congressional Budget Office estimates the total cost of taxpayers so far has been at least $660 million.

In November, West Virginia National Guard specialist Sarah Beckstrom was killed and another Guardsman injured in an ambush-style attack not far from the White House. An Afghan national is charged.

The president has also tried deploying troops to other Democratic cities in his second term, like Los Angeles, Chicago, and Portland. Courts ultimately blocked those efforts, but Joseph Nunn at the Brennan Center says they are concerning and unprecedented.

Joseph Nunn, Brennan Center for Justice: No prior president has attempted to use the military for domestic law enforcement in such a wide variety of ways and on the scale that the Trump -- second Trump administration has attempted to use the military.

Meanwhile, in D.C., where Trump has direct control of the National Guard, the Pentagon confirmed last month troops will remain on patrol through the end of the president's second term. The Department of Defense estimated the cost over the next 2.5 years would be at least $1.4 billion.

For more on what the data say about the impact of the National Guard on crime in the nation's capital, we're joined now by Richard Hahn. He is a senior manager for research in the Criminal Justice Department the Niskanen Center, a nonpartisan think tank based in Washington, D.C.

This is not a simple discussion. We will be getting into some of the complexities here, but I want to start with something clear. Major crime is down in the nation's capital. Let's look at some statistics. This is just year over year from the D.C. Police Department, homicides down 32 percent, robberies down 15 percent, car theft down 53 percent.

Again, that's today year to year since the National Guard arrived. In your research, what kinds of crime do you think the National Guard actually affected or impacted?

Richard Hahn, Senior Manager for Research and Evidence, Niskanen Center: Well, the National Guard seems to have impacted mostly low-level opportunistic property crimes, things like petty thefts, people breaking into cars. These are really vexing problems for residents in D.C.

As a D.C. resident, I know that this is something that bothers me and plagues many of my neighbors. However, it doesn't seem like the National Guard had much of an effect on violent crime, which was, of course, the banner issue that they were brought in to combat.

That has been going down though. So why do you think it's not due to the National Guard presence?

So the decline in all crimes in D.C. has been going on for the last three years, really since we saw those peaks in 2023. All types of crime had have been going down.

So what we did was try to distinguish between the effect the National Guard had and what was already going on in D.C. And what we found is, the National Guard had a great impact on low-level property crimes. They declined by about a quarter since the Guard deployed. However, there was very little change in violent and other serious crimes after the Guard deployed.

And that's looking at the difference between what was already happening, what sort of the trend looked like before, and after the National Guard got here. So, yes, year over year, all crime types are down, and that's a great thing. That's a wonderful story for D.C. But it doesn't seem like the Guard actually contributed much to that.

And you're saying that they didn't have an effect over and above the trend that was already happening.

Now, the White House, and I reached out to them today, they argue that federal officers and part of the kind of program that they put in place in Washington, D.C., one thing that they did was take weapons off the streets. Is it possible that that kind of effect could have impacted violence in the nation's capital?

I absolutely think it has impacted violence in the nation's capital.

I think the thing is that you got to look at you got to zoom back out and you have to look at what's been happening for the last three years. And gun arrests have been going up for the past three years. Arrests involving guns by Metropolitan Police have been going up for the past three years, and gun crimes have been going down for the past three years.

So this is really part of a larger trend that's been driven by our local police department that's not really been much affected by the National Guard's presence.

I know that's one of your main points here is, you stress after looking at all this that something that may be overlooked here is the job the D.C. Police Department has been doing.

And you also found and calculated that they have a smaller force now, but yet crime has been going down. How?

So our theory behind this really great trend in D.C. is that police have actually been better at being more proactive. A greater share of their arrests over the past three years have come from stopping crimes that are actually occurring when they arrive, as opposed to waiting in their cruiser or on the corner for a call to come through dispatch for them to respond to.

So they're catching more crimes happening in the act. And that's not something that the National Guard would really be good at. The National Guard tend to be untrained as professional police officers. Their duties entail a whole lot of other things besides policing, things like traffic safety, greening and cleaning.

And as it turns out, they have no arrest power. They can simply detain someone. So a National Guard troop should really not be judged by the same standards as of a police officer. You get much more crime prevention out of a police officer than you do out of a National Guard soldier.

And you sound like, in the police conversation, you're seeing less reactive policing, more proactive policing, make a difference in Washington.

To finish this conversation about the National Guard, they did have a positive effect, as you say, on things like carjacking that are important to so many people, but it will cost. Did you look at sort of the efficiencies here and how that worked?

Yes, we did. The National Guard is very costly.

So, per person, the National Guard costs a little bit over $600 a day, and that's because not only do we have to pay their salaries, but we also have to feed them, clothe them, transport them. And outside of that $600 figure, there's also a hidden cost to the economy in terms of people leaving their civilian jobs to do their tour with the Guard and also leaving their families, and that can't be captured in the figures that Congress released that on which we based the...

It's a very expensive intervention, whereas a police officer in D.C., which is a relatively expensive place to police, is still under about $400 a day.

So that's a considerable difference in cost, and you get much more crime prevention out of a police officer than you do out of a soldier.

Richard Hahn, thank you so much for looking at this, not a simple topic, but you're helping us understand it better.

By Gary Fields, Lisa Mascaro, Associated Press

## Top adviser to Iran's Revolutionary Guard leader sits down for rare interview

As the war with Iran continues its uneasy stalemate, we hear now from a top adviser to the overall commander of Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, the force largely responsible for border and internal security.

The U.S. and many other nations have designated it a terrorist organization. The IRGC, as it's known, rarely give interviews and almost never to Western media.

But Commander Mohammad Reza Naqdi sat down this morning in Tehran for an exclusive interview with special correspondent Reza Sayah.

Commander Naqdi, thank you very much for sitting down with us.

It has been a long-running policy of Iran's Revolutionary Guard to never speak to American media. What changed? Why did you agree to sit with us?

Brig. Gen. Mohammad Reza Naqdi, Supreme Adviser to IRGC Commander in Chief (through interpreter): In the name of God, the most gracious, the most merciful, we never received a request to reject it.

We would like for our voice to reach the ears of people in Western countries. It's Western media that don't like our voice to be heard. There's nothing we do that's indefensible and outside the values of humanity that we would want to cover up or not discuss.

Let's talk about this war. Where do you see Iran in this juncture of the war? Do you see yourself as winning? Are you in control?

Brig. Gen. Mohammad Reza Naqdi (through interpreter):

Certainly, victory is on our side, after the defeat of America's two main objectives, the toppling of the leadership and the breaking up of Iran.

We saw confusion and bewilderment in America. It became a war without a strategy. Every two or three days, they announced a new objective. For several nights, they said they want to invade Kharg Island. They said they want to open the Strait of Hormuz. They mobilized their battleships. Everything they tried, we can show how we were victorious.

In your view, is it inevitable that Iran has won?

Brig. Gen. Mohammad Reza Naqdi (through interpreter):

No, we have not yet reached our final objective. This is clear. Up until now, we are victorious. In the future, we will continue to be victorious.

The longer this war lasts, the more experience we gain. We had never seen a real war to gain real experience to learn how to fight with America. In these five months, we have learned how. We have also seen how the American military is weaker than what we perceived.

Is it Iran's objective to drag out this war, to maybe wait until Mr. Trump is out of office?

Brig. Gen. Mohammad Reza Naqdi (through interpreter):

Look, we have to attain deterrence so that the enemy never dares to attack us, so we can live with security. One way is to prolong this war until we get to the next term of the presidency and cause attrition, so that if anyone else wants to attack Iran, they will know there is a cost.

This week, on at least two occasions, maybe more, Iranian forces attacked non=military ships that were trying to cross through the Strait of Hormuz.

What is Iran's objective in attacking civilian ships?

Brig. Gen. Mohammad Reza Naqdi (through interpreter):

When we are at war, vessels need to pass through a region we can control. When they leave an area that we control, it could be that they're transporting supplies for the enemy.

Do you not see that as a violation of international law, attacking civilian ships?

Brig. Gen. Mohammad Reza Naqdi (through interpreter):

When you're in a state of war, any thoroughfare deemed to be a threat, we can control it. This is international law. Anywhere in the world where there is war, this law exists.

One of your most effective weapons in this war has been your missiles and your drones. Reports said before the war you had about 3,000 to 4,000 missiles. How many missiles do you have in your stockpile right now?

Brig. Gen. Mohammad Reza Naqdi (through interpreter):

First of all, we are not reliant on missiles and drones. If we are victorious, we relied on our faith to be victorious. God is with us.

As for our missiles, the Americans and the Zionists always want to count our missiles. First of all, the number of missiles we manufacture every day is more than the number we launch. In a country this vast, we can make them wherever we want.

Where are you making them? Is it deep underground?

Brig. Gen. Mohammad Reza Naqdi (through interpreter):

We do it in different ways. We have various ways to manufacture them securely. Rest assured, even if this war lasts for years, until the last day, Iran's rockets will be launched.

I will make this point, and this is more important than missiles. If there comes a day when Iran has no more missiles left, that's when we will be even more dangerous for America.

## Search for earthquake survivors in Colombia enters critical phase

In Colombia tonight, the desperate search for survivors is entering a critical phase after the powerful earthquake that devastated parts of that country. More than 180 people have died, but, 24 hours after the quake struck, there are still remarkable moments of hope.

In Colombia today, neighbors and rescue workers gathered shoulder to shoulder, clearing away mountains of rubble in the search for survivors.

In Santiago de Cali, where dozens of homes and buildings have collapsed, at least 86 people have been pulled from the wreckage, among them this 6-month-old baby and the child's mother. Rescuers say they heard the mother's cries beneath the rubble and then dug through the debris of their five-story apartment building to reach them.

More than 3,000 people remain missing after the 7.4-magnitude earthquake. In Pereira, roughly 50 miles from the quake's epicenter, Jairo Vargas joined the search. The architect says he traveled hours to reach his brother's apartment, convinced he's trapped inside.

Jairo Vargas, Searching For Brother (through interpreter):

I'm absolutely certain he's here, absolutely certain. His horse saddle and all his belongings are here. He's not answering his phone. The doorman didn't report him leaving, and his car is parked right there. And he's not out. He's got to be there trapped.

Pereira is among the hardest-hit areas, where entire residential blocks have collapsed into piles of concrete and twisted steel. But rescuers say there is still a critical window to find people alive.

Jose Fernando Usma, Red Cross Rescuer (through interpreter):

We still have a big window, about 24 more hours, of which there is hope of finding survivors. Our biggest advantage is that there may be isolated air pockets or survivable spaces that can allow people to stay alive for a longer period of time.

Colombia is mobilizing resources for a massive rescue and recovery effort. Soldiers who just a day earlier were forced to stop training and brace themselves as the ground shook are now being deployed to help with rescue and cleanup operations.

But reaching the hardest-hit areas itself is a challenge. Colombia's aviation authorities have closed six airports because of structural damage, potentially complicating the delivery of aid for weeks.

Edward de Burgh, Senior Global Security Officer, Project HOPE:

It was a very, very strong earthquake, much more than people had ever experienced before, and we live in an earthquake zone.
