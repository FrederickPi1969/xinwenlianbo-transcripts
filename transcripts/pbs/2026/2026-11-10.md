## 'A Matter of Hope' shines light on social entrepreneurs tackling big issues

Well, hope is tough to hang on to when faced with some of the world's most pressing problems, but it's also a tool to help solve them.

Jim Bildner is the CEO of the Draper Richards Kaplan Foundation, one of the world's largest venture philanthropy organizations. It finds and funds entrepreneurs tackling big issues, from health care access to clean water initiatives and more.

One of those entrepreneurs is Kiah Williams, co-founder of SIRUM, which redirects unused medications to people in need.

My life's work is trying to impact the communities like the one I grew up in, which are Black, brown, and low-income communities in this country. SIRUM started as a student project. And it was like a ragtag group of students, alumni volunteers.

The original idea was this idea that it's just as easy to donate as to destroy medicine. We have grown to a platform and a program that broadly distributes medications, and we have done over a quarter-of-a-billion dollars of medicine to over 300,000 people.

In his new book, "A Matter of Hope," Jim Bildner more stories of impact and how hope fuels that work. For the record, Jim Bildner and his wife, Nancy, are supporters of the "News Hour."

I sat down with Jim recently to talk about the book and his work.

Great to see you. Thank you for being here.

Jim Bildner, CEO, Draper Richards Kaplan Foundation:

No, so, so glad to be here. Thanks for inviting me.

So Kiah is just one of the leaders, changemakers, visionaries, as you call them, that you profile in this book. How did you decide who to choose, who to include in this book?

So, at Draper Richards Kaplan, we have funded nearly 280 leaders like Kiah. And you're asking me which one of these children, these adults who are making amazing things do I love the most. I love them all.

And the nine folks that are profiled in that book share this common characteristic, which is all of them have been profoundly impacted by the problems they're addressing. And unlike too many, they have refused to walk away from it.

And the characteristic that has led them to be able to do these things -- and, by the way, they're not super-resourced, they don't have a ton of money, they don't have incredible networks. It's this ability to see the world as it exists, but also to imagine a future that's different, and here's the key, to be able to turn that imagination into reality.

You capture all of that resilience, this idea to imagine a better world, as you say, under the word hope, which can feel very wishy-washy sometimes.

What did you learn about each of these leaders in terms of where that hope is derived from? How did they hang on to it in the face of what can be enormous challenges?

So, in our world, hope is a verb. It's something you do with, not something that you hope that just occurs. It's a point of view. It's this -- again, this ability to know the world you're living in, so you're not just blindly optimistic, but to see in clear ways what can be different, and the resilience of each of these folks, again, proximity to the problem.

Everyone profiled knew that problem cold because it hit them. It's the ability to believe and see incrementally that, if you stay with this problem and make a difference, over time, you will change it. We have funded 280 investments. Nearly two -- nearly three-quarters are impacting 10,000 lives or more, nearly two-thirds 50,000 lives or more.

And the world has not given me a free pass. And even in the things that I have had happen personally, and I'm not immune to the news, I wake up every day and know we have got incredible leaders who are changing the course and destiny of millions of people. And, I mean, that's just irreplaceable.

You write about a time in your life when hope has really seemed elusive. You write very vulnerably about losing your own son, Peter, to a drug overdose in 2005.

You write, as you put it, that the construct you built your life around was shattered in that moment. How did you hang on to hope in that unimaginable loss?

And so, like so many, at that moment, when I remember coming in -- I had been working out, and I got came into our house. And Nancy, my wife, was on the phone, and I could tell just by her look that Peter had died, and it was the sheriff who had been -- had called her and found my son's body.

And the gravitational pull for folks who've gone through this is almost irresistible. In our case and so many who have gone through this, there's a fear of -- not a fear. There's just a sense of, how did we fail our son who we loved and cared for?

But I quickly understood that you only have one life to live. And I could do more in service to Peter. So I think about him every single day. And the path I chose, as readers will know, now, about a month later, I penned an article that I then read on "Morning Edition" called "Anyone's Son," because I wanted all those folks who had lost their son or daughter to know they weren't alone.

Fifteen years after Peter's passing, your wife, Nancy, was diagnosed with stage four cancer. She was given six months to live. That was back in 2021.

You write in your book: "More than 80 chemo cycles, two clinical trials later, Nancy is still with me."

She's holding her own. She's an amazing human being, and I would not be the person I am without her, as I hope I made clear in that book.

And, again, contextualizing this for your viewers, I got that call that many people get from -- in Nancy's case from her surgeon, who finally got a look at the cancer and literally called me on my cell phone in our office and said: "I'm sorry to tell you this, Mr. Bildner, but your wife has cancer everywhere, and if I were you, I'd put her in hospice now."

And, of course, I didn't. And so what I learned from that experience, and, again, the gravitational pull, what's going to happen to her, what's going to happen to me, was in fact I could be a caregiver. And the joy of taking care of my wife and what she has given back to me, again, is priceless.

Jim, is there something about this moment in time in particular that made you want to write this book, that said this is the message people need right now?

Oh, my gosh, if there ever was a moment in time.

Again, in this prelaunch of the book and writing the book, I have talked to thousands of people, and the underlying feeling I get from them is a sense of hopelessness, a sense of no agency, a sense of an inability to make a difference.

And I just felt like, I have to help them see the counterfactual. We all can do so much more, and that book is about helping people see that.

The book is "A Matter of Hope." The author is Jim Bildner.

## U.S. inflation report highlights strain of rising food, energy and housing costs

Inflation remained stubbornly high last month, adding to the financial strain on Americans already struggling with the high cost of living. The latest Consumer Price Index shows prices rose four-tenths of a percent, driven largely by higher food and energy costs. Housing costs also climbed three-tenths of a percent, accounting for roughly a third of the larger-than-expected monthly increase.

Economists now expect inflation to run at about 4 percent for the year, outpacing wage growth and further squeezing household budgets.

Let's turn now to Austan Goolsbee, president and CEO of the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago.

So, today's inflation report came in hotter than many economists expected. When you look at the numbers, what stands out to you?

Well, it's another distressing report, borderline disturbing.

We have been hoping to get progress, not -- everyone understands that the war in the Middle East leads to higher energy prices, and we have been dealing with that. But several of these things that have been driving up costs were supposed to have gone away by now. So the impact of tariffs on goods prices was supposed to have faded by now.

It still continues. And the -- perhaps the most troubling part is that services inflation, including health care and a bunch of things that people don't need reminders to know that inflation remains too high, services inflation is not coming down, is probably not mostly driven by oil prices or tariffs or things that might be transitory.

And so we have got a real inflation issue on our hands that has been developing, and we're going to have to deal with that.

And you have got food and housing costs that continue to climb, and unlike other expenses, these aren't things that families can simply cut out of their budgets. What does persistent inflation in these essentials mean for the Fed's ability to bring prices under control?

Well, the Fed has a limited tool set, only has the interest rate, and it can try to cool off the economy to cool inflation.

Everywhere I go out here -- and the Chicago Fed's district is kind of heart of the Midwest -- it's on everyone's mind. It's on business mind. It's on nonprofits' minds. It's on ordinary consumers' mind. I got a farmer Downstate Illinois sent me a diesel pump, says prices are killing them, and it's cost-side-driven.

And now we're getting demand-side-driven component that's coming from the AI build-out, unfortunately, that everywhere you're going in the country, they're saying the costs of construction are high, the cost of computers, electrical equipment, computer chips, very high. And it's because they attribute this excess demand, almost conventional stimulus, if you want to think of it that way, that's coming from the data center build-out of multitrillion dollars.

I was going to ask you about the inflationary effects of the AI data center boom. What can policymakers do realistically to ease those kinds of pressures?

At the Fed, again, like I say, we just have a interest rate tool, but business investment does get affected by interest rates. And you have seen, as interest rates have gone up, you have seen various players in that space respond. That's all you can do. This is -- if it transpires the way it has been going, that's just old-fashioned demand overheating from excess stimulus that's coming from massive hype about AI overloading the economy in the short run.

Now, up until now, this has mostly stayed in its lane in the directly affected and adjacent sectors to AI.My fear and my concern is that we're starting to see this drift out into just overall overheating prices of a whole bunch of things, including services, rising way too fast, far higher than our target rate of 2 percent inflation.

At the same time, the job market has been pretty steady and stable. And the law gives the Fed two jobs, the so-called dual mandate, stabilize prices, maximize employment. The employment side is doing pretty well. The inflation side is not doing well, and we're going to have to come to terms with that if this inflation is going to prove persistent.

And inflation has remained above the Fed's 2 percent target for nearly six years now. Is there a concern that you have got businesses and consumers accepting high inflation as the new normal?

Yes, that's a major concern. If that's what happened, it'll be that much more painful to try to get rid of inflation if it kind of gets built into the expectations that everyone has about prices.

It doesn't have to be that way. And the only thing I will say is, it is true we have been above the 2 percent target for almost six years, but that hasn't been flat and high over that time. There were periods where we were making progress, and I thought we were headed back to 2 percent.

Now what we have been dealing with is a series of shocks, some from oil prices, some from tariffs, now some from the demand side, and those -- a lot of those things were supposed to go away. And they might still go away and prove transitory, but whenever you start hearing the word transitory, your -- the hair on your neck should be standing up a little bit, because we went through that, where it was supposed to go away.

And we found out that, when you have major disruptions to the supply chain, those oftentimes end up being far more persistent than let's call it the theory says they're supposed to be.

Hey, Austan, I almost made it through this entire interview without asking you whether the Fed should raise or cut rates, which I know you always enjoy.

But, look, we have got a minute left. And I ask the question because your colleague Christopher Waller has said he's open to a larger-than-usual...

... half-point interest rate increase in December. Given today's inflation numbers, what do you think?

Well, look, you know I'm not a fan of being very specific and tying our hands at the next meeting or meetings in the future.

I will say it's the -- literally the biggest table I have ever seen in my life in that room. So everything is always on the table, and we must get inflation back down to target, and it's going the wrong way at this point.

The ever diplomatic Austan Goolsbee, president of the Chicago Fed, always good to speak with you. Thanks.

Well, it's a fact that presidents of both parties have tended to either get chalk on their strips -- their spikes or go over the line prescribed in the U.S. Constitution.

Well, on that point, Hegseth could argue that the president, as commander in chief, has broad constitutional authority to order military operations, and he was simply carrying that out as defense secretary. How much legal protection would that offer?

If I were voting, I would reject that defense.

Because the president cannot declare war unilaterally. He -- the Constitution binds. It's the supreme law of the land. It governs him and it governs Hegseth. And the president cannot pretend to arrogate to himself the power that Congress alone possesses.

So the claim that Hegseth was only following orders doesn't get him anywhere, because those orders were illegal.

independent responsibility. That's the thing about a system of law and that includes accountability. You cannot invoke somebody else as the giver of orders and say, well, I wash my hands of this. I'm just doing what I was told.
