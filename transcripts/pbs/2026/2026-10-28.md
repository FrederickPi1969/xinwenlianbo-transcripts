## Remembering pioneering labor leader and civil rights icon Dolores Huerta

Finally tonight, we remember the life and legacy of Dolores Huerta, the pioneering labor leader and civil rights icon. Huerta died today from cancer at her home in Bakersfield, California.

Leaders from around the country paid tribute, including in California, where flags were lowered to half-mast in her honor.

"Yes, we can," Dolores Huerta's belief and words that helped build a movement.

It means activism. It means that people can have the power to change things in their lives, change things politically, and it means that every person should feel their own power and know that they can make a difference.

Her rallying cry power at the United Farm Workers union and more widely, the labor and civil rights movements.

Dolores Clara Fernandez was born in 1930 in New Mexico. As a young schoolteacher in the San Joaquin Valley, her students' poverty inspired her to start organizing farmworkers, as she told the "News Hour"s Ray Suarez in 2012.

These children that were my classroom were the children of the Dust Bowls, the little Opie and Archie kids, and they were coming in there also and kind of hungry and threadbare. I thought that was so wrong. So I thought, well it's better to just get up and start organizing the farmworkers and I decided then to quit.

She eventually joined forces with Cesar Chavez in California's Central Valley to found what would become the United Farm Workers Union.

The union joined the Filipino workers' strike against grape growers over low wages and working conditions that lasted five years. As the union grew, it organized multiple major strikes and boycotts through the '60s and '70s, and it helped negotiate contracts with big growers to win benefits for those in the field, as chronicled in this 2017 documentary "Dolores."

We will take economic pressure, strikes, boycotts to force recognition and obtain collective bargaining rights. The social and the economic revolution of the farm workers is well under way, and it will not be up until we receive equality.

Geraldo Cadava, a professor of history and Latino studies at Northwestern University, says Huerta's presence on the ground was key to her emerging voice.

She was a huge kind of influence. She was on the ground from the beginning. She was knocking on doors with Cesar Chavez. She was visiting farm workers in their homes. She was with them in the fields. So she was a real fighter and leader and influencer within the movement.

She spent almost four decades as the union's vice president and as the second in command to the charismatic Chavez until his death in 1993.

But for decades, Huerta had kept a stunning secret to herself. Chavez had raped her during those early years. Huerta only went public earlier this year.

I had to protect the movement. I think that we accomplished a lot with the movement. So I don't know. Should I have spoken out sooner? I don't know. But at least I'm glad that I'm finally getting this off my chest finally.

An investigation from The New York Times found that Chavez had sexually abused multiple women, some of them children. Huerta told The Times that Cesar Chavez's assaults resulted in two pregnancies that she concealed, two children she gave up for adoption.

She later was in a long-term partnership with his brother, Richard Chavez, and had 14 children overall. The revelations shocked the world, especially many in the Latino community. Throughout her life's work, she suffered again and again. Huerta was jailed 22 times over union demonstrations and was once critically injured after she was beaten by police.

She was active in politics for decades as well, volunteering for Senator Robert F. Kennedy's presidential campaign. Huerta was on stage with him when he was assassinated in 1968. Years later, she campaigned for Presidents Clinton, Obama, and Biden.

In 2012, President Obama awarded Huerta the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

On a personal note, Dolores was very gracious when I told her I had stolen her slogan, "Si, se puede," "Yes, we can." Knowing her, I'm pleased that she let me off easy, because Dolores does not play.

Maria Teresa Kumar, Founder and Executive Director, Voto Latino: Dolores Huerta was a giant.

Maria Teresa Kumar is the president of the political organization Voto Latino. Huerta was a mentor of hers.

When Voto Latino first started, one of the biggest challenges was how to navigate a space that didn't necessarily welcome women. This was after the first wave of anti-immigrant sentiment, and Dolores stepped up and she came to us and provided us counsel. She provided us cheering, she provided us strategy, and she provided us, I think, just generous, generous friendship.

Dolores Huerta never slowed down. The labor leader, civil rights icon, and feminist activist was still rallying well into her late 90s.

Every one of our segments of society that are trying to make positive change, that are fighting for social justice, this is what we have to do, come together, organize push back, take that direct action, and then we can make the world a better place.

## America's long history of gerrymandering and its role in this year's midterms

Nearly 35 million Americans will vote in congressional districts that were redrawn ahead of this year's midterms following partisan battles over political maps.

As the nation marks its 250th anniversary, our Judy Woodruff explores the origins of gerrymandering and why this centuries-old practice remains one of the most powerful tools in American politics. It's part of her ongoing series Crossroads: America at 250.

The items on these cards are rare political artifact, protected by Library of Congress curators who carefully brought them out of their Archives.

What you're seeing today is a copy of the original Boston Gazette, where you see the actual first appearance of the gerrymander cartoon.

At the time, Massachusetts Governor Elbridge Gerry had approved redrawn districts in the state in a bid to keep his party in power. As the story goes, a newspaper editor saw a salamander in the map's outline, combined it with Gerry's name, and a new word entered the American political vocabulary, gerrymandering, or, as it became, gerrymandering.

This is, I would argue, probably the very first example of a viral meme in American politics. So it spread around The Federalist newspapers in the New England era, and then over the subsequent year it was reprinted in almost 100 newspapers across the country.

It's depicted as this mythical salamander beast.

The library also has the wood blocks used to print this piece of history. More than two centuries later, gerrymandering is flourishing and playing out in state after state.

The latest round of mid-decade redistricting intensified last year when, at President Trump's request, Texas redrew its congressional maps to try to add five Republican seats. After that, other states entered the fight. California Governor Gavin Newsom called a special election to redistrict and pick up more Democratic seats.

Nick Seabrook is the author of "One Person, One Vote: A Surprising History of Gerrymandering in America."

He says the practice of manipulating district lines to protect political power is actually older than Gerry. It began with George Burrington, a colonial governor of North Carolina in the 1730s.

His attempt to manipulate the districts of the colonial assembly was motivated by a desire to try and prevent the columnists themselves, who elected the lower house of that legislature, from pushing back on the taxes and fees that his administration was charging to try and expand the footprint of the North Carolina colony.

So he had a motive, or motives, for what he was trying to do.

Does that qualify as redistricting or gerrymandering as we know it today, manipulating?

I would say this was certainly gerrymandering because there was no -- there was no legal obligation for him to make any changes to these districts. He approached it purely as an exercise in maximizing his own political power.

This year, that maximizing of political power has ended up with mid-decade redistricting in nine states, Texas, California, Utah, Ohio, Tennessee, Louisiana, Alabama, Florida, and North Carolina.

From the colonial days of George Burrington to today, North Carolina has been one of the most gerrymandered states in the country. When congressional maps are redrawn, they sometimes split communities right down the middle. That's the case in the small town here of Sharpsburg, North Carolina, where three counties come together.

Several counties shifted from one district to another, changing the demographics of both, spreading out eligible Black voters and weakening their electoral power. The city of Wilson is one of the communities plucked out of the First District and placed in the Third. For the 50,000 people who live here, the question is, what will the switch mean for their voice in Congress?

Because elections are our only voice, there's no way that we can that we can fix that when the election itself has been -- has been rigged against us.

## How the Associated Press tracks and calls races on Election Night

Nearly 40 million votes have already been cast ahead of Election Day. And that's for races that will determine not only who controls Congress, but also governors, mayors, and city councils.

And, as we said, elections are locally run. So how many people does it take for AP to gather results? And is this done in person, online, both?

Both, all. Wherever we can get vote count, we're looking for it. And that's actually one of the hallmarks of what we do is multiple sources.

So we will employ. 4,000 stringers this year, reporters essentially, who go to local election offices and wait for results to be posted or they talk to election officials as they are releasing ballots. We also know that those results are posted online in many places. Some states and some counties have electronic feeds.
