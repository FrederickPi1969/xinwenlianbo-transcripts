## On 'Settle In,' Kathryn Jezer-Morton and Geoff Bennett discuss social media's impact

Social media has become the main way we share our lives with family, friends, and strangers alike.

In this week's episode of "Settle In," I spoke with Kathryn Jezer-Morton, author of the new book "The Story of Your Life" about how the incentives of social media platforms have narrowed the kinds of stories we tell and shapes how we see ourselves.

You write about what you call the redemption narrative, the pressure to turn whatever difficulty one is going through into this story of personal growth or triumph. Why are we so drawn to that?

Kathryn Jezer-Morton, Author, "The Story of Your Life: How Social Media Shapes the Way We Experience Everything": You know, I think that's something that is really at the core of American identity. I mean, I think that the project of the United States was sort of built on this idea of starting something in a new place and overcoming the odds in order to make it work.

And I think that's the story of capitalism. It's the story of economic progress as we know it today. So, to me, this is just an extension of that. But it's just not the only story. And I do think it makes sense to tell a story about redemption when you're talking about a business, when you're talking about an enterprise.

I think it makes less sense to lean on that narrative when you're talking about a family or an individual, just because a family's life is always going to have ups and downs.

There's something almost radical these days about saying, yes, this experience was difficult, and it didn't necessarily make me a better person.

It was just something I had to go through.

Yes, right. That's right. That's -- to kind of just let it land on that kind of unresolved note is really dissonant, I think, to the contemporary ear. People want to hear a resolution. They want to hear the takeaways, what I learned from.

And this is really, I think, a product of this narrative orientation that we have towards redemption. Make it better. Learn something from your troubles. And I just don't believe that that's always the path that we take. And the people that I spoke to in this book really reinforced that idea for me that it's not always just better and better and better.

Well, say more about the people with whom you spoke for the book and how you determined who you would speak with and what stories you would include.

Yes, I mean, it really just started leaving my bubble, trying to find strangers.

I joined like Meetup.com groups, just any place where I could go where I would be able to talk to strangers without making people uncomfortable. I went on Facebook and community groups, and I asked if I could talk to people. I really just kind of tried everything.

And I just spoke to a lot of different kinds of people, mostly millennials, I would say. And I heard a lot of stories. Basically, if someone told me a story that really stuck in my head, I would be like, I'm going to build a chapter around this, and then I would find other stories, and I would just kind of put them together.

But I kind of let the data lead the way, and I was just looking for people to surprise me. And it was not hard to find people to surprise me, I have to say. It was a lot easier than I thought it was going to be.

One of the real paradoxes you identify in your book is that social media promises individuality and self-expression, but actually produces an enormous amount of conformity.

What did you find when you were sort of researching and stitching together that part of your book?

Yes, I call it like a small-C conservatism. People want to replicate ways of being that they have already seen, which is a fundamentally conservative orientation. Like, I want it to be the traditional way.

And I think a lot of that is just because people -- not everyone is like a natural-born storyteller with a huge creative well of ideas, and they just figure, hey, that looked cool, I'm going to do it that way. So, I mean, that's just a basic human orientation.

But I also just think that we -- this is now our version of success. When we see someone taking a cool-looking vacation, we're like, that's what success is. I want to do that. If I'm able to do that, I will have succeeded. And so, yes, it does sort of funnel us into these ways of telling our story that are really, really similar to each other.

And you can watch all episodes of "Settle In" on our YouTube page or wherever you get your podcasts.

## Arthur Jafa explores complexities of Black American life in new exhibition

Senior arts correspondent Jeffrey Brown met with Jafa for our arts and culture series, Canvas.

Yes, I mean, you know what I'm saying? You -- if there's something you're interested in, you don't necessarily just want to consume it. You want to produce it as well.

A lot of it is just leaning into an aspect of moving images that I think is musical.

Music, culture, history, the full variety of lived Black experience and all its complexities, as in one of Jafa's newest works, "M.J.," for Michael Jackson, the mesmerizing performer, the troubled individual.

There's the moment in "Love Is the Message, The Message is Death" where a woman says, what would America be if it loved Black people as much as it loves Black culture?

So you are looking at the both the beauty and the violence.

Yes. Sometimes, I say we like the canary in the mine shaft, a Western civilization, but the mine shaft is an incredible place to be. Black culture exists in a perpetual state of emergency. And I mean that in both senses of it, like crisis and the pressure of crisis, but also constantly emerging, constantly coming up with new things.

And that coming up with new things, this sort of inventiveness, the improvisation, all that, it is -- and it's strictly bound up with the pressure of Black life. You can't separate the two.

And you're capturing that in these films.

I'm capturing it. I'm trying to -- like I said, I'm trying to make a thing that's like it, that is modeled on in some kind of way, so is a rich enough proxy of the actual thing, you know?

You have said many times you don't think of yourself as a protest artist or...

Yes. So it's not a political artist. This isn't -- it's not...

It's not, not political either. If you make a rendering of a Black anything, Black child, Black grandmother, it's going to be inherently political just because this is an anti-Black environment, but it doesn't mean that's necessarily what your motivations are. It's like we exist, we continue to exist, we have a very complex relationship with our continued existence, and we recognize that other people have a complex relationship with our continued existence.

So they say you make art about what you know, and this is what we know, so I'm trying to make art about what I know.
