## Is the American Dream still achievable for younger generations?

Achieving the so-called American dream has been a national ethos for generations. But what does it mean today and how attainable is it?

Judy Woodruff has our look. It's part of her series Crossroads: America at 250.

## Utah Gov. Spencer Cox on his blueprint for depolarizing a fractured nation

A year ago, in the aftermath of the shooting death of conservative activist Charlie Kirk in Utah, Governor Spencer Cox emerged as a prominent voice calling on Americans to step back from the political hatred and violence gripping the country.

Now the Republican governor is expanding on that message in a new book, "Off Ramp: How to Be a Peacemaker in an Age of Contempt." Part memoir, part examination of America's deep political divisions, the book makes the case that Americans can disagree, even fiercely, without treating one another as enemies.

I spoke with Utah Governor Spencer Cox recently.

You have spent years telling Americans to disagree better. Is it working?

Look, as we travel the country, the data's pretty clear that 70 percent of Americans hate what's happening in our politics right now. They're desperate for something different, for something better, and maybe something older, a return to the norms that were so important in the formation. And really, our country lasting 250 years, they want to be able to work together to solve problems.

They haven't seen a lot of problem-solving coming out of Washington, D.C., recently. And I'm as hopeful as I have been in a long, long time that people are ready for something better.

And President Trump, of course, routinely uses the dehumanizing language that you warn against. He's called his political opponents vermin. He's described Americans who disagree with him as the enemy from within.

And you endorsed him in his 2024 race. How does that reconcile with the central argument of this book and what you say you stand for?

Well, it's something that I talk about in the book, and I hope people will read what I wrote there. But I didn't support him in -- as the Republican nominee in 2016 and 2020, and realized that that didn't have much of an impact. I tried the adversarial approach.

And then, after an attempted assassination on his life, I wrote him a very long and detailed letter about what I hoped he could do differently to try to bring the country together, and that I would I would work with him to do everything I could to help that happen.

Now, he would tell you if he was here that that's not his approach to politics, and it's something that we disagree on. And yet we figured out how to work very closely together on issues that are important to Americans and important to Utahans, like saving the Great Salt Lake and some of the energy issues that we're facing.

And so I also had a great relationship with President Biden, someone I disagreed with passionately on lots of different issues, and yet we found ways to work together as well. And I'm hoping that that can be a bit of a model to the rest of the country, who really are hungry for this.

How much can regular Americans really change the political culture, when the incentives of social media and cable news and electoral politics reward outrage? That's the uncomfortable political reality, is that outrage works.

And here's -- maybe the most important takeaway of the book is this, that we actually can change it. One of the things I'm most worried about isn't what's happening at the national level. It's what what's happening at the personal level.

If you go back 15 years ago, only about 5 percent of Americans had ever ended a close personal friendship or stopped talking to a family member because of politics. We have quadrupled that number in just a few years, and it's continuing to climb. That's where the biggest problem is.

Look, we get the politicians we deserve. If we want something different, we have to decide, every single one of us, to be better. We have a whole bunch of ideas in there about how to have a conversation with your uncle who's MAGA or your niece who's woke or what whatever it is, about how we can repair those relationships.

Look, politicians are not worth ending friendships over. We're just not worth it. And I truly believe that, if we can start to work in our communities and fix those relationships, we can actually fix our country in a very big and profound way.

You know, it strikes me this idea of disagreeing better can sound like a luxury to people who feel like their lives and livelihoods are very much on the line.

How do you respond to that, to people who want to see some sense of urgency in the political conversation?

There's a difference between peacemaking and peacekeeping. Peacekeeping is just that. Hey, go along to get along, kumbaya. We all have to hold hands and get along no matter -- it doesn't matter what you're going through. That's not what this is.

Peacemaking is actually -- it's harder. It takes real courage to do, and it's the only way to accomplish what we want to accomplish. It's so easy to tear down and burn it all down and destroy institutions and tear people down, but what you end up with is just ashes.

Building is much, much harder. But it's also much more successful. And I give countless examples of how this has happened throughout our politics historically in this country. It's how we got a Constitution in the first place, this Constitution that requires us to work together. We always think, if we just win the next election, we will never have to deal with those people ever again.

And the Constitution says, no, you're always going to have to deal with those people. And if we -- the things that are making people so angry, I think, are a byproduct of the polarization, not a byproduct of us actually working together.

And so this, to me, is how -- the things that people are angry about, this is how we actually deal with those things and fix those things.

One thing you write in the book really stood out to me. You said that Americans want architects, not arsonists.

The question is, in today's politics, can architects still win?

We have been working with some researchers at Stanford. They have a Depolarization Lab there that studies these types of issues. And what we found was, when we -- we have gotten governors together, Republicans and Democrats, to film these ads that we release. We're saying, hey, we can disagree without hating each other.

We disagree on many things, but we can work together, find common ground. And it turns out they have tested those messages on thousands of Americans and found not only does it have a depolarization effect, which is good. It also has a -- it improves our support for democratic norms, which is also good.

But maybe the most important thing is, it significantly improved the way that voters saw both of those candidates and made them much more likely to support those candidates in the future, which shows that, while, yes, we know that negative ads and negative campaigning can work, positive campaigning also works.

And as that pendulum starts to swing, as that supermajority of Americans that hate politics right now start looking for something different, they have proven that you actually can win elections with a positive message. And, in fact, it may be the best way to win elections going forward.

The new book is "Off Ramp: How to Be a Peacemaker in an Age of Contempt."

Utah's Republican Governor Spencer Cox, always great to speak with you. Thank you.

## Evan Gershkovich reflects on arrest in Russia and reclaiming his life after release

It's a story that now feels familiar. An American goes to Russia, gets arrested, and after spending time in prison and going through a sham legal process, the American is ultimately released in exchange for a Russian criminal jailed in America or elsewhere.

It's called a prisoner swap. And it's a tactic Russia's President Vladimir Putin has used time and again. In 2024, Evan Gershkovich, a Wall Street Journal correspondent, was freed from detention in Russia as part of a complex prisoner swap that included 16 people from seven different countries.

Evan details his arrest and his time in prison in his new book, "This Cursed Beautiful Land."

I have said your name so many times. It's so nice to say, Evan Gershkovich, welcome to the "News Hour."

Evan Gershkovich, Author, "This Cursed Beautiful Land: A Russian-American Story": It's nice to be here. Thanks for having me.

I want to back it up a little bit, so people know a little bit about you, because, like a lot of first-generation American kids, you're the child of Soviet emigres, you grew up with a foot in both worlds. You write about speaking Russian at home and watching Soviet cartoons in suburban New Jersey.

And then you write in the book: "Why were my parents after leaving the Soviet Union so fixated on preserving their Sovietness?"

So, we have had an argument about that. I think it's nostalgia, and I think I'm right. They grew up in a place and then they left that place, and that place no longer exists in their lives.

They speak Russian to each other. They have Russian names. Why would they -- why would their kids be suddenly, like, completely foreign?

So my sister and I both had Russian names. Mine was Evan or Vanya and is. And so my home was Russian-speaking and the food was Russian. I think it's natural for immigrants to want their kids to retain something of themselves.

So you're on a reporting trip after a couple of years there to Yekaterinburg in 2023. You're meeting with a source who a colleague had referred you to, so a trusted source, who said he was going to provide insight into tank production, right? This is a part of your reporting into the Russian military buildup, possible invasion of Ukraine.

And he hands you papers, and as he does that, masked men stormed the restaurant. You write in the book: "It registered immediately that this wasn't the police. This wasn't a misunderstanding."

You seemed to know right away you were being arrested.

I was reporting fairly sensitive stories. I'd reported a story about how Vladimir Putin was getting information from the front, making decisions about the war.

I'd gone to Pskov, which hosts the 76th Division of the Russian airborne forces, which had occupied Bucha in Ukraine and committed a series of war crimes there. And I was speaking with people around the Kremlin trying to gauge always whether it was safe or not, and was getting the sense that they wanted -- they continued wanting to have foreign media there.

What I got wrong was that the FSB, Russia's domestic intelligence service, which formerly the KGB during the Soviet Union, operates above that. And so when they come in to arrest me, or when they say they're FSB, that's when you know something's really serious.

And then the next signal is actually when they take me into the van and then they take me to the station. What I was expecting after 5.5 years of reporting is potential torture, being tied up to a chair like this, electrocuted. I thought, if they really think I'm a spy, perhaps they're going to be interrogating me for information or telling them about the source.

None of that happens. They just hand me my charges of espionage, and that's when I have, I describe it in the book, a sense of relief, of, oh, I know what this is about, how you started this segment with...

... people have been set up for swaps before.

Because you had seen previous, you had covered previous cases, right?

You have covered Trevor Reed. You covered Brittney Griner. So do you think right away, OK, I'm going to be freed in a swap eventually?

While I was covering the Brittney Griner swap, a source had told me that the Russians were actually asking for Paul Whelan for Vadim Krasikov, who was an FSB assassin held in Germany at the time sentenced for life.

And leading up to my arrest, we were trying to pay attention to the negotiations that were going on, and the Americans were telling the Russians that this is insane. Germany's a third country. And the Russians were saying, well, if Paul Whelan is ever going to leave Russia, Krasikov needs to leave Germany.

And I had talked to my Russian lawyers early on, and they remember me in the first week saying, I think I they took me for Krasikov, like stacking Americans, basically, to go back to Washington and say, talk to Berlin.

I got to ask you about the way that a lot of folks in the rest of the world saw you during your time in detention, which was during your court hearings. And you write about in the book how you knew that would be when a lot of people would be able to put eyes on you.

You were held in this glass cage during your court hearings as this sort of sham process plays out. And a lot of folks thought you might have been having, like, a slight smirk on your face or a slight smile. What was going through your head in those moments?

So, really early on, I start figuring out that they're going to treat me in a kind of special way, so to speak. So what -- the experience I went through is not like it's something pleasant. And it was very difficult and grueling in periods.

But I -- really early on, you could tell they're not using the methods that they can use, right? So I'm not getting tortured, I'm not getting deprived of food, hot water, and so on. So the Russians are really, really, really calculating. And in this case, I assume that they're taking somebody, Wall Street Journal correspondent, an American.

It's going to be global uproar. And they want to be able to say, well, we caught him red-handed. We have legal due process. We're not mistreating him, also a journalist that's going to write a book about it after.

And so, understanding that, you also understand sort of like where the lines are for you. My family and loved ones and everybody is freaking out at this point. They don't know what's happening with me. It's a black box. I understand this is the first time they're going to see me, but also global news media is going to cover it.

And so I wanted to send a kind of, I don't know a message -- maybe not a message, but present a kind of fit stance about my stance about the case. I was also told I can't speak in court. And so, instead of incurring any sort of potential bad punishment for speaking up, I decided, well, I can do it with how I present myself, and one of the ways is kind of like signaling what I think about the process.

And clearly it worked. Everybody was in on the joke, so to speak.

You write about a time, because your parents fly over to be there with you and help advocate for your release and keep you updated.

And you write about one time that your dad is able to speak with you, and he says: "We have come here with respect for you."

What was that like to hear that at that time?

So I was racked with guilt at the beginning -- at the beginning, when the arrest took place and sort of thinking, it's one thing to take a risk to be reporting in Russia at the time, right?

And then the story we were reporting on, I mean, it's incredibly sensitive information during a war where Russia feels like it's not just at war with Ukraine, but with what it calls the collective West, with the U.S. at its backer, and I'm an American journalist. And so I'm thinking, like, why did we do this? Why did my newsroom and I decide that this is OK to be doing? My poor family.

And so I was worried when they were going to come to court. The conflict had developed to the point where Ukrainian drones had started reaching Moscow, and I'm worried about my parents coming, but also, yes, this point of, would they have kind of be mad at me, admonish me?

And so when they come into the courtroom, the first thing they turn and see me and they just smile. And so that was really this kind of immediate sense of relief. They come up. And the FSB investigator in my case allowed us five minutes to speak.

And so first it's jokes back and forth, catching up on various things. And then my dad paused my mom and me and said: "I just want to make sure I tell you this." And he softly said: "We have come -- yes, we have come here with respect for you."

And I think that's the most important thing he could have possibly said, because it immediately -- all those worries of have I done something wrong float away. It just helped me deal with it better.

You are, as we said, eventually freed in one of the most complicated swaps in recent history, negotiated by the Biden administration.

But I wonder how you look back on your 16 months in detention at a time, as you document in the book, everyone seemed to know, the prison guards, your investigators, everyone else, that you would eventually be freed in a swap.

And yet 16 months of your life go by while two political adversaries work it out. Does any of that make you angry now?

I mean, what makes me angry is the system of hostage diplomacy, so to speak, using people cynically to return people. And I think that's something that we haven't completely dealt with.

But in terms of the actual swap, 16 months, that's not long. I reasoned early on that it would take a year-and-a-half to three. And, ultimately, I think it happened fairly quickly, 16 months compared to -- and some people have told me, well, it could have happened immediately. We all know what the swap's about.

But we still have to get there. And, ultimately, you -- I do hope that the moral good outweighs the moral bad in this case, given not just myself. I wasn't the only one released. But some of the people that were included in the swap compared to who Russia got in return, I hope says something about our values.

What's the adjustment after being released been like for you?

Took just amount the -- just about the same amount of time as I was in. I went through the prison experience with this sort of really stoic approach of I can't let myself break down or cry. I tried not to be angry at the system, tried to stay level-headed, but that takes a toll.

And so when I am released, all that starts coming out, but also through adjustment to regular life, I described it like coming down from the International Space Station and having left kindergarten into adulthood all at the same time, because you're incredibly isolated. So I'm overwhelmed by all the senses, the sights, the smells, the sounds, and I can't sleep in the beginning at all.

And then you have lost your agency, so relearning how to make decisions, you know, what you say yes to, what you say no to. And so I was in PTSD therapy in the beginning, sort of tracking my symptoms. And then after six months, I graduated. Everything was improving over time.

Then I did talk therapy, and then I worked on the book, and that in a way is a kind of narrative therapy. You look at the traumatic event, you think about it, you process it, and you deal with it. And I think that also -- but that also keeps you in prison in a way, like the event...

Yes. I was constantly thinking about it. And so it also, I think, kept me from completely returning to life. So I finished the book in the late spring, got back to work for The Journal, became a reporter again.

The news had faded. People were no longer recognizing me. I'm kind of just like back to my normal life and feeling like a normal person. And it's great, but it took a bit.

One day, I would hope to be able to go back, but I just don't -- I'm not that optimistic.

The book is "This Cursed Beautiful Land." The author is Evan Gershkovich.

Evan, it's good to see you. Thank you so much.

## Former diplomats assess U.S. withdrawal from Iraq and region's future

U.S. forces formally withdrew from Iraq today, marking the end of a long and turbulent military presence. The withdrawal brought celebrations in the capital, but also concern about what comes next.

Thousands gathered on the streets of Baghdad for a celebration more than 20 years in the making, the military trucks and soldiers in fatigues, this time Iraq's very own, as the U.S. announced its forces have officially left the country. Iraq's prime minister called the withdrawal an opportunity.

Ali Faleh Al-Zaidi, Iraqi Prime Minister (through interpreter):

A new phase has begun, one defined by Iraq's sovereignty, the strength of its institutions, the readiness of its forces, and balanced partnerships with its friends.

But as the war in Iran expands and draws in neighboring countries, U.S. bases in the northern Kurdish region have come under attack. And the Kurds who remain fear further attacks by Iran-backed groups or the resurgence of the Islamic State.

Osman Kamusashi, Erbil, Iraq, Resident (through interpreter):

I don't favor this at all. I mean, the departure of the American army and the coalition from the Kurdistan region will impose a serious threat on the Kurdistan region. And there is another threat, which is the restructure and the return of the Islamic State.

Despite those concerns, the U.S. was facing a deadline. The withdrawal had been agreed to two years earlier under former President Biden and was ultimately carried out by President Trump.

The Pentagon called the orderly departure a milestone that reflects the success of a 12-year campaign that defeated ISIS and said it will continue to provide targeted training and intelligence support. That continued support is still a dramatic scale-down from more than two decades of U.S. military involvement in Iraq.

U.S. forces invaded in 2003 and toppled the country's oppressive dictator, Saddam Hussein. The first U.S. withdrawal came in 2011, only for U.S. troops to return three years later to help Iraqi forces fight ISIS. Decades of brutal fighting resulted in the deaths of more than 4,000 U.S. troops and hundreds of thousands of Iraqi civilians.

Now, as Iraqi leaders tout strength and sovereignty, the world watches to see if Iraq can withstand outside forces.

For insight and analysis of the formal U.S. withdrawal from Iraq, we turn now to James Jeffrey, who served as ambassador to Iraq under former President Barack Obama and was the special envoy to the Global Coalition to Defeat ISIS in the first Trump administration. And Feisal Istrabadi served as Iraq's ambassador to the U.N. after the U.S. deposed Saddam Hussein. He's now a professor at Indiana University.

What does the U.S. withdrawal from Iraq change practically starting today?

James Jeffrey, Former U.S. Ambassador to Iraq: Practically, what it changes is a mission accomplished. In a time where we have not had that many successful military operations, this one from 2014 on was spectacularly successful in defeating a major terrorist entity, the Islamic State.

We did it, tragically, with casualties, but very, very few compared to our other wars. And we did it with the cooperation of Iraqi and Syrian officials. That's very important. But the bottom line was, when it was finished, they said, we would like you to go at this time, and we said, fine.

It doesn't change very much, because, as we just heard, we will still have a military presence, as we did after 2011, when I was ambassador, under the embassy.
