## Ben Wikler and Geoff Bennett discuss the Democrats' plan on 'Settle In'

While there is no shortage of analysis about what's wrong with the Democratic Party, Ben Wikler is focused on what Democrats plan to do about it.

The former Wisconsin Democratic Party chair has written a new book. "This Is the Plan," laying out lessons from one of the most closely divided states in the country and what they could mean for Democrats nationwide.

I spoke to him for the latest episode of our PBS News podcast "Settle In." Here's a clip of that conversation.

I want to return to what you said about the systemic reforms that you are calling for, eliminating the filibuster, reforming the Supreme Court, effectively replacing the Electoral College.

When Democrats controlled the White House and Congress during the Biden presidency, they failed, chose not to enact many of these same reforms. So why should voters believe that Democrats would behave differently if given the option again?

Ben Wikler, Author, "This Is the Plan: How to End America's Meltdown and Save Democracy": Well, the first thing is to choose Democrats who are ready to take action if they have the power to do so.

Now, some of these things came very close to passing during the last Democratic administration. The national gerrymandering ban was contained in the For the People Act, and that passed the U.S. House. It was the first bill that the House majority passed in the U.S. Senate. There were almost enough senators to amend the filibuster to allow pro-democracy reforms to go through.

And Joe Biden called for that to happen, said he would sign it into law. The two senators who stood in the way on the Democratic side are not in the Senate anymore. That's Joe Manchin and Kyrsten Sinema. Every major Democratic nominee for Senate that I'm tracking across the country is committed to either amending or ending the filibuster to do pro-democracy, pro-voting reforms if they get into power.

And that's a really big shift from where we have been just recently. Down the road, we will think through who will be in the presidential primary. And I don't know who the right person to meet the moment is going to be. But I do think that it's on all of us to look out for people that are not going to try to restore some status quo that, frankly, was broken in and of itself, but rather want to move to a future where things actually work for everyone.

And the thing that I kept kind of bumping into as I was working on the book is that to shift the way our institutions work in order to build a better democracy is actually in line with the best and oldest traditions in our political system. At our country's founding, there were horrific problems and crimes. There was slavery. There were -- only about 6 percent of people had the right to vote when the Constitution was enacted.

But there was also embed in The Federalist Papers and the Constitution, the Declaration of Independence the fundamental idea that the legitimacy of government came from support of the majority of, as James Madison wrote, the great body of the people, that that was the basic idea.

And over and over, we changed the number of Supreme Court justices, we changed the size of the House, we amended the Constitution, we added states, we changed the way people were elected. All those changes were commonplace. It was a matter of course that, as the country evolved, we were going to adjust how these things worked to be able to meet the moment.

And the circle of freedom kept expanding in fits and starts and contractions sometimes. But we became a better democracy over time. And then things got stuck. About half-a-century ago, it's like it all got frozen in place. The filibuster got more and more restrictive. We stopped amending the Constitution. The House got stuck about a century ago.

And now it's treated as though all these things were etched in stone, written from on high. We can't change any of those things. We have to wriggle through these tiny passageways to make any kind of change. And right now, the way our rules are set up, it's easy to cut taxes for rich people. It's easy to confirm Supreme Court justices who want to overturn Roe v. Wade and strip away protections for people across the country.

But it's very, very hard to do anything that helps protect the right to vote or supports people organizing a union or doing things that most people across the country across party are crying out for. So we don't have to live like this. We can elect people and then advocate and push those people to deliver changes to how laws are made that then allow us to make laws that serve everyone, that serve the common interest.

And you can watch that full episode and more from our "Settle In" podcast on YouTube or wherever you get your podcasts.

## New exhibit showcases the long-hidden artwork of Avital Sagalyn

At the very tip of Cape Cod, a new exhibition is showcasing the work of an artist who spent two summers there back in the 1940s. For much of her life, she remained largely unknown in the art world, by her own design.

But now, as her paintings receive greater public attention and remarkable stories from her life emerge, including an unlikely friendship with Pablo Picasso, that is all starting to change.

GBH Boston correspondent Jared Bowen introduces us to the work of Avital Sagalyn for our arts and culture series, Canvas.

This is a discovery, these works painted in 1945 and 46 here in the coastal community of Provincetown, Massachusetts, with their defining lines and bold, broad splashes of color. The artist delivers us directly into the harbor's salty air, where the dewy fog almost settles on your skin.

Christine McCarthy, CEO, Provincetown Art association and Museum: It was one of those truly 100 percent discovery moments where not just myself, but the members of our exhibitions committee, when we were looking through the proposal, we're like, why don't we know who this artist is?

The artist is Avital Sagalyn, a painter for most of her 95 years before her death in 2020. For her, painting was a private practice. She had no interest in museums or galleries, which might tell her how to do it.

That gave her a purity, says Christine McCarthy, the longtime CEO of the Provincetown Art Association and Museum, now featuring a survey Sagalyn's Provincetown work.

That's one of the most beautiful parts about being an artist who isn't interested in any of the commercial side of it, and I'm making this body of work because I want to record either something that moved me or moved me to make this. She's an artist that should be known. Her story is really, really good.

That story is now being shared by Sagalyn's son, Dan, and his wife, Elaine Grossman.

Dan Sagalyn, Son of Avital Sagalyn: It wasn't until 2017 that I really got a sense of all her work.

Both he and Grossman are journalists gallery. Dan is a foreign affairs producer for the "PBS News Hour," and began teasing out the details of Sagalyn's artistic life.

Elaine Grossman, Daughter-in-Law of Avital Sagalyn: She didn't want to talk about it a lot, but she felt like she was ready to share it, and that she could share it on her own terms.

Avital Sagalyn demonstrated an aptitude for art by age 3, but hers was a childhood marked by the savagery of World War II. In 1940, her family fled their home in Belgium as the Germans invaded and attacked, dropping bombs on innocents.

I remember putting my arm -- my hands over my head and becoming very religious, asking God to save us.

The family passed through Spain, where Sagalyn's mother introduced her to old masters in the Prado Museum, as she explained to her son in this 2016 conversation.

We had to see Velazquez and Goya and El Greco. Even at a very early age, I was very interested in art.

As a teenager, she studied art in Lisbon as the family awaited visas, enabling a move to the United States.

Arriving here, she was soon featured in a student exhibition at the Museum of Modern Art.

Avital painted one of the works that we think is the most moving and dramatic work of her lifetime while in high school called The Horror of War, showing this black what I think is a crow flying overhead, meant to symbolize the Nazi warplanes and very graphic details of soldiers in the foreground.

Sagalyn wouldn't show in a museum again for 70 years and never returned to the subject of war. But she continued painting and it flourished in Provincetown.

Over two summers here, she captured daily life images, such as peers, telephone poles, and buildings, returning to paint them again and again and again.

Avital had a practice of doing what she called variations on a theme in almost all of her artworks, and she felt compelled to explore and explore, to experiment, to experiment using different materials, different perspectives on her subject matter, until she had finally captured what it was she was trying to say.

The way that she's applied the color, it's subtle. She has really created the depth within this piece, and you feel the fog. You feel the movement.

McCarthy says she's especially struck by Sagalyn's use of the line, how such simplicity and spareness can say so much.

I can tell you that I have umpteen boats sunsets, but when you look at these, the dunes, even that line, like, the line is so exquisite and elegant, and it's almost like she never even lifted whatever tools she was using, whether it was a pencil or a brush. It's so lyrical.

And I think of a conductor doing this type of this with, like, a baton.

Sagalyn was always lured by light, especially in Provincetown, where the glow from sand and sea spills into the town in ever-shifting shades. In studying the works of Turner and Cezanne, she came to revere what she called the transformative effect of light that drew her into spaces, whether a beach or Notre Dame Cathedral, to nearly divine effect.

She was not a very religious person, but she saw a sense of sacredness or a sense of spirit. She used to sometimes call it the essence of what it is that she's looking at.

For Sagalyn, Provincetown was a pilgrimage. This is an arts colony where the greats have gathered, from Jackson Pollock to Mark Rothko to Helen Frankenthaler. She sought out the guidance of artist and teacher Hans Hofmann, who taught numerous soon-to-be-famous painters.

She showed him her sketches and she showed him her drawings, and he looked at it and said, I can see you have your own style and your own technique. You don't need to take classes. Just keep doing what you're doing.

And she did. After submitting two of her Provincetown paintings, she was named a Fulbright Scholar. The program was a pathway to Paris, where Sagalyn immersed herself in an artist's circle that led to Picasso.

She went to the gallery to look at his work and then out came Picasso and he said, "Avital," and he gave her a big hug, and my mother was trembling because she said, here I am with the arms of Picasso.

They had a great many discussions back and forth and really took to each other immediately.

Despite having Picasso as a mentor, Sagalyn continued to shun public attention, until 2019, when, at age 94, she finally consented to her first solo museum exhibition.

Now, with this second show, and as public attention expands, her legacy and work are creating a new ripple in the history of American art.

Now, when I see you know a fishing boat or a pier, it's going to be a Sagalyn pier or a Sagalyn telephone pole. Like, now in my mind, it's -- she's arrived here, and in such a big, big way.

For the "PBS News Hour," I'm Jared Bowen in Provincetown, Massachusetts.
