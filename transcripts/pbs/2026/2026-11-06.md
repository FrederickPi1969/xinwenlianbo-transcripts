## Remembering Jay Rockefeller's life and legacy of public service

And now we remember Jay Rockefeller, the former West Virginia governor and longtime U.S. senator, who devoted more than half-a-century to public service.

Born into one of America's wealthiest and most prominent families, Rockefeller forged his own path in politics, becoming a leading voice on health care, education, and the needs of working families.

Across five terms in the Senate, he earned a reputation for putting the interests of his constituents ahead of partisan politics. He was also the husband of Sharon Rockefeller, president and CEO of WETA, the public television station that produces the "PBS News Hour."

His early jobs were at the Peace Corps' offices in Washington, but Rockefeller wanted a more hands-on position tackling poverty. That drive took him to rural Emmons, West Virginia, where he worked to improve the lives of young people in the community.

Over the next decade, he instead rose up the West Virginia political ladder. He also met and married his wife, Sharon Percy. Their wedding was featured on the cover of "LIFE" magazine.

## Former Sen. Flake, outspoken Trump critic, on the GOP reckoning post-midterms

As Democrats celebrate their historic gains, Republicans are beginning to take stock of major upsets and what comes next for their party.

Here with us now is former Arizona Senator Jeff Flake, one of the most outspoken Republican critics of President Donald Trump during his first term and the author of the 2017 book "Conscience of a Conservative."

Senator Flake, welcome back to the "News Hour." Thanks for joining us.

I want to begin with that piece of news today that President Trump has elevated Stephen Miller to be his new chief of staff. This is the man who's the architect of the president's hard-line immigration agenda, so many other policies.

What does that decision, just days after huge Republican losses in the midterms, say to you?

Well, it suggests that the president is doubling down.

I had hoped that, on that issue to immigration, that that's one area where the president might reach an agreement on certain issues revolving around immigration. I know, when I was in the Senate and the president was in his first term, he seemed willing to do that, but he was always pulled back by people like Stephen Miller.

And so I'm a little discouraged to see that, because, on some issues, he might be inclined to work with the Democrats, but not so much now.

Does that tell you anything about what we should expect to see these next two years?

Yes, I -- unfortunately, I think it does.

I think the president would not have elevated Stephen Miller if he expected to reach some deals with Democrats on certain issues. I hope I'm wrong on that, but that's kind of been the history.

We know that there are still a few races left to be called from this election, but the picture's very clear. We're talking about generational losses for the Republicans. Extinction level is how Florida Governor Ron DeSantis put it.

And I'm sure you have seen President Trump, Vice President Vance have basically said this was all about the Iran war. Do you agree with that?

No. Certainly, that played a big role, but on the economy and affordability, obviously, was the biggest issue for voters. The original sin for this administration economically were tariffs. Tariffs raised prices for everyone.

And that was long before the war started. So, no, it wasn't just the war. And then it wasn't just policy as well. It was just style, Republicans referring to Democrats or anybody they didn't agree with as socialists or communists or pedophiles or jihadists, that just hope -- gratefully ran its course, and people are tired of it.

And it was reported late today that Arizona Democrats gained a trifecta to control the state house, Senate, and governor's office for the first time in 60 years. What does that tell you about what's going on in your state?

One anecdote to let you know how it is for a clerk of the superior court in Maricopa County -- Maricopa County is the fourth largest county in the country with about nearly four million residents -- a Democrat, a 19-year-old, won that seat from a former state legislator Republican who wasn't scandal-ridden. That's kind of how it went in Arizona, and I think was replicated elsewhere in the country.

So, back in 2019, you wrote this in a tweet.

You said: "One day in the not-too-distant future, Republicans will wake up and say, we did this for this man?"

Well, I think so, from some other Republicans, yes, but then you hear some of the talking heads or those who have closely aligned themselves with the president, whether it's think tanks or other advocacy groups, and you see they're sticking with it.

Well, nothing focuses the mind like a huge election loss, and we underwent that.

Those are winning issues if we can get somebody who has some distance from the president, because I think to have some saliency with the voters, you're going to have to have distance from the president.

I take that to mean you don't think that J.D. Vance will be the party's nominee. So who would you like to see? Would you support someone like Marco Rubio?

Yes, Marco has managed to keep some distance. It kind of depends on how long he stays in the position he's in. I don't suppose he can be there very long without tying himself directly to the president. He's managed so far.

So, yes, Marco could. Marco's very bright, and you know where his heart is. I have known him for years, and I think he really doesn't agree with some of the things that the president has done. So, yes, he's one. There are others. I'm hopefully -- hopeful some new faces will come up, or maybe some governors, if there are any left, who can actually breathe new life into the party.

What about you? Would you ever consider running?

I don't think so. I did 18 years in elected office, and that's probably enough.

Former Arizona Senator Jeff Flake, thank you so much for being with us tonight. Appreciate your time.

## Georgia Gov.-elect Bottoms reflects on historic victory and the path forward for Democrats

Georgia voters made history this week, electing Keisha Lance Bottoms as the state's first Democratic governor in more than two decades and the first Black woman ever elected governor in the country.

The former Atlanta mayor campaigned on lowering costs, expanding access to health care, and pushing back against the Trump administration. But she will face the challenge of advancing that agenda in a state where Republicans still control the Senate.

I spoke with the governor-elect earlier today.

Governor-Elect Keisha Lance Bottoms, welcome to the "News Hour."

You have, of course, made history as the first Black woman elected governor in U.S. history. What does this moment mean to you personally? And what message did Georgia voters send with your victory?

It's deeply personal for me. My family goes back at least five generations in Georgia. We are able to trace our roots to a plantation in Crawfordville, Georgia, where my great-great-grandparents were once enslaved.

So just on a personal level, to have come from people who had to toil the soul of Georgia, and to now be elected governor has exceeded every dream that I ever had for my life. So I'm grateful for that.

And just for the people of Georgia, for us to have had the blue wave, or the blue tsunami, as it's being described in this state, really represents a shift in Georgia, throughout the South, I believe, that's going to reshape who we are as a party, as a state for many years to come.

I wonder, have you spoken with Stacey Abrams since your victory? As our viewers will know, she ran for governor herself and spent years rebuilding the political infrastructure that allowed for Georgia to be competitive for Democrats.

What do you think this moment means for the movement that she helped build?

Yes, I haven't spoken with her, but for so many of us across the state, it's been about building on progress that's been made. And we take all the lessons from elections passed, we keep all the great things that were done, and then we make corrections where necessary.

And this has been decades of work in Georgia. We haven't had a governor, a Democrat governor, elected since 1998. And there have been so many elections since then, so much work that's been done, not just from groups that Stacey has worked with, but a lot of groups across the state, names who never get called on national news, but people who've been deeply committed to building a true grassroots movement in this state.

And it's finally all come together in the way that we have hoped that it would for a very long time.

What lessons should the national Democratic Party take from your winning campaign?

I think, across America, hopefully, everyone realizes that you have got to listen to people and those issues that matter to them and focus on those things that impact their daily lives.

Throughout the course of this campaign, it's been about the cost of living and people asking me directly, what can you do, what will you do as governor to address these issues, and then going back and delivering plans to do just that?

And, hopefully, Democrats across the nation -- and just based on the results, it appears that we all had the same takeaways, that we needed to focus on the things that mattered to people and not get distracted and not get pulled into fights that weren't productive for us to be able to deliver.

Affordability, as you mentioned, was central to your campaign. You have promised to raise the minimum wage. How do you plan to deliver on promises like that, given that Republicans still control the state Senate? How quickly can Georgians realistically expect to see your agenda put into action?

Well, we were able to take the House this time, and we are really one seat away from controlling the Senate. So we're going to try and find those things that we can get bipartisan agreement on, those things we can get done immediately.

And if minimum wage, for example, is one of those things, then we're going to move forward with it. But it may be something that we have to wait until we have control of the Senate. But I do believe things like health care and education funding are things that we can cross party lines to find consensus on immediately.

Finally, there is enormous symbolism in your election, especially for the Black women who have been so central to the Democratic Party's success over the years.

Symbolism also creates enormous expectation. I wonder, do you feel additional responsibility to deliver, knowing how many people see themselves in your victory?

You know, for me, it is just the responsibility to deliver. I moved around this state, and I asked people to support our campaign and to believe in the vision that I was setting forth for the state of Georgia. And I want to make sure that I'm true to my word.

So it is -- it's beyond who I am and the symbolism, representation of who I am. It is just about being an effective leader on behalf of all Georgians.

Georgia's Governor-Elect Keisha Lance Bottoms, thanks again for your time. We appreciate it. And congratulations.

## Sen.-elect Hamilton reflects on why Kansas voters chose a Democrat for U.S. Senate

Democrats' midterm election wins have reached red states throughout the country. That includes Kansas, a state that hadn't sent a Democrat to the U.S. Senate since 1932.

That will soon change after Reverend Adam Hamilton, who leads the largest United Methodist Church in the country, defeated Republican incumbent Roger Marshall on Tuesday night.

Congratulations, Reverend. Thanks so much for joining us.

Oh, it's a joy to be with you today. Thank you.

So, 94 years since Kansans have sent a Democrat to the Senate. Why do you think they chose to do so this year?

I think there was a lot of frustration with what's happening in Washington.

Across the state of Kansas, there were people who had voted Republican all their lives and yet looked at what was happening in Washington and felt like things were happening there that were -- that were not kind, they were cruel, they were immoral, they were corrupt, and policies that were hurting people in our state.

And I think there was a lot of folks who said, this is not OK. This is not who we are.

You put messages of decency, of morality right at the center of your campaign. You said that those are rooted in your faith. How does your time at the pulpit translate to how you talk to voters in this campaign?

Well, first of all, I have spent my life communicating with people. That is what I do. And so every Sunday you're asking the question, how does this -- how does our sacred text, the old New Testament, how does it speak to people's daily lives? And part of that is trying to understand where are people, what are they struggling with, what's happening in their lives.

So, when you go out, and, as a pastor, I will just tell you I deeply care about people. I tell folks I don't have very many remarkable gifts. My superpowers are that I listen carefully, I care deeply, and I fight for people.

And so, when I traveled across the state, listening to people, hearing their stories, hearing their pain, their frustrations, their disappointments, and then imagining with them what a better future looks like comes naturally as a pastor. And then communicating on the campaign trail is a lot of fun for me.

And -- but the biggest thing is, I have spent 38 years trying to ask, what should the world look like? And Jesus talks about this in the Sermon on the Mount in the Lord's Prayer. He asks us to pray, thy kingdom come, thy will be done on earth, as it is in heaven.

And I tell people who have no particular religious faith, I say, if you think about that, this is like, what is the world supposed to look like? And we're hoping to see that happen. And as a -- as somebody who's running for office, you're imagining what that world should look like, and then you are willing to go fight to see it happen.

One of the first big decisions you're going to have to make is what you want your leadership to look like when you get to the Senate. You said you're not going to support Chuck Schumer to be majority leader. So who do you think should lead the party in the Senate at this point in time?

And I mean no disrespect by saying that about Chuck Schumer. He's had a long and distinguished career. I just -- I think we need leaders who are going to bring people together, Republicans and Democrats together, centrists, moderates, and progressives bringing people together, conservatives. And I would like to see...

And you don't think Chuck Schumer can do that?

I'm not saying he can't. I'm just saying, in my mind, I don't know Chuck very well. I'm just saying that I feel like we need to have people who are determined to do that.

I also think it's time for fresh ideas, young newer ideas, newer people coming in, and folks -- I hear that a lot from people in Kansas as well, both Democrats and Republicans, saying, I think we have got to have fresh ideas. Can I ask you about some news? We heard just today about President Trump naming Stephen Miller as his new chief of staff. What kind of message does that send to you as an incoming member of the Democratic majority that he has promised to work with?

Well, I would say I think, for many of us, Stephen Miller seems to be the author, the person who has put together so many of the things that the president has done that we don't actually like.

So, whether it's about immigration and how we talk about immigrants, or it's the war in Iran, it's policies related to tariffs, in all of these ways, dismantling so many of the important agencies in the federal government, if he is in fact the person who is -- who's behind these ideas, placing him as the chief of staff seems -- it just seems difficult and challenging, I think, for many of us.

Well, look, I want to stop the corruption, and I do think there has been a lot of corruption happening in Washington and just ways of doing things that would have been considered irregular, immoral, unethical in the past. And what about the accountability looking back?

So what I also want to say that there's a part of me that says, I don't want to spend the next two years in trials.
