## Remembering Dolly Parton, whose artistry and generosity touched generations

And, tonight, sadly, we add another name to that legendary pantheon, Dolly.

Bryan Seaver, Nephew of Dolly Parton: Dolly opened doors, not just for me, but for her entire family to reach the potential we didn't even know was possible. Dolly also opened doors for generations of singers, songwriters, musicians, and dreamers from every walk of life and across every continent.

By her example, we believed that anything was possible. She never saw a door she couldn't open, and the doors she opened made and changed history.

Dolly Parton's seven-decade career brought her international acclaim and unadulterated adoration from fans drawn to her music, her unparalleled charisma, and her trademark wit.

Born in 1946, the fourth of 12th children, Parton grew up in a one-room cabin in the Smoky Mountains of East Tennessee. Her father paid the doctor who delivered her the only way he could afford, with a sack of cornmeal.

We had a roof over our head, I always say, even if it did leak. We had something to eat on our table, even if it wasn't exactly what we wanted.

Parton began composing songs before she could read or write, in 1959, at just 13 years old, making her first appearance at the Grand Ole Opry, a stage she would return to time and again.

From the early days of her career, Parton championed the rights of women and their ambitions in her music. On her second solo album, 1968's "Just Because I'm a Woman," she brought the women's liberation movement to her lyrics.

Through the more than 1,000 songs Parton wrote, her message and her music resonated. She sold more than 100 million records, with 49 top 10 country albums and released more studio albums than any other female country artist. She won her first Grammy in 1979. Nine more followed, with a total of 55 nominations throughout her career.

And, in 2011, Parton received the Recording Academy's lifetime achievement award. Two of Parton's songs, "I Will Always Love You" and "Jolene," have been inducted into the Grammy Hall of Fame.

In 1980, she made her big screen debut alongside Jane Fonda and Lily Tomlin in the hit comedy "9 to 5."

In a male-dominated industry, Parton was larger than life and unapologetic, speaking openly about her cosmetic surgery and navigating the industry as a woman, including with Judy Woodruff in 2020.

Of course, I have been hit on all my life, and especially in those early days. But I knew how to manage that myself. I never sold out, never did anything that was going to take away from who I was as a human being.

Rivaling her cultural impact through music, her social impact through philanthropy. In the early days of the COVID pandemic, Parton's million-dollar contribution to Vanderbilt University's coronavirus research helped to develop the promising Moderna vaccine.

Parton publicly got a taste of her own medicine in 2021.

That didn't hurt, just stung a little bit, but that was from the alcohol pad, I think.

Bucking the expectations of her time, Parton chose career over kids, but famously said -- quote -- "God didn't let me have children so that all kids could be mine." She helped millions through her Imagination Library, donating 200 million books to children around the world.

The idea, she told "News Hour" in 2013, came to her from her own father, who couldn't read or write.

It really, really started out as a very personal thing for me, and it was originally just meant for the folks in my home county because of my dad.

Parton never endorsed a political party, but did speak passionately on a few issues, including the Black Lives Matter movement and LGBTQ rights.

When someone blatantly asks you, do you think Black lives matter, well, of course, Black lives matter. We're all God's children. We all have a place in this world. We all need to be loved and respected. I don't care if you're, like I say, Black or white or whatever. I don't care if you're gay or lesbian or transgender. Everybody should have an equal chance.

Over her lifetime, Parton's many businesses comprised a massive empire worth an estimated $650 million. An early entrepreneur, 20-year-old Parton first established a publishing company in 1966, helping her retain her own publishing rights over the years.

In 1985, she co-founded Sandollar Productions for film and TV projects. One year later, her amusement park, Dollywood, held its grand opening just minutes from her hometown.

But the best thing about this memory is, you can relive it.

I know that. They do. They think that I just made him up. Some say I'm having a relationship with my girlfriend Judy. We have just had a great friendship. But we have been married. My husband just does not want to be in the spotlight.

From small-town girl to global icon, Parton earned single-name status and a legion of devoted Dolly fans, leaving behind a catalog of incomparable hits, a legacy of generosity, and a shining example of a life well lived.

## A look at the devastating impact of USAID cuts in rural Afghanistan

Well, it's been 18 months since the Trump administration made across-the-board cuts to foreign aid and began the process of dismantling USAID,or the U.S. Agency for International Development.

Tonight, we examine one of the country's hardest hit, Afghanistan.

Refugees International attributes more than 160 deaths over the past 18 months to cuts in aid, with devastating consequences for mothers and infants.

Now special correspondent Leila Molana-Allen and videographer Eric O'Connor have the latest report in our continuing series from Afghanistan five years after the U.S. military withdrawal.

Some names have been changed for protection. And a warning: Some of the images and accounts in this story are disturbing.

The remote rocky mountains of rural Herat. We're heading to one of the many villages here, now cut off from medical assistance by international funding cuts. As we arrive, a man suddenly runs over asking for help. Someone's collapsed. He tells us to hurry.

Abdul Salem, Village Resident (through interpreter):

He was struggling to breathe, and then he fell.

When Abdul Salem's brother started having chest pains, they called a doctor in the nearest town 30 minutes away. Unless they could get him here, the clinic said, the family was on their own.

Then he started having a heart attack. They still wouldn't send an ambulance. Our team is trained in emergency first aid. We jump in to do what we can to save him. But he's been without oxygen for too long.

His name was Abdul Rahman (ph). He was the head of this community, a beloved father, husband and brother.

His brother says he was the best man in the village. He tries to tell us about him, but he's overcome by emotion and can't continue. All day, the mourners come. As the sun sets, they bury him high on the hill overlooking his home.

These desperately poor villages were once served by rural and mobile clinics and feeding services funded primarily by the U.S. Agency for International Development, or USAID. But after the devastating funding cuts of the past two years, that lifeline for remote areas like these has disappeared.

As we start to speak to other villagers, it's the same story down every alley in every home. Like most of the women here, Simagul never has enough to eat. She tried for seven years to have a baby. Finally, last year, little Fahim arrived. She was so undernourished she couldn't breast-feed or afford other food for him.

Unable to get to a government hospital or pay their fees, 6-month-old Fahim got worse.

Simagul, Bereaved Mother (through interpreter):

He constantly had fever, vomiting, and diarrhea. If he got better for two days, then he'd get sick again.

One day, Fahim fell unconscious, shaking violently. With the economy here in tatters, Simagul's husband was away in Iran looking for work. Knowing that Taliban checkpoints wouldn't let her through alone, she had to search both for a male chaperone and a motorcycle to borrow, wasting precious minutes as little Fahim convulsed in her arms.

By the time I found my brother-in-law to take us, my child's condition had already become critical.

When they finally reached the nearest clinic, miles away in Adraskan, there was nothing they could do for him. They were sent to the hospital two hours further in Herat City.

He had another seizure. He died on the way before we even reached Herat. At that moment, when they told me my son had died, I lost all sense of myself. I didn't know what to do.

Now her baby girl, Somaya, not even a-year-old, is starting to show the same signs, constant diarrhea, a distended belly, rashes on her skin.

I just pray my daughter doesn't die like my son.

Yeganeh is just 21. Her husband is 70. She's lost five children in her young life.

Yeganeh, Bereaved Mother (through interpreter):

I was 15 when I got married. I gave birth to one child every year and they all died.

She remembers when her first little girl began to waste away, riven by the symptoms of malnutrition and bacterial infection.

We didn't have anything to treat her with and she died. Each one, their stomach suddenly swelled, and they died. I feel heartbroken. As a mother, it's very hard to give birth to five children, and they all die.

Now she has another baby on the way. The villagers believe she's cursed and want to take her baby away from her. She knows all she needs is proper care for her and the child.

The doctor said you should treat yourself, but we don't have any money to pay for my treatment. I'm afraid that this child will die like the others. I wish for this baby to live.

Her stolen children are buried in the vast rolling plains that lie between Yeganeh and the hospital that might have saved them. She can't even visit their graves without permission from her husband.

Laleh is a midwife who used to work for a mobile clinic here run by the International Rescue Committee; 80 percent of their work in Herat was funded by USAID.

Once, we saw a woman being transported by her husband on a motorcycle. We delivered her baby in the middle of the road. If we weren't there, the mother or the child could have died on the way to the hospital.

They lost that funding last year and tried to limp on to sustain these vital services. But, earlier this year, the money ran out and they were forced to close.

Now, without support from health workers like her, maternal and child mortality is spiking.

Now I think, who provides services to them when we're not there? I'm really heartbroken when I hear that women and children of my country have died, and I couldn't do anything for them.

Worse still, the Taliban has decreed that women must be accompanied by a mahram, a male guardian from their family, at all times outdoors, even in a medical emergency. Health workers across Afghanistan report women and babies dying at Taliban checkpoints because they aren't allowed to pass to get to hospital.

There was a woman here who was in labor. Because the midwife who came didn't have a mahram, they sent her back home. As a result, both the mother and the baby died.

Laleh now faces unemployment, and with the Taliban crackdown on women working, she can no longer support her family. She fears female-focused health care will soon disappear entirely.

Because education for women is banned, no new midwives have graduated for five years. I see the future for Afghanistan and Afghan women in a very dark situation.

This crisis isn't a slow decline, but a sudden and crippling shock. After last year's USAID cuts, nearly 500 clinics closed; 300 more shut earlier this year, leaving six million people without any local health services.

As always, it's the poorest of the poor with no other options who are the hardest hit. Child hunger in Afghanistan has now hit a record high, with over a third of the country's provinces facing critical levels of acute child malnutrition. And, this year, nearly half Afghanistan's children aren't getting enough to eat.

Even for the lucky ones who do make it to a hospital, their condition is often so severe they can't be saved.

Dr. Sikander Ghani heads the child malnutrition ward at Nangarhar's government hospital on the other side of the country from Herat.

Dr. Sikander Ghani, Head of Child Malnutrition Ward, Nangarhar Government Hospital: Yes, she is three months old.

And the seizures and sepsis are caused by the severe malnutrition.

With local maternity clinics and child feeding services across the country now closed by funding cuts, parents travel huge distances from four surrounding provinces to get here. Many wait too long out of fear they can't pay. Nearly 20 percent of the babies who come here now die.

Last month, they hospitalized more than 250 kids, their highest ever intake.

Dr. Sikander Ghani (through interpreter):

Most of the children come in very bad condition, often unconscious or in a coma. When they come late, the symptoms are much worse. So curing it becomes much more difficult.

The hospital used to receive USAID funding, but now that's gone too.

Dr. Sikander Ghani (through interpreter):

They shouldn't tie these health services, these humanitarian services to politics. When USAID was funding us, the condition of the hospital was good. Specifically, the conditions of malnutrition were better. See for yourself. Now the equipment here, most of it's damaged.

With such limited resources, it's an endless battle. Sometimes, they have enough medicine, oxygen, beds. Sometimes, they don't.

Dr. Sikander Ghani (through interpreter):

It's truly distressing when a child dies in front of our eyes. With a well-equipped unit, their lives could have definitely been saved. It hurts. But what can we do? We're trying our best.

Farmi sits quietly in the corner, her shoulders sagging with despair as she clutches her emaciated baby to her chest.

Farmi, Mother of Patient (through interpreter): His bones are sticking out.

Mortaza is 6 months old. He weighs just 4.5 pounds. He should weigh closer to 20.

He needed formula milk, but we could not even afford that. We also couldn't even afford to bring him here. Poverty is a terrible thing. When you have no one and no money, you can't do anything. No one cares about you.

Cradling her baby on the floor of the hospital, she tries desperately to feed him formula over and over again. But he's too sick and can't keep it down.

He can't even tell us where he's in pain. Whenever you touch him, he cries.

They have been here six days. The doctors have done what they can, but Mortaza's condition is too advanced. He's not getting better.

When they said he would not get better, my heart dropped. I lost hope and thought he's going to die.

Mortaza is Farmi's only child. His twin brother died in childbirth. She doesn't know what she will do if she loses him.

My message to Americans is this. Help this place. I cry constantly for him. He has suffered so much. When I look at him, my heart breaks.

A generation of mothers and children abandoned to suffer and die in the dark.

## New study reveals widespread signs of brain trauma among former NFL players

At least one in four NFL players has CTE when they die, that is the concerning conclusion of a new study out today that examined the brains of former pro-football players.

Chronic traumatic encephalopathy, known as CTE, is a brain disease thought to be caused by years of sustained head trauma. Symptoms can include depression, anxiety, and aggression, and, later in life, memory trouble and dementia. Because CTE can only be diagnosed after an autopsy of the brain, the study's researchers say the actual number of NFL players suffering from the disease may be even higher.

For more, we're joined by Dr. Chris Nowinski. He's a former college football player, co-founder and CEO of the Concussion and CTE Foundation, and a co-author of this study.

Dr. Nowinski, welcome to the show. Thanks for joining us.

Chris Nowinski, Co-Founder and CEO, Concussion & CTE Foundation: Thanks for having me.

So, in the study, you focused on about 235 donated brains from former NFL players who died between 2016 and 2021. I mean, did you expect the rate of CTE to be so high? Tell us more about what you found.

Yes, I mean, ever since we started studying NFL players' brains, it's been more than 90 percent of the sample. But, over time we got much better at getting a larger sample of NFL players and built a partnership with the retired community so much that we got more than 25 percent of NFL players' brains.

Unfortunately, that rate is still more than 90 percent in that sample. We have always hoped that this disease might be rare, but I think this is that final nail in the coffin to say, no, this is absolutely not a rare disease in the NFL population. And now we know it's somewhere between 25 percent and 98 percent.

And we need to figure out what it is between those two numbers.

So that one in four number we're seeing is likely a conservative estimate? Is that what you're saying?

It's it's almost certainly an extremely conservative estimate.

Do you know from these relationships you have with the families and former players if any of those players or all the players who had CTE also had symptoms they exhibited before their deaths?

Yes, unfortunately, nearly two-thirds of them had dementia while they were alive. So most of these were very severe, most of them were very impacted, and that's why their families reached out.

But we are also able to go back and get the national death records of every NFL player we didn't get. And the unfortunate thing is, we got far fewer than half of NFL players who died with of a neurodegenerative disease.

Obviously, this is resonating across a lot of people. But we did speak to a former NFL player today. That's Randy Grimes. He played in the league for nearly a decade. And we asked him about his reaction to the study earlier today. Here's what he had to say.

They're tested in the brains of former NFL players, and a lot of them had a lot of years in the league. But we're not testing the brains of high schoolers, people that played a couple of years in college. So I think there's a lot more research that needs to be done.

But at least we're talking about it and we're moving in that direction.

Dr. Nowinski, there are millions of people who played as young players in youth leagues, who played in high school, who played in college. What should we take away from your study about them?

Well, yes, I know Randy. He is a great guy, and he's helped us with supporting families. I guess he -- I do need to let him know, though, we are also studying college football players. We have studied nearly 400 of them, and we have studied more than 100 former high school players.

What we have shown through the study of people with different career lengths is a clear dose response relationship. And we need to really ramp up our investments in making this a curable disease in our lifetime.

I just want to put to you part of what the NFL sent to us in a statement in response, because they stress that they are continually striving to make football safer. They talk about implementing strategies to reduce concussions and head impacts. That includes new rules, new helmet technology.

We have made some modest changes to the game, and that's great. But also players are bigger, stronger, and faster than they have ever been, and they're starting younger, and seasons are longer. So I wouldn't be surprised if it's worse today or it peaked very recently.

## Constitutional law professor explains Supreme Court's ruling on mail-in voting

The U.S. Supreme Court issued an emergency order yesterday that allows the Trump administration to move forward for now with plans to restrict mail-in voting ahead of the midterm elections. It's a ruling opponents say would bring chaos to the election process only days before ballots in some states are mailed out.

Our White House correspondent, Liz Landers, has more.

The ruling allows the administration to proceed with an executive order President Trump signed in March directing the U.S. Postal Service to determine which voters should receive mail-in ballots. It also directs the Department of Homeland Security to build a database of U.S. citizens who are eligible to vote.

There are a lot of moving parts. And here to help explain it all is Steve Vladeck. He's a constitutional law professor at Georgetown University Law Center.

Steve, thank you for joining the program this evening.

Steve Vladeck, Georgetown University Law Center:

So there are two lawsuits that are challenging the president's executive order about mail-in ballots. The Supreme Court decision yesterday had to do with a group of states that sued, while the other case that was brought by the League of Women Voters.

What did the Supreme Court's ruling tell the states?

Yes, so the Supreme Court's ruling only applied, Liz, to the states' lawsuit, and it only said that the states had sued too early because they brought their lawsuit before the U.S. Postal Service issued their final rule, which, of course, they did last Friday. So it was, I think, a win for President Trump, but a very time-limited one.

It didn't say anything about whether the underlying executive order is lawful. It didn't say anything about whether the new Postal Service rule is lawful. That issue is now going to be litigated probably pretty quickly in both of the cases that are back before Judge Talwani in Boston.

OK, so let's talk about that executive order. There's another part of President Trump's E.O. that bars the Postal Service from delivering ballots to people who are not on federal lists of eligible voters. We're really more in the middle of the story, where there's going to be more litigation and where it's possible that these questions are back in the Supreme Court perhaps by this time next week.

You wrote a Substack post about this. And you wrote about the Purcell principle, which is this idea that election rules shouldn't be changed too close to an actual election because it confuses both voters and it makes it difficult for election administrators as well.

We're now 70 days from the midterm Election Day and some states like Minnesota are starting their absentee voting, which includes by mail, on September 18.

We spoke earlier today with Minnesota Secretary of State Steve Simon about how plausible it is to carry out the Postal Service rule change. Here's what he told us.

Steve Simon, Minnesota Secretary of State: I think there is serious doubt about whether there's enough time in the 2026 election to stand up this scheme. That has been imagined by this executive order. Keep in mind, they're talking about a whole new architecture here.

Or is it all going to be too late and this is really actually just a fight for what happens in the 2028 election cycle?

I think the most important takeaway from yesterday's ruling is that it doesn't actually resolve any of that and that we're going to have to see what happens in the coming days and perhaps weeks, whether this goes back to the Supreme Court, whether something happens to put this rule into effect that could very well upend a lot of mail-in voting, versus the status quo, which is what the rules were last time.

The Supreme Court ruling said in it -- quote -- "Time will tell if the president's executive order was lawful." I thought that was very interesting.

From what you have seen, how do you think the Supreme Court will rule and decide on that?

Yes, I mean, I think it'll be sooner, rather than later. I mean, I'd be very surprised if we haven't heard something more conclusive from the Supreme Court about whether this executive order and the rule implementing it can go into effect really, Liz, by the end of next week. It's going to move that quickly.

So I think they should for now, but I also think voters should be diligent, and voters should check in with their local election officials, with their secretaries of state, and voters should be watching the news, because, unfortunately, the reality of the times we live in is that confidence today could turn into instability tomorrow.

Steve Vladeck, thank you so much for your time this evening.

There's real-life consequences to scaring people and not relying on actual doctors and actual medical professionals to provide unbiased information to parents so that we can make reasonable decisions for our kids.

The governor added that he spoke with us Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who has pushed to revise the nation's approach to vaccines. Shapiro says he was -- quote -- "blunt" with him about the rhetoric coming from government officials.

A hearing is set for Thursday on a bid to stop Trump's name from being added to the center.

We have made clear on many occasions that China firmly opposes illegal unilateral sanctions that lack a basis in international law.

## Canadian trade minister says reaching deal with U.S. in best interest for both countries

And we turn now to Canada's trade representative, Dominic LeBlanc, a central figure in Canada's negotiations with the U.S.

Dominic LeBlanc, Canadian-U.S. Trade Minister:

Just days ago, you said the U.S. and Canada were very close to a trade agreement. Now the talks have fallen apart. The two countries are moving toward a trade war. You were at the negotiating table. What happened?

So, you're right. Last week, middle of the week, Wednesday, Thursday, we thought that we were close to an agreement. We -- President Trump had announced that there was a deal. We said that we were optimistic, we were positive, we were very close, as you said.

That was our view. And then, as we got into the final hours on Friday, President Trump had set a deadline of midnight Friday for a series of Section 338 tariffs at 50 percent to apply to almost $28 billion of Canadian exports to the United States. That was the -- sort of the rush, the intensity to try and get the agreement.

In the end, on that Friday evening, there were sort of three or four significant issues. What was included in the auto tariff reductions? How do we treat steel and aluminum derivative products? Big economic issues for us. And we weren't able to get to a landing spot that Prime Minister Carney thought was in Canada's economic interest.

So, at that point, we weren't able to conclude the deal.

The U.S. trade representative, Jamieson Greer, said that the U.S. offered to do the following, reduce a 25 percent tariff on cars that President Trump implemented last year, which you mentioned, reduce the tariff on steel from 50 percent to 25 percent for the majority of steel that Canada sends to the U.S., and reduce all aluminum tariffs from 50 percent to 25 percent.

What about that was insufficient, in your view?

First of all, we started the conversation from a premise that the USMCA agreement that President Trump had signed in his first term had zero tariffs in those sectors. So the U.S. government unilaterally decided in the 232 tariffs to impose on key sectors of our economy, steel, aluminum automobiles, our case, softwood lumber as well.

These same sort of economic engines that President Trump is promoting in the United States are critical to our economy. And the challenge for us is, those are the sectors over the last number of decades where we have become the most integrated with the United States. Automobiles is the best example.

So, 50 percent of the cars made in Canada are made with United -- with American parts. We're the biggest customer. We buy more American vehicles than a whole bunch of other countries combined. We have an integrated economy. At one point, President Trump decided to apply these tariffs.

So, if there was a different tariff treatment for something as important to Canada as auto parts or medium- and heavy-duty trucks, then you can see that Prime Minister Carney just concluded that the economics of that didn't work for the Canadian industry. That's just one example, but it's an instructive one.

Well, given the enormous amount of integration between the U.S. and Canadian economies, is it even possible for both countries to engage in a trade war without inflicting major damage on both countries' economies?

Well, and that has been exactly our concern. So we certainly didn't seek this particular trade conflict. We were hopeful that we could find an agreement that would be in the economic interest of both countries, respecting Canadian sovereignty, respecting critical parts of our economy, just as President Trump wants to build and protect those parts of the American economy.

We still believe that there's an agreement possible that is in the best interest of both countries, that -- for example, in steel, we have been working with the United States on stopping non-market economies from dumping steel into the North American economy.

We -- Canada was the first country to put in melt and pour rules around where was the steel melted and poured to qualify as Canadian steel in our case. So there are many common objectives.

You say a deal is still possible, even as Canada is preparing to retaliate dollar for dollar. What sectors will Canada target?

So we're obviously looking to protect the same sectors that -- of our economy that are facing this latest round of 338 -- Section 338 tariffs at 50 percent. So there's steel products. There's paper products. There are aluminum derivative products.

I mean, these are key parts of our economy that have exported to the United States and we have imported from the United States in a tariff-free context for decades. So we're going to make sure that there's not an unfair advantage for American exporters sending goods to Canada.

The prime minister said over the weekend that the U.S. is using economic integration as a weapon, that was the phrase that he used, and that America's signature is now, as he said, written in pencil.

That sounds like something much bigger than a dispute over tariff rates. Are we witnessing right now a permanent rupture in the U.S.-Canada relationship?

The U.S.-Canada relationship is so broad-based. There's a critical economic partnership that's existed for decades. There's a security and defense relationship that has also seen us stand shoulder to shoulder with the United States in global conflicts for decades.

It's also a very personal relationship. I think, for Americans to understand that, my perspective, there -- almost every Canadian has some personal connection to the United States. In my case, I went to university there. I have five first cousins that live in Massachusetts.

Many Canadians own property in the United States, go on holidays in the United States, have business relationships. So, there's a deep personal connection. I represent a district on the east coast of Canada, just sort of in a province that borders the state of Maine. These small border towns in Canada and in Maine, in New Brunswick on our side and in Maine, share fire departments in the case of a major fire.

The fire trucks literally cross the border in these small towns to help each other out. So this is a deep, deep relationship built on years and years of common interest and goodwill. So I'm not pessimistic. We're into some economic turbulence and headwind. But we will navigate. We will take our responsibilities as a government of Canada to do what we have to do.

But I remain convinced that it's an enduring relationship that is that is very, very broad-based.

Dominic LeBlanc, Canada's minister in charge of U.S. trade, thank you for joining us this evening, sir.

Thanks for having me on your program. Have a great evening.

And a note, the "News Hour" has requested an interview with the U.S. trade representative, and we hope to bring you that interview soon.

## Canada retaliates with tariffs against U.S. as relations deteriorate

The tariffs will hit a range of U.S. products, from industrial goods to everyday items. At the same time, President Trump is threatening Canada with even steeper tariffs.

In Ottawa today, Canada's government had a clear message: It's firing back.

Francois-Philippe Champagne, Canadian Finance Minister:

Canada will meet the moment. Canadians will meet the moment. We will meet the moment together. We will support our workers, our businesses and our industry with whatever it takes for as long as it takes.

Starting September 8, Canada will impose or raise tariffs ranging between 15 and 50 percent, affecting $27 billion worth of imports, and that will hit over 700 U.S. products, including some key U.S. imports.

Francois-Philippe Champagne (through interpreter):

Our response will specifically target products in the sectors of steel, aluminum, household appliances, farming equipment, paper products, and electronics.

Canada's retaliation follows the Trump administration placing 50 percent tariffs on roughly $20 billion worth of Canadian imports on Saturday, along with threats of more to come. President Trump said he will impose 50 percent on steel and autos starting next year.

And this clash between allies has gone beyond goods. President Trump wrote on social media that the U.S. doesn't expect to do business with the Canadian border province of Ontario, adding -- quote -- "The United States is giving serious consideration to changing the name of Lake Ontario to Lake America."

To that, one Canadian official simply said today:

Melanie Joly, Canadian Industry Minister:

We will always call it Lake Ontario, period.

But for all the rhetoric, Trump last night did acknowledge the U.S. relies on Canada for some key products like aluminum.

You know, selfishly, we need aluminum in this country. We don't have it. We get it all from Canada for the most part, and we need it badly.

President Trump has long lamented the trade deficit between the two countries, which stood at nearly $50 billion last year. But for a trading relationship as big as the U.S. and Canada's, these tariffs still hit just a fraction of the roughly $800 billion of commerce between the two neighbors, which U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer has echoed.

Jamieson Greer, U.S. Trade Representative:

At the end of the day, this is 5 percent of imports from Canada. It's 0.06 percent of our overall consumption in this economy. So there's no possible way it can really affect U.S. well-being.

Still, for states like Michigan, which trades heavily with the nation to its north, this trade war is already having consequences.

Michael Howard says his furniture business has started losing clients.

Michael Howard II, Howard Family Designs:

With a 50 percent tariff, all those costs have skyrocketed, and our -- just that one project has cost our family thousands of dollars in profit, and which impacts the ability for us to put food on the table for our family.

On the U.S.-Canadian border, where $2.5 billion in goods cross every day, the costs to citizens on both sides continue to grow.
