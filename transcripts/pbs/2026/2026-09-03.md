## European countries confront Russian sabotage and shadow warfare

Today, German police arrested two people suspected of attacking a weapons manufacturer in Munich. The incident comes after Germany officially blamed the Russian government for another kind of sabotage attack last month, when armed drones came close to downing an aircraft as it landed in Leipzig.

European officials say these so-called hybrid attacks are part of a wave of sabotage by Russia targeting members of NATO.

In the distance, an electricity substation explosion and what German officials fear is another Russian provocation. A swarm of police descend on the scene. A phalanx of officers comb fields for evidence and find 10 other bombs. It's the second such incident in two days, and German officials say it was no accident.

Herbert Reul, Interior Minister, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany (through interpreter):

Let me be clear. This was not a faulty switch. It was deliberate. It is a criminal offense, sabotage directed by a foreign state or left-wing extremism. We cannot rule out either possibility.

The apparent Russian sabotage threat became very real early last month, when, in the Leipzig Airport, a bomb squad found drones with military-grade explosives, the suspected target, a Ukrainian cargo plane.

But authorities also said another drone exploded next to a plane as it landed. This week, Germany cast blame officially.

Alexander Dobrindt, German Interior Minister (through interpreter):

We assume that Germany is the target of further hybrid attacks by Russia. Let me make one thing clear. We are not at war. But we are a daily target of hybrid warfare.

European officials accuse Russia of years of hybrid warfare, from attempted arson outside the British prime minister's home, to firebombing warehouses in Latvia storing aid for Ukraine, to an assassination plot against a German weapons manufacturer, as well as drones that cross into NATO territory and have destroyed property.

But Russian President Vladimir Putin calls all the accusations fiction.

Vladimir Putin, Russian President (through interpreter):

It is unclear why all these accusations are being made. Yet there is one explanation, the need to counter an aggressive Russia. But where is the evidence? Well, they have planted the evidence.

Germany is Ukraine's largest European benefactor. European officials say it's no coincidence Moscow's reported sabotage targets Kyiv supporters. Germany vows it will close a Russian consulate and seek more sanctions. But collective European punishment is still under debate.

Kaja Kallas, High Representative, European Union:

It is clear that it has all the hallmarks of state-sponsored terrorism. The question is, what do we do about this?

Also today, Denmark's National Security and Intelligence Service accused Russia of recruiting Danish citizens for sabotage against Danish defense firms with ties to Ukraine.

For perspective on all this, we turn to David Cattler, a former senior member of the U.S. intelligence community and former assistant secretary-general for intelligence and security at NATO from 2019 to 2023. He's now a senior fellow at the Center for European Policy Analysis.

Thanks very much, David. Welcome back to the "News Hour."

David Cattler, Former U.S. Intelligence Official:

It is very rare that Germany actually calls Russia out on one of these sabotage attempts. How significant is the calling out and what does it say about the seriousness of the Leipzig Airport incident?

Well, I think it's very significant that the German government has taken this step now to publicly attribute a broader campaign, not just the attack on Leipzig, to Moscow, saying that they are orchestrating and directing these sorts of hybrid attacks.

I think it also matters because a hybrid attack doesn't mean that the attack is harmless. And just because the attack failed doesn't mean that there aren't and shouldn't be consequences for it. There's almost certainly a lot of intelligence in the background here.

But the fact that they have taken this step is a -- it's a big deal. What we need to wait and see now, though, is what consequences come. And does that actually provide the deterrent effect that Berlin and NATO and other allies wish to have happen?

Before I ask about the consequences, let's talk about the attacks themselves. Are they increasing in number? Are they increasing in location? And does the Leipzig attack especially indicate that Russia is willing to take more risks when it comes to the sabotage?

So, yes to all of those things. I think where you end it is exactly the key point for people to understand, that what you're seeing is an increase in the pace. You're seeing an increase in the scope and in the range of activities.

You have the Leipzig attack. You have arson, sabotage, murder, attacks on critical infrastructure also. And Russia has more than one service likely engaged in this campaign. You have military intelligence in the GRU, and you have the FSB that's sort of a combination of the CIA and the FBI.

And if they're not checked, if they're not deterred, you should expect to see some tests of what the risk tolerance is of the victim also.

How far can they push before they provoke a response?

European officials tell me Putin is trying to test what he can get away with. He's trying to test NATO resilience. He's trying to undermine support for Ukraine, perhaps all three. Why do you think Russia is apparently launching these attacks?

Well, all of those reasons, and I think, at the strategic level, he's really trying to accomplish two things. One is to break support for Ukraine.

So let's go and try to conduct an arson attack at a weapons depot, or let's try to assassinate the CEO of a major German arms company that's made statements and is investing not just in increased arms production, but direct cooperation with Ukraine. And, at the same time, that can support an even broader goal, which is to break solidarity within Europe and within NATO, to try to change the security architecture for Europe as a whole.

And if he can accomplish those two things through these activities without a direct conventional attack on NATO, all the better, I think, from Moscow's perspective.

And I should mark, of course, Russian authorities deny any involvement in this, and we heard Vladimir Putin himself saying this is all fiction.

The German response, as you brought up before, to Leipzig was to close the Russian consulate in Bonn, terminate its agreement with the Russian House cultural center in Berlin, and summon Russia's ambassador.

Is that sufficient to create that deterrence that you described before?

Well, we will see, right? Because, again, that's -- those are the steps that Germany says publicly they're prepared to take. They may be taking other steps in private also. And other nations likely will take actions in solidarity with Berlin.

But I think it's too -- important for people to understand, when you hear things like we're going to close a consulate, we're going to close a cultural center, you think, OK, it's no big deal. But there have been longstanding allegations about Russian activities conducted from those facilities, signals intelligence, human intelligence collection, influence operations.

But even does -- forgive me for interrupting.

But does kicking out some intelligence agents really deter Russia?

It might not deter them, but it makes them -- it makes the operations more difficult to conduct. They have to do them remotely, might have to use the Internet, communications, that sort of thing.

I had the Latvian foreign minister, Baiba Braze, on the show last week, someone you worked with at NATO. And she said that while European intelligence services are indeed concerned about these sabotage attacks, and the attacks themselves might escalate, there's no immediate military threat to NATO, because Russia is bogged down in Ukraine.

I think it's right that we don't see signs, I don't see signs of Russian preparation for a conventional attack on a NATO ally.

I think, in that case, the deterrence and the defensive capabilities that NATO has arrayed are sufficient to convince Moscow that the cost of that attack would be too high. But they're doing this hybrid campaign. And they are trying through the hybrid campaign to achieve the same results that they might have sought through a conventional attack.

The risk there is too high. So they have decided to go in a different direction.

## Steinem stayed true to herself despite intense pressure, Julie Taymor says

For more now on the life and legacy of Gloria Steinem, we're joined by Julie Taymor. She's director of the 2020 film "The Glorias," a biopic that spanned eight decades of Steinem's life.

Julie, welcome to the "News Hour." Thanks for being with us.

So I saw you smiling there as you were hearing Gloria's voice and listening to her answers.

I just want to start with your reaction to the news. What did you think when you heard the news that she had passed?

Oh, well we knew she wasn't well, but it's such a terrible passing. We need her right now. We need that voice. And I'm glad we're bringing out the film right now again, because you have to understand this woman was an extraordinary pioneer. She really was.

And she was humble all the time, wasn't -- she never wanted her face on the cover of a magazine. People would steal it and put it there. She really -- she said things like: "Without me, there would still be a woman's movement."

She really was just an extraordinary person, a friend, and an inspiration for men and women.

You once said, "I thought I knew her, but I didn't," when you began working on this film.

What do you think people might not have understood about Gloria Steinem? What did you learn in telling her story?

Well, I know the inner fears. I know what she went through when she had to keep that power face on.

Even her little laugh at the end of things that she says for people when she's critical, she had to navigate that territory that many women do, especially leaders, where they don't want to come on too strong, but they have something strong to say and to do.

I also think the joy and what she and the other women of the time of her period of them working together was such a lesson that people have to see. Most movies, they're about women who want to get a husband or have to get a divorce or dealing with an affair or clawing at each other.

And here was a woman and other women who really, really had a love affair with each other as they were working to make it better for all women all over the world. I didn't know things about how she had learned about the talking circles in India.

I mean, there were many things in the book, but one of the things that was so great with her when I was making this movie was her openness to me doing things that were surreal, her inner journey, her inner story. She was so supportive of me being the film director I am, dealing with real material in a -- sometimes with documentary, but sometimes in a surreal way, instead of saying, oh, I don't think that way.

She was extremely generous, had a great sense of humor, was gorgeous, and that didn't stop her. You know, people always would say things like that. But that was what was great about her, that she had a sense of style and knew how to simplify her dress. And yet people loved her for how she was her. She stayed true to herself.

And she navigated so much, including, as we noted in that report from Jeff Brown, criticism from other women that were in part of that feminist movement. You saw some of the concerns from people like Alice Walker, bell hooks, prominent Black leaders and thinkers who -- Alice Walker even leaving "Ms." magazine for the lack of diversity there.

How did Gloria take that criticism and how did she handle it moving forward?

Well, it's one of the scenes in the movie when a Black journalist says, how do you respond to the women's move -- the Black women's movement saying that the white women's movement does not listen -- does not include them?

And she said: "I wouldn't say anything. I'd listen."

Now, the thing is that Gloria Steinem always had a Black partner, whether it was Flo Kennedy or Dorothy Pitman Hughes. She was always working with women of various locales. Dorothy, what was it -- Wilma Mankiller, the Cherokee chief, they were best friends. This is the most important friend that she had who was an extraordinary woman, or Dolores Huerta, who I spoke to when I was doing this film.

But I heard that Alice Walker was a very close friend of Gloria. So that might have been a period of time right there on "Ms." She wasn't running "Ms." by herself. She was she was always part of a collaborative team. But I know they were friends.

And Betty Friedan is an interesting thing. I didn't put her physically in the movie, but I had Bella Abzug played, oh, God, Bette Midler -- perfect -- say you have to basically confront Betty Friedan. And she says, I'm not going to do that. If she wants to be the head of the National Women's Caucus, let her do it. And they said, no.

So she wasn't really ever seeking conflict. But she had to confront it sometimes. And there -- obviously, there would be competition, like there's competition among men. So it's not like every -- that women are going to be pristine and can get away with what's not essentially human.

Julie, in the minute or so I have left, I have to ask you because you were with her in a couple of key moments, election night in 2016. You were there with cameras. You thought that you would witness her witnessing the first woman president in America, and then you followed her instead on the Women's March that followed that election.

What did you learn from her and take away from her in those more fraught moments?

Yes, well, at -- we were at Samantha Power's apartment, the ambassador to the U.N., and there were 40 women from different countries there, and you watched the night go on as each of these women, these ambassadors, knew that it would be difficult for them, for the women in their countries.

The last person in that room that night before she went over to see Hillary was Gloria Steinem. And these were her words: "We have to look at the upside of the downside."

And if there was any haiku for her spirit, that's how I -- that's what I get from her, which is always looking to, OK, don't be defeated. Do. Don't -- you have to act.

And then we did. We took the documentary crew to Washington, D.C. It's in my film. The film has four Glorias, plus the fifth, the real one. And to listen to her speak at that and say that this is what democracy is about it, it makes you cry right now. It actually -- I haven't seen it today. I saw it this summer. It was being screened.

And I hope it gets screened now so people can really see her in action and understand her full life. But she was a positive energy, positive force, and not just for women. This is the thing. It was about relationships between men and women, how to live in an equal society.

Julie, I know it's a tough day for you. We can't thank you enough for taking the time to join us, Julie Taymor.

## Remembering Gloria Steinem and her trailblazing fight for women's rights

Gloria Steinem, the pioneering journalist and fierce activist who was an icon of the women's liberation movement, has died at the age of 92.

For more than half-a-century, Steinem challenged entrenched ideas about gender and power, helping bring the fight for women's equality from the margins of American life into the mainstream.

For more than five decades, Gloria Steinem was one of the most prominent faces of American feminism. A trailblazing journalist, political activist, and public intellectual, she helped lead a movement that revolutionized both the professional and personal lives of women across the country.

Gloria Steinem, Journalist and Activist: We have marched for every other movement that exists, and we worked for everything else except ourselves. And now to see women supporting other women and standing up for their own rights is really very, very moving and gratifying.

Born in Toledo, Ohio, in 1934, Steinem overcame a tumultuous childhood, which included her parents' divorce and taking care of her mother for many years. She graduated from Smith College in the late 1950s and soon moved to New York to begin a career as a freelance journalist.

As a young writer, she famously went undercover as a Playboy Bunny for "Show" magazine, exposing exploitation and sexual harassment.

You had to have an internal physical exam, which they told us was a requirement of the food servers in New York state.

She became a columnist for "New York" magazine and established herself as a leader of so-called second-wave feminism with thoughtful and often pointed commentary. She also covered the fight for abortion rights, a topic that hit close to home, as she told the "News Hour" in 2019.

I would have had a profoundly different life if I had been responsible for rearing a child. I had had an abortion in England. It allowed me to live my own life, not to marry someone for whom I would have been the wrong wife and he would have been the wrong husband.

She also said the fight for reproductive rights was a catalyst for her own career in activism.

And I began to see what, of course, the radical feminists were saying in the first place, which was that it is a caste problem and that women are an oppressed group. And then, finally, I understood that, even though I was not married living in the suburbs, that it was a problem that affected absolutely every woman.

In the early '70s, Steinem co-founded "Ms." magazine, a radical break from traditional women's publications. With a masthead made up entirely of women, it tackled topics like sexism, harassment, and equal pay, and became a clearinghouse for some of the biggest names of the women's liberation movement.

It started, in desperation, I think, because there were just a great many women writers and editors who didn't feel that they were working on magazines that they read and that they had the opportunity to really be honest about their own experiences in a magazine.

Shirley Chisholm has not got the depth and breadth of the coverage she deserves.

As a political activist, she worked to elect women to office and was a co-founder of the National Women's Political Caucus, traveling the country, speaking out against sexism and economic exploitation.

It's the kind of blue-collar working woman who needs these reforms the most, I think, and also poor women and welfare women. But it's -- I think it really is going forward on all on all levels at once, and it's an important bridge between black women and white women.

She remained a prolific writer, a bestselling author, and prominent public speaker for much of her long life, advocating for women's rights internationally, opposing apartheid, and campaigning prominently for Democrats, including Hillary Clinton.

Along with others in the movement, she helped solidify gains that many these days may take for granted. Still, when she spoke with Judy Woodruff in 2020, she said she felt it much remained unfinished.

We have the right to vote. We still don't fully have the right to our own bodies. Many women are still subject to bodily invasion at a rate far exceeding that of men. I think we have yet to understand the rights that the female half of the human race need as females.

She condemned the Dobbs decision in 2022, which overturned abortion rights.

I'm old enough to remember before there were any real abortion rights. Nonetheless, about one in three American women had had an abortion at some time in her life even when it was maybe not legal.

And as she told Amna in 2020, she felt each person could be part of the fight for equality.

I just want to say that we should stop asking passive questions. I mean, it's not what will happen, what should happen, what -- it's, what am I going to -- what are we going to do? And that means tomorrow morning what are we going to do?

Steinem, who in her trademark aviator glasses became nearly synonymous with women's liberation in the media, was not without her critics.

In the late '80s, black feminist Alice Walker resigned as a contributor from "Ms." Magazine, citing a lack of diversity on staff and in its pages. And Steinem had a major falling out with Betty Friedan, herself a towering figure in the movement and an erstwhile ally.

They battled over many issues, including Steinem's critiques of men and traditional marriage, which Friedan could be counterproductive. Through it all, Steinem emphasized she was just one part of a much larger struggle, as she said before receiving the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2013.

I'm grateful to have the opportunity to say here that I would be crazy if I didn't understand that this was a medal for the entire women's movement.

Over the years, Steinem dated a number of prominent men, including director Mike Nichols. She married briefly later in life but had no children.

Today, tributes poured in from across the globe, including from Oprah Winfrey, Jill Biden, and Hillary Clinton, who called her one of the architects of the entire structure of women's equality.

In a 2015 interview for her memoir, I asked what advice she'd give young women today.

Here's my advice. Do not listen to me, really.

No. I want to support their listening to themselves. Each of them has things that they love, things that they do that they love so much. They know their own life and situations. I'm here to support that and say, you're not -- OK, you're not more important than somebody else, but you're not less important either.
