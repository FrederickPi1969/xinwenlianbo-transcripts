## How France is leveraging a lottery to finance historic preservation

The Palace of Versailles, the Chartres Cathedral, the Eiffel Tower, France is home to a number of famous monuments and structures. But it also has a big and growing problem protecting and preserving its lesser-known sites.

Jeffrey Brown reports from France on an experiment in raising new funds for that purpose.

It's part of Canvas, our ongoing series on arts and culture.

An old mansion in the suburbs of Paris, home to a once world-famous 19th century French artist named Rosa Bonheur, best known for her paintings of animals, and, says Katherine Brault, who bought the chateau two years ago, it remains largely as Bonheur left it.

All the objects here are Rosa Bonheur's belongings. They have never left the castle. It's not like in a museum, where objects are placed to show how it was before. Here, things are real. That's what is unique.

Rosa Bonheur was a pioneer, the first woman to receive the Grand Cross from the French Legion of Honor, a proto-feminist who chose how she would live and what she would do.

And, in her time, she was hugely successful. Her works are in the collection of places like the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

This is the costume Buffalo Bill gives to Rosa Bonheur.

But now her home is falling apart, and Brault wants to preserve it and the legacy of the artist.

We have to act very quickly. The problem is significant. If we wait a year or two, it's over. We won't be able to restore anymore.

It's just one small example of a phenomenon across France, small historical sites suffering decades, even centuries of neglect.

And with so many projects requiring so much to protect and restore them, the country has turned to a different model for raising money: a so-called heritage lottery, in which everyday citizens purchase tickets, at two price points running around $3 or $16, to try to win big.

Most of the money goes to the pot. Winners can take home more than 1.5 million in dollars. But a portion also goes to a different pot, aimed at restoring threatened heritage sites across the country.

Why do you visit France? It's not because we are very charming. It is not because we speak very good English. Look at me. And it's because we have a lot of treasures to visit.

The program is the brainchild of Stephane Bern, a popular television and radio personality, who modeled it on a similar program in Great Britain.

Bern wants us to see beyond France's most famous, well-cared for sites, to the thousands of smaller ones that receive little or no government funding.
