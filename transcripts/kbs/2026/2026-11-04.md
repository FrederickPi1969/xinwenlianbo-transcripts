## Proportion of Economically Inactive Population Aged 60 and Older Reaches All-Time High

The proportion of individuals aged 60 and older within the economically inactive population has reached an all-time high, nearing 50 percent.
The Ministry of Data and Statistics released a report on Wednesday titled “Survey on the Economically Inactive Population and Non-Wage Earners,” based on August 2024 data.
According to the report, as of August this year, the economically inactive population stood at 16-point-three million, up 77-thousand from a year earlier.
The “economically inactive population” consists of individuals aged 15 and older who are neither employed nor actively seeking employment.
As of August, that segment of the population made up 35-point-four percent of the total — the same as the previous year, and the lowest level recorded since the survey began in 1999.
Economically inactive people accounted for 29-point-four percent of those aged 70 and older, 19 percent of those in their 60s, and 13-point-one percent of those aged between 15 and 19.
Among the economically inactive population, the number of people “taking a break” fell for the first time in four years.
The number of people “taking a break,” defined as those without serious illness or disability who simply want to rest, stood at two-point-55 million, down 89-thousand from a year earlier, reversing three straight years of a steady climb.

## S. Korea Sees Autumn Whiplash: October Ranks as Fifth Warmest on Record

Anchor: This past October, South Korea saw unusual autumn temperatures, with daytime highs above 30 degrees Celsius before temperatures plunged, and frequent rainfall. The Korea Meteorological Administration says last month was the fifth-warmest October on record, and it points to El Niño as one of the drivers.
Report: The Korea Meteorological Administration said Wednesday that the average temperature nationwide this October was fifteen-point-eight degrees Celsius.
That’s one-point-five degrees above the long-term October average, and the fifth highest since nationwide records began in 1973.
On October 12, Seoul’s daytime high hit thirty-point-one degrees, the highest October temperature ever recorded in the capital.
However, temperatures swung wildly as warm and cold air took turns moving in.
The nationwide daily average fell ten degrees in four days, then rose seven degrees, then dropped nearly nine degrees again by the 28th.
Precipitation totaled 108-point-five millimeters, about one-point-seven times the October average.
Rain fell on an average of eight-point-six days, compared with a normal five-point-nine.
The weather agency says El Niño had an effect, strengthening high pressure southeast of the nation, which fed warm, humid air northward.
That air collided with cold air from the north, forming rain clouds.
Even so, the agency says the number of meteorological drought days in October was one of the highest on record.
The drought index is based on six months of accumulated rainfall.
Looking ahead, this year’s El Niño has already broken records and become a “super El Niño.” In September, ocean temperatures in the key monitoring zone in the Pacific ran two-point-five degrees above average.
[Sound bite: Dr. Axel Timmermann, Director of the IBS Center for Climate Physics]
"I think we should see that event a little bit like a postcard from the future. In many areas, things will get worse. This is almost a projection of what's going to happen long-term and more persistently as a result of global warming."
The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration in the United States says the El Niño event will most likely peak around December and could last into March.
The Korea Meteorological Administration expects a warmer, wetter winter in South Korea, but after an autumn of whiplash weather, forecasters say the winter ahead could be just as unpredictable.

## Special Counsel Raids NEC in Ballot Shortage Case on Allegations that Officials Altered Voting Logs

The special counsel team looking into the shortage of ballots during the June 3 local elections raided the National Election Commission(NEC) and its Seoul office on Wednesday.
The team, led by special counsel Lee Tae-han, sent prosecutors and investigators to the two locations after coming across evidence that the election watchdog forged or altered voting logs.
A voting log is an important official document that records how voting was conducted at a polling station on Election Day, and how ballots were managed and distributed.
Such logs must be sealed after the vote count is complete and preserved until the end of the elected official’s term.
The special counsel team says ballots without serial numbers were distributed and used in some 140 polling stations on June 3 due to the ballot shortage.
Under the Public Official Election Act, ballots must have serial numbers printed on them.
The team believes the NEC, to avoid being held liable or sued for using such ballots, instructed its regional offices to arbitrarily enter serial numbers for them in the voting logs.
The team estimates that such modifications to voting logs were made in at least 40 of the 140-something stations that had received ballots without serial numbers.
The latest allegation is one that had not surfaced during an earlier investigation into the ballot shortage, conducted jointly by the prosecution and police.
