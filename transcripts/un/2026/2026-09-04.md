4 September 2026
Daily Press Briefing by the Office of the Spokesperson for the Secretary-General
The following is a near-verbatim transcript of today’s briefing by Stéphane Dujarric, Spokesman for the Secretary-General.
Programming Note
Alright.  Just a reminder, Monday is Labor Day here in the US.  So, the office will be closed; there will be no Noon Briefing that day. However, if you need to reach us, we will always be on call for you.  We will return on Tuesday.  We will return here on Tuesday with the briefing.
Also on Tuesday, it will be handover day for the Presidents of the General Assembly and we expect a stakeout by the new President of the General Assembly, Dr. Khalilur Rahman of Bangladesh; that will be probably around noon, as well, on Tuesday, but we will coordinate so we don’t clash with the briefing.  The incoming President of the General Assembly will deliver brief remarks on his vision for the eighty-first session, and he will take a couple of questions.  And that will be at the GA Stakeout area, in what you know as the East Foyer.  Given that this is going to be a long weekend, unfortunately I have a few notes for you today.
Sudan
A number of you have been asking me about the activities of Pekka Haavisto, the Secretary-General’s Personal Envoy for Sudan. Mr. Haavisto, I can tell you, arrived in Khartoum today.  There, he will engage key stakeholders on ways to advance measures to de-escalate violence and strengthen the protection of civilians.  Yesterday, he spoke by phone to General Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo, otherwise known as Hemedti, the Commander of the Rapid Support Forces to discuss those same issues.
On Tuesday, 8 September, the Personal Envoy will be joined in Khartoum by our partners from the Quintet, which as you know brings together the African Union, European Union, IGAD — the Intergovernmental Authority on Development — and the League of Arab States, for a series of meetings.  They will have exchanges with key stakeholders on the current political landscape, including recent initiatives and proposals to advance an intra-Sudanese dialogue.
We continue to emphasize the importance of a negotiated settlement in this conflict that reflects the democratic aspirations of the Sudanese people.  Mr. Haavisto will continue to engage with a broad range of Sudanese actors, from both inside and outside of the country, to identify the best ways to support a broad-based Sudanese-led and Sudanese-owned process, that is inclusive and also comprehensive political process to achieve lasting peace in Sudan.
Nepal
Moving to Nepal:  A bit earlier this morning in Nepal, we and our humanitarian partners launched a nearly $50 million Flash Appeal to support the emergency response following the recent catastrophic flash floods and landslides.  The four-month appeal aims to help about 84,000 people who have been impacted by the landslides.
Our humanitarian colleagues tell us that survivors face tragic losses of family members, loss of homes and loss of livelihoods.  Widespread destruction of critical infrastructure has severed transport routes, disrupted power grids and washed-out local water supply and sanitation systems, leaving isolated populations without basic necessities. The UN Resident Coordinator in Nepal, Lila Pieters Yahia, said that the appeal, under the leadership of the Government, will help get cash, shelter, healthcare, food, clean water and protection to some of the hardest-to-reach communities.
Meanwhile, we continue to support the response.  Our colleagues at the World Food Programme (WFP), in the first week after the disaster, dispatched food for about 8,000 people and procured a further 180 metric tons of food locally.  WFP is also standing by with air support, including helicopters and unmanned aircraft, all that to deliver food and other supplies to people in the most isolated areas.
And the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) tells us that this morning a flight carrying 84 metric tons of critical humanitarian supplies landed at Kathmandu in Nepal, with the support of the European Union Humanitarian Aid. UNICEF is also dispatching supplies to some of the hardest-hit areas.  The supplies from UNICEF will help families access safe water, strengthen disease prevention, support children's learning and ensure front-line health and nutrition workers have the tools to identify and assist vulnerable children and mothers.
Ukraine
Turning to Ukraine, the Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) colleagues in Ukraine tell us that residents of Ukraine’s largest cities, including Kyiv and Odesa, have faced repeated attacks over the past two days.  We and our humanitarian partners continueto support the response despite damage in our own assets.
Our humanitarian colleagues note that several hours ago, a drone struck an area in central Kyiv near the Saint Sophia Cathedral, which — as a reminder — is a UNESCO [United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization] World Heritage Site.  We, along with our humanitarian partners, responded rapidlyto provide first aid, psychosocial support and emergency supplies to repair damaged homes in the Kherson area and also in Kyiv.
We continue to support front-line areas.  Today, a humanitarian convoy delivered vital aid to the hardest-hit communities in the Donetsk and Kherson.  The supplies included hygiene and first-aid kits, along with essential medicines and medical supplies for local hospitals.
Humanitarian operations have also been impacted by recent attacks.  On Tuesday, a UNICEF-contracted water truck, carrying drinking water to children and families in Kharkiv, was struck, seriously injuring the driver.  On the same day, an office of the Ukrainian [Red Cross Society] in Kherson was destroyed.  And on Wednesday, the World Health Organization (WHO) said that an air attack in Kyiv oblast impacted one of WHO’s main warehouses with humanitarian medical supplies.  WHO stressed that it remains committed to delivering life-saving aid to support people across Ukraine.
And Matthias Schmale, our Humanitarian Coordinator for Ukraine, just returned from a field visit to the Sumy region, which borders the Russian Federation.  He called on the international community to continue supporting Ukraine, including in hard-to-reach areas.
Yemen
Turning to Yemen, I’ve been asked about various reports concerning the recent sharp military escalation in various parts of that country. I can tell you that we saw these reports, and we are deeply concerned by the ongoing military clashes across multiple front lines in Yemen, including around Taiz.  We call on the Yemeni parties to exercise restraint, de-escalate tensions and refrain from actions that could lead to further military escalation which risks devastating political, economic and security ramifications for Yemen and all of its people.  The parties must prioritize dialogue and engage constructively with United Nations-led efforts toward a negotiated political settlement.
Lebanon
Turning to Lebanon:  Today, our Humanitarian Coordinator there, Imran Riza, visited Nabatieh in southern Lebanon.  There, he met with communities receiving many returnees and displaced people.  Authorities outlined the challenges that people returning to their homes face, including damage to vital infrastructure impacting basic services like water and electricity.
As an example, the Village of Mayfadoun, where Mr. Riza visited.  There, damage to a water well station, including solar panels and pumping equipment, has affected water supply in parts of the village.  He stressed the importance of early recovery efforts to restore these essential services, to support municipalities and help communities recover.
OCHA says that the 2026 Flash Appeal, which we launched, as you will recall, back in March, wrapped up last month.  Despite being less than 52 per cent funded, with only $330 million, our partners reached more than 1.2 million people with at least one form of critical assistance since the escalations began.  This includes more than 900,000 people who received cash assistance, over half a million people with food and agriculture support, and nearly 556,000 with health assistance.
Yet, as Emergency Relief Coordinator Tom Fletcher stressed, the needs continue, access remains constrained and many families still cannot return home safely.  To support people in need of critical humanitarian assistance, our partners will update the Lebanon Response Plan to cover the period from this month through the end of the year.  We reiterate the need for continued humanitarian access and support to people in need, including those who are trying to return home.
Occupied Palestinian Territory
And moving south to the Occupied Palestinian Territory, I can tell you that looking at what is going out on the ground, we remain deeply concerned about the safety of civilians in both the Gaza Strip and the West Bank.  Meanwhile, we and our humanitarian partners on the ground continue supporting people’s preparations for the rainy season, although more funding and access are urgently needed for that support anywhere to be sufficient.
Yesterday, the Director-General of WHO, Dr. Tedros [Adhanom Ghebreyesus], said that public health risks are growing as winter approaches, with most people still displaced in overcrowded tented sites.  Dr. Tedros warned that Gaza’s already overwhelmed health system cannot withstand additional surges in disease and illness. With the onset of rains, flooding is likely to spread sewage and contaminated water through densely populated areas, increasing the risk of disease.  He called for increased access to lab and diagnostic supplies that remain restricted, as well as by essential medicines, rehabilitation equipment and public health.
Turning to the West Bank, our humanitarian colleagues say that since 2023, more than 6,400 Palestinians have been displaced in 130 communities by settler violence.  Of those displaced, more than [2,500] people were forced out this year alone, almost half of them children.  That has left 48 Palestinian communities fully displaced since 2023, including a dozen just this year.  Our partners warn that the cumulative impact of repeated displacement, recurrent settler attacks, damage to homes and infrastructure and restrictions on access to land and essential services is deepening humanitarian needs.
Syria
Moving to Syria, I wanted to flag the dangers facing civilians from explosive remnants of war remain deeply concerning, particularly for children.  Our OCHA colleagues tell us that, over the past week, a dozen explosive ordnance incidents were recorded across 6 Syrian governorates, killing 9 people, including 6 children, and injuring 13 others.
The deadliest incident occurred in Deir ez-Zor on Monday, when a landmine detonated on a civilian vehicle, killing five children and injuring three others.  Raqqa recorded the highest number of incidents, underscoring the continued dangers of remnants of war in northern Syria.  The scale of contamination remains immense.  More than two thirds of these incidents have occurred in agricultural and grazing land, underscoring the urgent need for large-scale clearance operations to reduce the risks.
Our partners working on mine action stress that explosive ordnance risk education, clearance operations and assistance for those affected are essential to protect civilians and allow them to return safely home.  In 2024, our mine action partners surveyed 215 million square metres of land.  In 2025, that increased more than 18-fold, to 4 billion square metres, roughly five times the size of New York City, while nearly three times as much explosive ordnance was destroyed.
Deputy Secretary-General’s Travels
Our Deputy Secretary-General, Amina Mohammed, is in Rome, Italy, where she is meeting with senior Government officials of Italy and the Holy See to discuss their sustained commitment to the United Nations and their efficient support to multilateralism, including to advance the food system transformation.  The Deputy Secretary-General also met with the Heads of the Rome-based United Nations agencies.  She will also participate in the fourth regional convening on Artificial Intelligence and Human Development, building on previous gatherings in Cairo, Mexico City and Xi’an.  Together with experts and thought leaders from the country and the region, the convening will explore how artificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping societies, economies and communities, and how its development can be steered to advance human development.

Colombia
Also, just a note on Colombia:  Our humanitarian colleagues tell us that relief efforts continue following the recent earthquake.  So far, 29 humanitarian organizations have reached nearly 125,000 people across 49 municipalities in the departments of Valle del Cauca, Chocó, Risaralda and Caldas, they provided water, they provided sanitation as well as hygiene, health, shelter, food and protection assistance.  The UN Resident Coordinator, Maria Jose Torres Macho, recently visited Chocó, one of the most affected areas, together with humanitarian partners to meet affected communities and local authorities and help ensure a coordinated response.
Meanwhile, the UN Development Programme (UNDP) and the UN Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC) are working with the Government and other partners already on early recovery. This takes place amid continued violence involving non-State armed groups, which is hampering humanitarian access and putting civilians at risk.  We and our partners continue to appeal for additional resources, as we have only received $40 million of the $148 million required to respond to the earthquake.
Migrants/Canary Islands
There are a lot of stories about migrants dying at sea, and I think it’s important not to forget them.  You may have seen one particular tragic story.  Our colleagues from the International Organization for Migration (IOM) tell us that they are deeply concerned by the news of a boat that was adrift in the Atlantic for almost a month before it was found, and by the loss of so many lives.  Our thoughts are with the survivors and with the families still waiting for news.
IOM noted that, unfortunately, cases like this are not an exception on this route.  The distances were enormous, and monitoring and rescue capacity along the West Africa Atlantic route remain limited.  The Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) said that this tragedy underscores the extreme risks faced by those undertaking dangerous journeys, including people fleeing conflict, violence and persecution.  IOM said that Spanish authorities recovered 44 survivors. More than 80 people are believed to have died or gone missing during the crossing; only 5 bodies were recovered.
Food Prices
The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) released its monthly food price index.  As we’ve been warning, food prices are climbing again, and the weather is driving much of that.  Our colleagues at [FAO] say that the benchmark index of world food commodity prices edged up in August, as high temperatures and other weather-related risks raised concerns over supply.
The Index averaged 133.3 points last month, up 1.9 per cent from July and 2.5 per cent from a year ago.  Sugar saw the sharpest rise, up nearly 12 per cent.  That was driven by adverse weather hitting sugar beet yields in the European Union and lower sugar production in Brazil.  Also, behind the rise were concerns over the impact of El Niño on production prospects in key Asian producing countries, and India's announcement of duty-free raw sugar imports.
Wheat prices were up 15 per cent on a year earlier, amid disruptions in the Black Sea export logistics and high temperatures in much of Europe.  Looking ahead, FAO forecasts global cereal production this year to be at 2.98 billion tons.  That is still the second-largest harvest on record, but down 2 per cent from last year.
International Days
Lastly, it’s a long weekend, so I have a long list of international days to observe.  And tomorrow is the International Day of Charity.  In fragile regions, charitable efforts often fill critical gaps, and, at its core, charity is an expression of global solidarity, bringing us together to build more just and resilient societies.
Tomorrow is the first International Day of the World's Indigenous Women and Girls.  Indigenous women and girls remain disproportionately affected by poverty, by violence, and by unequal access to land, to healthcare and to justice.  Tomorrow is about making sure their leadership is seen and their challenges are not forgotten.
On Monday, besides Labor Day, we mark three international days. We have the International Day of Clean Air for Blue Skies.  I will not sing that song.  And in a message for the Day, the Secretary-General notes that, for too long, we have been told to choose between economic growth and environmental protection, between today's priorities and tomorrow's climate.  These are false choices, he says, pointing out that air pollution is taking a devastating toll on lives and economies.
Also Monday, the UN Environment Programme (UNEP) and the Climate and Clean Air Coalition will release new findings from their Global Economic Assessment of Integrated Climate and Clean Air Action.
On policing, the Secretary-General says in his message for the International Day of Police Cooperation that, by working together across borders, law enforcement agencies can better support survivors, apprehend perpetrators and advance safety for all.
And lastly, Monday also marks World Duchenne Awareness Day. Duchenne muscular dystrophy is one of the most common childhood genetic rare diseases, and there is currently no cure. This year's theme puts the focus on families, and the role they play in the lives of those living with the disease.
