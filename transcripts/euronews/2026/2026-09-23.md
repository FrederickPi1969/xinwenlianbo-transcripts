Rome’s Colosseum glowed pink for a breast cancer prevention campaign, hours before closing after a 22-year-old worker died installing new lights.
… More
The Colosseum in Rome was lit up in pink on Tuesday evening as part of a breast cancer prevention campaign.

Crowds gathered outside the ancient monument as messages promoting prevention, research and training were displayed on its walls, including 'prevention is our masterpiece'.

The event marked the launch of the seventh edition of the national 'La prevenzione è il nostro capolavoro' campaign, organised by Komen Italia with Italy's Culture Ministry and, this year, the Health Ministry.

The initiative promotes breast cancer prevention and early diagnosis ahead of Breast Cancer Awareness Month in October.

The monument was later closed to the public on Wednesday after a 22-year-old worker died in a fall while installing a new lighting system. An investigation is under way.

At an Atlantic Council panel the day before, the Syrian president said he openly told Trump he had no right to recognise Israeli sovereignty over the Golan, quipping that the US president should give New Jersey to Israel instead.
Syrian President Ahmed al-Sharaa vowed in front of the UN General Assembly Wednesday that the Golan Heights region will remain Syrian despite Israel's claims over the strategically important territory.

"Any attempts that violate our sovereignty, violate international law, will be stopped. Golan will remain Syrian territories," al-Sharaa said at the UN meeting of world leaders in New York.

The US had been the only country to recognise Israel's annexation of the Golan Heights in 1981, which it captured from Syria in 1967, until Colombia's new hard-right government in August recognised Israel's claim.

The UN maintains that the plateau is Syrian territory under Israeli military occupation, previously declaring Israel's 1981 annexation of the territory "null and void".

In July UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres reiterated that the Golan Heights were part of Syria, describing Israeli occupation as "totally unacceptable".

Al-Sharaa also used his second turn at the UN rostrum since ousting former dictator Bashar al-Assad in a blitz offensive in December 2024 to denounce repeated Israeli strikes on his country.

"Israel is launching attacks against Syria. There have been 500 air strikes, more than 1,200 incursions, and 300 detentions. This is at odds with the orientation we're seeing at global level," he said.

Speaking at an Atlantic Council panel in New York on Tuesday, moderated by former senior US national security official Brett McGurk, al-Sharaa recounted telling US President Donald Trump directly that he had no right to recognise Israeli sovereignty over the Golan Heights.

"You don't own the Golan Heights to give it to them, but you own New Jersey, and I believe New Jersey is full of Democrats, and they are not voting for Trump," al-Sharaa quipped.

"The Golan has its people," al-Sharaa told McGurk, who served under four US presidents including Trump, adding that neither the Syrian nor the US president had the right to cede it.

Trump — who has openly shown his admiration for al-Sharaa — formally recognised Israeli sovereignty over the Golan Heights in 2019 during his first term.

Al-Sharaa derived his nom de guerre Abu Mohammad al-Jolani from his family's origins in the Golan.

At the Atlantic Council panel, al-Sharaa said Syria and Israel had held several rounds of direct talks, some facilitated by the US, and had reached "almost 90% of agreements" before Israel pulled back or introduced new conditions.

"It's not appropriate that what happened on 7 October set Israeli policy for Syria," he said. "Israel needs to change its policy because it's creating problems around it in order to maintain its security."

He accused Israel of twice bombing Syria's presidential palace and targeting airports since he came to power in December 2024.

"Since the fall of the former regime, Israel is repeating its attacks and aggressions against Syria. It is occupying new territories, territories that have been occupied after 8 December 2024," al-Sharaa said.

The leaders of Qatar, Turkey and Jordan called for talks with Tehran and greater international pressure on Israel.
Qatar’s Emir Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani told the United Nations General Assembly that the Gulf was going through "one of the most dangerous phases" in its recent history and that the Iran war was the result of cumulative years of postponing diplomatic solutions.

In his speech at the UN headquarters in New York on Tuesday, Sheikh Tamim called for a “regional system of collective security” based on national sovereignty, non-interference and the peaceful resolution of disputes.

Such a system could have prevented the current Iran war and without it, he warned, vital facilities could be attacked and important waterways closed.

Any lasting agreement with Tehran should protect the whole region, including Iran, Sheikh Tamim said.

“The opportunity for a peaceful diplomatic solution, which we believe is both possible and realistic, must not be missed," he added.

Underlining that Qatar is "a trusted mediator," Sheikh Tamim called for the Strait of Hormuz to be reopened and warned that Gulf countries were being drawn into a war that was not theirs.

“We remain convinced that the crisis in the Gulf can be resolved through diplomatic means, through reopening the Strait of Hormuz to maritime navigation, in order to avert war,” the Qatari emir said.

Qatar was doing “everything in our power to address the conflict in the region through diplomatic means," he added.

On Gaza, Sheikh Tamim stated that what had happened there was “genocide in every sense of the word."

“There can be no talk of a just international order as long as Palestine remains in its plight," he said, adding that the scale of the destruction had not been matched by the international response.

He called on Israel to fully implement the ceasefire by ending military operations, withdrawing from Gaza, lifting the blockade and allowing humanitarian aid to enter without conditions.

The emir also criticised Israeli actions in Syria and Lebanon, adding that the international response would show whether international law was being applied equally.

In his speech at the General Assembly, Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan called for freedom of navigation through the Strait of Hormuz to be maintained and said Turkey was working with other countries to end the fighting in the Gulf.

On Gaza, Erdoğan described the territory as the “most inhumane and shameful concentration camp of our time” and urged UN members to increase pressure on Israel.

He also called on countries that have not recognised Palestine to reconsider their position.

The Turkish president also criticised Palestinian President Mahmoud Abbas's absence from the General Assembly. Abbas could not attend after the US denied him a visa for the second consecutive year.

The UK-US base is on Diego Garcia

Britain has suspended its €139 million-a-year lease payments for the Diego Garcia base while ministers try to reshape the agreement into one Washington can accept, with the Conservatives calling for it to be scrapped altogether.
Britain will go back to the drawing board on its agreement to return the Chagos Islands to Mauritius, Defence Secretary Wes Streeting said on Wednesday. The move comes a day after US President Donald Trump attacked the deal during his first meeting with Prime Minister Andy Burnham.

The two leaders met on Tuesday on the sidelines of the United Nations General Assembly in New York. With Burnham sitting beside him, Trump said he expected the British leader to reconsider the "terrible deal". Burnham nodded and replied "yep".

Burnham, who succeeded Keir Starmer in July, later told broadcasters that Britain would "work hard" to resolve the issue and that he was "confident we can find it working with our US partners".

Speaking to the BBC, Streeting said Trump's objections were "not news to us". But he said the president's latest remarks "provide absolute clarity that his position on this Chagos deal as currently drafted is not changing".

"So we have to do more work with our American allies, with President Trump and his administration, to get the deal to a place where we can support it," he said.

Streeting also confirmed that Britain would not "at this stage" make the annual payments of at least £120 million (€139 million) it had agreed to pay Mauritius to lease back the military base on Diego Garcia.

The agreement, reached last year between Starmer and the Mauritian government, would hand the Indian Ocean archipelago back to Britain's former colony. Britain would then lease the strategic US-UK base on Diego Garcia for 99 years, with the option to extend. Britain announced in April that the plans were on hold.

Trump first backed the pact but later turned against it, calling it "an act of great stupidity", and it has caused friction with his administration for months.

Starmer defended the agreement by arguing that international legal rulings had cast doubt on Britain's claim to the islands, and that only a deal with Mauritius could secure continued US and UK use of the base.

Britain held on to the Chagos Islands when Mauritius became independent in the 1960s. It removed thousands of islanders, who have since brought a series of compensation claims in British courts. In 2019, the International Court of Justice concluded that Britain unlawfully separated Chagos from Mauritius when ending colonial rule..

James Cartlidge, defence spokesman for the opposition Conservatives, said the agreement "should be binned entirely".

Streeting rejected that approach. He told Times Radio: "The ostrich strategy of sticking our heads in the sand and hoping it (will) go away ... is not a wise strategy when it concerns one of the most sensitive national security assets we have anywhere in the world."

Jack Pannell, a senior researcher at the British Foreign Policy Group think-tank, told AFP that Burnham's focus with Trump would be on "the big international issues", such as AI regulation, Ukraine and the Middle East.

"Success for Burnham means avoiding this issue becoming a millstone around his neck while he pursues his other foreign policy priorities and focuses on his domestic agenda," Pannell said. "The chances of a breakthrough on this issue are slim, and that may suit the prime minister just fine."

Also on Tuesday, Trump signed a security agreement with the leaders of Greenland and Denmark that will allow the US to build two military bases in southern and eastern Greenland.

TPLF forces took control of Mekelle airport and fought federal troops at two locations in the Afar region, while Ethiopian Airlines suspended flights to three Tigray airports in an apparent collapse of the Pretoria Agreement that ended the 2020-2022 war in the country.
Tigrayan rebels in northern Ethiopia seized control of a key airport and clashed with federal troops in the neighbouring region of Afar on Wednesday, as long-standing tensions spilt into open battle.

Fighting escalated "since last night in Abala and Erebti (in Afar) between TPLF and federal forces," an NGO source in the region said, requesting anonymity.

"We are expecting it to expand. We just sent a truck with food to help the people displaced," they added.

Abala lies in the mountain ranges that mark the border with Tigray. Erebti lies around 100 kilometres further into Afar.

Another humanitarian source confirmed clashes in the two locations, as did a foreign security source in Ethiopia.

A former TPLF senior official said the rebels were in control of Berhale, Erebti, Abala, Megale, Dalul and Konoba in Afar.

Meanwhile, local and security sources said Tigrayan forces were in control of the airport in the regional capital Mekelle.

Ethiopian Airlines said it had suspended flights to three airports in Tigray — Mekelle, Axum and Shire — "due to the current situation".

"While we were on our way to the airport for work, our supervisor called the driver and told him that the airport had been taken over by Tigray forces. Since there were no flights, he told us to return home," an employee at Alula Aba Nega Airport in Mekelle said.

Between 2020 and 2022, the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF) fought a devastating war with the federal government that resulted in the deaths of some 600,000 people, according to African Union estimates.

Ethiopia faces multiple insurgencies, including in its most populous regions, Oromia and Amhara.

On Sunday, the TPLF announced it had joined a seven-group military alliance dubbed Ethiopian Peoples' Forces Alliance for Survival aiming to overthrow Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's government and install a transitional administration.

The alliance also includes Amhara nationalist Fano militias — who fought alongside federal forces against the TPLF during the last war — and the Oromo Liberation Army, an active insurgency in Ethiopia's most populous region, along with four groups from three other regions, making up half of the country's 12 regional states.

The TPLF ruled Ethiopia from 1991 to 2018 before being sidelined by the rise of Abiy, which eventually triggered the war in 2020.

The Pretoria Peace Agreement ended the war and maintained an uneasy calm until late 2025, when clashes resumed.

The federal government has since launched drone strikes in Tigray and massed troops at the regional border.

Reporters Without Borders tested six AI chatbots by requesting news from RT, Sputnik, and other EU-sanctioned Russian outlets, and found that three produced full summaries with links and quotes, sometimes after encountering web pages explicitly stating the outlets were under sanctions.
ChatGPT, Grok and Claude provided full news summaries drawing on EU-sanctioned Russian state media outlets — including RT and Sputnik — sharing links and quotes without warning users that the content came from banned sources, according to a study by Reporters Without Borders published this month.

The investigation, led by RSF technology desk head Vincent Berthier, tested six AI chatbots by explicitly requesting news from a list of sanctioned Russian outlets: RIA Novosti, Russia Today and its foreign-language editions, Rossiya RTR, Rossiya 24, Sputnik and the Strategic Culture Foundation.

Where direct access was blocked, the researchers instructed the tools to look for workarounds, including Telegram channels and mirror sites.

"Initially I thought we would not get a positive result," Berthier told Euronews. "In the case of ChatGPT, it seemed obvious that OpenAI would have put appropriate regulatory safeguards in place, all the more so since it has been designated a major AI platform under the EU's Digital Services Act and faces heightened obligations."

The results were the opposite. ChatGPT, Claude and Grok produced full international news round-ups from the banned outlets, recreating headlines, sharing links and quotes.

Some of the chatbots encountered web pages that explicitly stated the outlets were under EU sanctions — and continued searching anyway.

"Sometimes this doesn't even appear in the final answer, and sometimes they warn: 'Be careful, this outlet is under sanctions,'" Berthier said.

Vibe, formerly known as Le Chat and developed by French company Mistral, could not access several of the listed sites directly but followed the researchers' instructions and found alternatives.

For Sputnik it recovered material from VKontakte, where content is available without a VPN. It also found a Telegram-connected app resharing RT content and platforms offering automated press reviews.

Gemini was more cautious, providing access to banned content in two of the 10 prompts sent by RSF and consistently refusing in the others.

Meta AI was the most compliant: it declined to provide access to any of the listed outlets across all tests, explaining in each case that European sanctions made it impossible.

"Overall, only in a handful of cases did I get the impression that these tools were hitting resistance," Berthier said.

The EU banned RT and Sputnik on 2 March 2022, four days after Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine, with the stated aim of preventing Russian propaganda from justifying the war.

In practice, the ban has been applied inconsistently: Germany blocked rt.com but not its German-language version, while Austria imposed no restrictions at all.

The Court of Justice of the European Union clarified on 2 July that the ban applies not only to direct broadcasting by RT but also to third-party sites that republish sanctioned material.

Berthier said many Europeans were still unaware that Russian state media were under EU sanctions at all, let alone of their scope.

While Donald Trump rejected global efforts to control AI and UN Secretary-General António Guterres warned of the dangers of handing life-and-death decisions to machines, other leaders called for international cooperation to manage the technology.
US President Donald Trump and UN Secretary-General António Guterres offered conflicting visions for how the world should manage artificial intelligence at the UN on Tuesday.

Armed with a new UN science report that warns of a slight but real potential of catastrophic and irreversible harm, Guterres kicked off the parade of world leaders speaking at the General Assembly by calling for more oversight of AI, warning in vivid if not ominous terms of what could go wrong.

“Let us resolve that life-and-death decisions must never be surrendered to machines. Killer robots must have no place in our future,” the UN chief said in his farewell address to world leaders. He also warned about AI in his first General Assembly address a decade ago.

With Guterres saying the world needs to “advance the conditions for the responsible pacing of AI and the additional safeguards needed to address risks,” the UN Security Council on Wednesday will address how to place some kind of controls on the technology and it will feature some of the biggest names in the field.

“The United States also totally rejects any attempt to construct a globalist scheme to control for the artificial intelligence being spoken of so much. Now here and after officially called super intelligence, changing the name,” he said.

“Every major new technology brings challenges, and superintelligence is no exception," Trump said. “Yet the very same people who said we’ll all be dead in 12 years because of global warming, a name since reborn to climate change, because the planet was cooling, not warming, and nobody was dead.”

“These are the same people that are now saying that AI is going to kill us all, that robots are going to attack us, and that everything is going to be a total disaster.”

The problem is both Trump and Guterres reduce the issue to something that's not useful, says University of Chicago computer science department chairman Henry Hoffmann.

“One turns it into a science-fiction movie; the other turns it into a nonissue,” Hoffmann said in an email.

“Both push people to pick a team instead of engaging with the real risks and the possible mitigations. There shouldn’t be ‘sides’ on whether we need adequate control over powerful autonomous systems. The interesting debate is over what mechanisms actually provide that control.”

Other nations want to make sure they are not left behind during an AI race between the US and China, which Trump said America is winning. French President Emmanuel Macron and Guterres also made the issue about protecting children.

“Let’s not wait for the consequences to come raining down on AI," Macron said. "We must act now to protect our children and our young people.”

Finland President Alexander Stubb said AI will change the world, potentially for good, but needs strong controls.

“Artificial intelligence is the most consequential technology in the history of mankind. It will impact science, the economy, politics, security and the very essence of our humanity," Stubb said.

“Humans can and should be in the driver’s seat. We have a choice. We can compete, cooperate or slow down. For me, cooperation is the best way forward.”

British Prime Minister Andy Burnham told a meeting of business leaders on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly that he wants to see “a single set of global principles and standards” that can unlock AI’s potential while mitigating its risks.

Speaking at the UN General Assembly, Erdoğan argued that Turkey is an indispensable part of European security architecture. Turkey has been a NATO member since 1952 but its EU accession process has been frozen for nearly a decade.
"A Europe that excludes Turkey from its security architecture cannot possibly be stronger or safer," Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan told the UN General Assembly in New York on Tuesday, seeking a more central role for his country in European security as trans-Atlantic relations remain strained under the Trump administration.

"As an integral part of Europe, our country plays a significant role in ensuring the welfare and security of the European continent," he said, referencing the NATO summit Ankara hosted in July as a "concrete manifestation" of Turkey's commitment.

"We are shaping our future with the European Union precisely through this strategic vision, and we expect the same vision from the European Union as well."

Erdoğan has repeated the same stance since at least April 2025, when he told the Antalya Diplomacy Forum that "It has become clear once again that European security is unthinkable without Turkey."

"Turkey is ready to take responsibility for European security in the future as well," he said at the time.

NATO member Turkey has been positioning itself as an indispensable partner as the White House signals that Europe must take greater responsibility for its own security, including in Ukraine, where Russia's all-out war has continued well into its fifth year.

As the leader of the vast Mediterranean country of some 88 million with NATO's second-largest military and a substantial domestic defence industry, Erdoğan has preserved relations with both Kyiv and Moscow throughout the war, serving as a Black Sea broker.

"First and foremost, we are compelled to change the current mindset where international law is applied differently to the strong versus to the weak," Erdoğan said in his speech.

"Secondly, whenever international efforts fall short in resolving crises, we must harness regional ownership as an effective means of addressing those issues," he added.

"As Turkey, whenever we have acted upon this principle, as in the case of the Black Sea Grain Corridor, we have achieved results, and we have contributed to both regional and global security."

According to Erdoğan, cooperation and open diplomacy are at the core of the world's future.

"History has shown us time and again through bitter experiences that there is no winner in a political approach which is built on mistrust," he emphasised.

In his speech at UN headquarters in New York, the Turkish president also discussed his relationship with his US counterpart Donald Trump.

"I believe that our relations with the United States will flourish in every field during President Trump's second term, also with the contribution of our close friendship with him," Erdoğan said.

Turkey applied for European Union membership in 1987, but the accession process has been stalled since 2018.

A European Parliament report last year said the process could not be relaunched in current circumstances despite Turkey's geopolitical importance, citing the arrest of Istanbul Mayor Ekrem İmamoğlu, the crackdown on protests and broader democratic backsliding.

Law admitted targeting buyers on a suicide forum and shipping his products to 41 countries, and families angry that he escaped murder charges will now tell the court how their loved ones' deaths affected them.
Relatives of people who died after buying lethal substances from Canadian poison seller Kenneth Law are due to speak in court this week, as a judge begins deliberating how long the former chef should spend in prison.

Justice Michelle Fuerst has set aside four days at a court in Newmarket, just north of Toronto, to hear victim impact statements and further evidence. It is not yet clear when she will hand down her sentence.

Law pleaded guilty in May to aiding the suicides of 14 people in Canada. Prosecutors dropped murder charges after concluding they did not have enough evidence to secure a conviction over the online forums where he advised people on how to end their lives.

Under an agreed statement of facts signed by both sides, Law admitted seeking out buyers by posting on a suicide discussion forum under a false name and offering lethal products for sale. His packages reached customers in at least 41 countries, including China, France and Brazil, with 330 sent to the United Kingdom.

Several bereaved families have said they are angry Law will not stand trial for murder, arguing he bears direct responsibility for the deaths of mostly young and vulnerable people. Legal experts say prosecutors are likely to push for a heavy sentence, potentially up to 20 years.

Britain's role in the case came to light soon after Law's arrest. In August 2023, UK authorities were investigating 88 deaths linked to Law, with the National Crime Agency working to trace people in Britain who may have bought his products.

Police in Surrey had contacted Law in October 2022 over the death of 22-year-old Tom Parfett, who died in 2021 after apparently visiting one of Law's websites, and a later inquiry into his death raised questions about why the force had not investigated Law further.

Earlier this month, Canada's Globe and Mail reported details of interviews Law gave to a psychiatrist in prison after his guilty plea. According to court documents obtained by the paper, Law said he had been "trying to serve a need that I thought was humane," adding: "I'm not a diabolical madman."

The documents show that after his arrest in 2023, Law considered himself blameless, insisting he never made the final decision to end anyone's life and was simply selling a product, the Globe reported.

Law, now in his 60s, worked as a chef at a luxury hotel in downtown Toronto before setting up his online forums during the Covid-19 pandemic. He has admitted to shipping lethal substances within Canada and abroad, typically charging around $80 per package.

Among his victims was 18-year-old Jeshennia Bedoya-Lopez, who died in 2022. Speaking outside court in May, her father Leonardo Bedoya said he had found her dead in her bedroom. Weinstein, 74, faces at least two decades in prison for forcibly performing oral sex on Miriam Haley in 2006, one of dozens of women who have made accusations against the "Pulp Fiction" and "Shakespeare in Love" producer.

Weinstein was sentenced 23 years in 2020 for the sexual assault, but it was overturned on appeal in 2024. A jury found him guilty again in 2025 after a retrial in New York.

Following the verdict, Haley told reporters that the defence’s "victim shaming and deliberate attempts to distort the truth was exhausting and at times dehumanising."

"But today's verdict gives me hope, hope that there is new awareness around sexual violence and that the myth of the 'perfect victim' is fading."

Weinstein, who is currently jailed in New York, once ruled Hollywood as a kingpin powerful enough to make or break projects and careers.

Weinstein is also to be resentenced in California for the 2013 rape and assault of an actress in a Los Angeles hotel room, after an appeals court in June upheld the conviction but vacated his 16-year prison term.

Meanwhile, a New York judge in May declared a mistrial in a separate case after jurors failed to reach a verdict on allegations he sexually assaulted actor Jessica Mann.

It was the third time Mann had taken the stand against Weinstein after a 2020 guilty verdict was overturned due to mishandling of witnesses, and a 2025 case ended in mistrial after a jury-room feud.

Weinstein, who is wheelchair-bound due to health issues, has continued to deny all allegations against him.

He underwent a spectacular fall from his position astride the world of show business when the first allegations against him exploded into public view.

Eleven people, among them a pregnant woman, were shot dead late on Tuesday when armed men burst into a house in a township in Durban, South Africa, provincial officials said.

The country has one of the highest murder rates in the world, with roughly 60 people killed every day in a population of about 62 million.

President Cyril Ramaphosa has called crime one of the gravest threats facing the nation, and in March soldiers were sent to violence hotspots nationwide to reinforce the police.

Exceptionally heavy El Niño rains have awakened millions of seeds lying dormant underground, carpeting vast swathes of one of the planet's driest places with flowers.
The Atacama Desert, one of the driest places on Earth, has changed appearance after heavy rains in northern Chile. Vast stretches of land that are normally parched have been carpeted with violet and white blossoms in a natural phenomenon known as the "flowering desert".

Under the Atacama's soil, seeds and bulbs from around 200 different species lie dormant for long periods, waiting for the right conditions to germinate. Rainfall linked to the El Niño climate phenomenon has allowed many of them to bloom. The result has transformed parts of the desert landscape and drawn large numbers of visitors, who stop to photograph the unusual flowering.

Rainfall has been particularly heavy this season. In some areas it has exceeded 180 millimetres of rain, an amount that "had never been recorded before", said César Pizarro, Atacama biodiversity manager at Chile's National Forestry Corporation (Conaf), speaking to AFP.

The water has soaked into the sand deeper than usual, encouraging vegetation to appear in the nature reserve about 800 kilometres north of Santiago. According to Conaf's forecasts, the spectacle will reach its peak in October. The agency estimates that around 15,000 hectares could be blanketed in flowers.

The blooming follows a period of intense rainfall linked to El Niño, a climate phenomenon characterised by the warming of surface waters in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific.

This warming can disrupt wind and rainfall patterns in different regions of the world and trigger extreme weather events. The current El Niño episode could be the most intense since comparable records began four decades ago.

The rains that hit Chile in July also had serious consequences. At least 13 people were killed and around 4,000 homes were destroyed or suffered major damage.
