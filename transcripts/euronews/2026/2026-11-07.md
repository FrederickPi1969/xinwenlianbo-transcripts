The US military carried out new airstrikes on Iranian defence systems, sending ripples across the Gulf, but Trump insists they are “minor strikes” while intense diplomatic efforts continue to avert an all-out war.
US military carried out what it said were limited new airstrikes in southern Iran on Friday night, further raising fears across the Middle East and among Gulf states sparked by the possible resumption of an all-out war, but Qatar and the US insisted that the negotiations for a diplomatic settlement are continuing.

US President Donald Trump told the media on Friday evening that a “minor” military action targeted Iranian air defence systems on Qeshm Island.

“They built a little bit of an anti-aircraft device and we knocked it out in about 15 minutes, maybe five," Trump said.

Trump insisted that “we are not fighting” and made it clear that in parallel with the “minor strike,” the US has made “a lot of progress with Iran” with the ongoing negotiations spearheaded by chief mediator Qatar.

Iranian state-run media reported that two missiles hit the Suza area of Qeshm Island without identifying the targets or the damage, while locals went on social media reporting the explosions.

Earlier on Friday, the Iranian Revolutionary Guard (IRGC) claimed that it shot down a US military drone over southern Iran, but the US did not confirm the claim.

Friday’s strike is the first since the Democratic Party scored a major victory in midterm elections, with officials and analysts tying the result to voters' disagreement with Trump’s decision to enter into a war with Iran.

The attack follows another series of what the US military called defensive strikes on Monday, when explosions were heard on the islands of Qeshm and Hengam in the Strait of Hormuz and in the nearby mainland area of Bandar Abbas.

In an earlier speech in Florida on Friday, Trump appeared to defend his Iran war decision by stating that “if I allowed Iran to have a nuclear weapon, today there would be no Israel,” and that “Israel would have been wiped off the face of the Earth.”

Qatar rushed to deny conflicting reports circulating on Friday night about the purported collapse of the negotiations, insisting in a statement that “Qatari mediators remain in close coordination with the United States and Iran to support ongoing negotiations.”

Earlier on Friday, two Middle East government officials told AP that Iran was reviewing proposals presented by Qatar aimed at addressing differences with the US about Tehran’s nuclear program.

But one of the officials stressed that the Iranians, at least at the moment, remain unwilling to engage in negotiations with the Trump administration over their atomic activities until the US agrees to several core conditions that Iran has been demanding be part of any ceasefire agreement, according to the AP.

Differences with Iran over its nuclear program remain the thorniest issue, and one that Trump insists must be resolved as part of any deal meant to end the more than eight-month-long war.

Tehran insists on several concessions from the US which Trump has declined in the past. Among the core demands Iran says must be met before engaging in nuclear talks are the ending of the US military blockade on Iran’s ports, lifting sanctions, unfreezing its assets, issuing compensation for war damage and ending hostilities throughout the region, including Israeli military operations in Lebanon.

Meanwhile, a flurry of diplomatic phone calls between leaders in the Gulf and beyond indicates intense efforts to prevent the resumption of an all-out war which would have devastating consequences for the region.

Trump called Qatar’s Emir Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al-Thani on Friday night to discuss “the ongoing diplomatic efforts aimed at de-escalating tensions in the region (and) to pave the road for reaching a comprehensive agreement that resolves the crisis and ensures security and stability in the region,” according to Qatar’s national news agency.

“In this regard, His Highness emphasised the importance of continuing dialogue and embracing diplomacy as a means of resolving disputes, preventing further escalation in the region, and safeguarding freedom of maritime navigation and the security of energy supplies,” the statement said.

Also on Friday, US Secretary of Defence Pete Hegseth spoke with his Qatari counterpart, Sheikh Saoud bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, according to the Qatari government.

In parallel, Qatar’s Prime Minister and Minister of Foreign Affairs Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman bin Jassim Al Thani told an Oxford University event on Friday that “for the past couple of weeks, we have worked with colleagues in the US administration who have very genuine intentions to find a way to contain the situation and de-escalate it,” as this crisis “cannot be left unresolved”.

He insisted that the ongoing negotiations aim “to prevent the situation from spiralling out of control,” and that “thanks also to some wise voices in Tehran, we have prevented that from getting out of control”.

“We will never have a perfect peace deal, but we cannot just wait for this moment of a perfect deal and avoid having a good deal,” Qatar’s prime minister said. "Our engagement did not stop and will not stop.”

Meanwhile, Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said he also held phone calls with no less than 12 foreign ministers over the last two days to explain Iran’s “fundamental positions".

In an Instagram post, Araghchi called for “regional security arrangements without the interference of superregional powers”.

Iran’s military continued its threatening rhetoric on Saturday, with army spokesman Brigadier General Mohammad Akraminia warning that “we will never allow the enemy’s bases in the region to be rebuilt,” Iran’s Fars news agency reported.

Former head of Israel’s military intelligence research department and Iran specialist Danny Citrinowicz said in a post on X that "Tehran is not intimidated by the prospect of another confrontation with the United States and Israel."

The State Department said: "The regime’s lack of compliance with the IAEA on safeguards obligations has led to the agency’s loss of 'continuity of knowledge.'"
The United States is offering a reward of up to $20 million as well as "possible relocation" for information on "Iran’s nuclear program," according to the US military.

The State Department's Rewards for Justice initiative is offering the reward to people who can provide knowledge of its "facilities, equipment, and associated personnel, such as those in the scientific community, or the movement of any of the above," US Central Command said.

The Rewards for Justice programme was established in 1984 to offer money for information that could help protect national security.

In a statement on the reward, the State Department said: "The regime’s lack of compliance with the IAEA on safeguards obligations has led to the agency’s loss of 'continuity of knowledge' regarding Iran’s production and inventory of key components like centrifuges."

President Donald Trump has repeatedly said that US forces have "obliterated" Iran’s nuclear facilities.

"We're also ensuring that Iran will never have a nuclear weapon," Trump said on Friday during a speech at the Kennedy Space Center in Florida. "They will never have it. Not even close. They're not close."

"If they did have it, if we didn't go in with those beautiful B2 bombers a little more than a year ago, if I allowed them to have it today, there would be no Israel," he added.

Tehran's nuclear ambitions have been central to negotiations over ending the Iran war, which began in late February following US and Israeli strikes on the country.

Trump's comments came as Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian confirmed that his government was continuing efforts to resolve the conflict through dialogue.

Ousted ex-President Santiago Peña was placed under house arrest on Friday after he was impeached over a major banking crisis.
After three years as vice president, Pedro Alliana suddenly found himself catapulted to Paraguay's presidency on Friday following the impeachment of ousted leader Santiago Peña.

Alliana, a former national team basketball player who would go on to lead the Colorado Party, takes office amid a political and financial crisis in the South American nation that was sparked by the collapse of Ueno Bank this week, which froze the savings of half of the country’s adult population.

After taking office, the 52-year-old vowed to tackle the situation before his term ends in August 2028.

It comes as Peña has been placed under house arrest in the capital, Asunción, and is awaiting questioning on Saturday over alleged crimes of breach of trust, influence peddling and misappropriation of funds, prosecutors said.

Born in February 1974 in Pilar, on the banks of the Paraguay River, Alliana built his political career with the backing of former president Horacio Cartes, one of the most influential figures in the country and the leader of the ruling Colorado Party.

Before going into politics, Alliana pursued a business career and also made a name for himself as a basketball player, representing the Paraguayan national team in 1991.

Politics was present in Alliana's life from an early age, with both of his parents having served as local councillors.

His first venture into politics ended in defeat in 2006, however, when he unsuccessfully sought the mayoralty of his hometown at the age of 32.

But two years later, he managed to get elected as governor of the southern department of Neembucu.

He later served in the Chamber of Deputies from 2013 to 2023, presided over the lower house, and then led the Colorado Party.

In 2021, Alliana expected to run for the Senate, but he would later recall that Cartes told him he would instead become Peña's running mate. Peña previously said that Alliana could become "the best vice president in Paraguay's history."

Alliana has faced criticism over the number of relatives holding posts in public administration, among them his eldest daughter, Montserrat. The father of four is married to congresswoman Fabiana Souto.

Alliana did not hide his presidential ambitions. A year after taking up the vice presidency, he publicly acknowledged his desire to one day take the top job.

"Pedro, you are blessed," he said at a Colorado Party event, where he called for supporters to get behind him as the "next president of the Republic."

However, the relationship between the two eventually broke down. After Cartes withdrew his support, Alliana distanced himself and stated on 26 October that loyalty should not be confused with "blind obedience."

His luck changed again when lawmakers moved to impeach Peña but rejected proceedings against Alliana.

Prime Minister Christopher Luxon and his National Party-led right-wing coalition are hoping to secure a second term as they face off against main challenger Chris Hipkins of the Labour Party.

With polls closed and the vote count underway, the outcome is too close to call. Labour is edging ahead on 29.71% of votes to the National's 29.41% at the time of writing.

The Green Party is next on 13.96% of votes, followed by National Party coalition partners ACT New Zealand on 10.63% and the New Zealand First Party on 9.41%.

In New Zealand, people cast a party vote as well as an electorate vote in a general election.

With their electorate vote, New Zealanders vote for a candidate to represent their local electorate (voting area) in parliament. They also vote for the party they want to be in government.

People of Māori descent are able to choose between the general roll and the Māori roll. Selecting the Māori roll means a voter picks a candidate in the Māori electorate they live in. If they opt for the general roll, they vote for a candidate in the general electorate.

As an example, someone who lives in Rotorua on New Zealand's North Island that chose the Māori roll would vote in the Waiariki electorate. The candidate who won the most votes in the Māori electorate of Waiariki would then become their local MP.

If they chose the general roll, they would vote in the Rotorua electorate. The person who won the most votes in the general electorate of Rotorua would become their local MP.

'We have proven ourselves': UAE vows to remain reliable energy supplier to Europe

One of the UAE's most senior energy officials told Euronews diversification has carried the country's energy sector through months of regional crisis, as Europe looks to the Gulf for secure supply.
The UAE will remain a reliable energy supplier to Europe and the world despite the regional crisis, Undersecretary of Energy and Petroleum Affairs Sharif Al Olama told Euronews.

Speaking at ADIPEC in Abu Dhabi, Al Olama said the UAE's oil exports continued throughout the crisis and that the country is using the experience to strengthen its energy infrastructure.

Away from the cameras, conversations among the senior officials gathered at ADIPEC from across the world were dominated by the threat of renewed military strikes in the region amid the ongoing US-Iran war. Many had resigned themselves to their inevitability, while still hoping diplomacy could find a way forward.

Al Olama was candid about the pressure the sector has faced. "This is not a big secret. We are going through a crisis. The first few months were very difficult."

He credited the UAE's long-term decision to spread its energy mix across nuclear, renewables and fossil fuels, with hydrogen and other future fuels in development.

"To diversify the energy sources was the right decision to make and it has helped in terms of making sure that our systems continue to operate and the services were uninterrupted during the entire period of the crisis," Al Olama said.

He credited investment in diversified export routes for keeping oil moving to international customers, with further improvement expected "as time goes by".

Lessons from the period are now being built into facility design, the capacity under construction and the back-up held across the system. "We have learned a lot from the current crisis when it comes to the design of our facilities, when it comes the capacities that we are building, when it come to the redundancy in the systems itself."

On renewables, Al Olama said the UAE now has around 8.2 gigawatts of solar capacity, with plans to grow to 20 gigawatts and beyond.

"We are getting some of the cheapest kilowatt-hours that renewable energies can produce in the world here in the UAE and we're only expanding on that," he explained.

The rapid growth of AI data centres, a recurring theme on the exhibition floor at ADIPEC, presents what he described as a global challenge for energy planners, and one Europe faces as it races to build its own AI capacity.

"The growth you're seeing is no longer even exponential. It's a vertical growth. That's massive," Al Olama said.

The UAE's answer starts with efficiency, he pointed out, citing cases where AI tools have cut power consumption at some data centres by 40%. Planning for expansion, cooling and the use of existing infrastructure follows.

"This is a challenge that we're living through, but we are planning properly for the future," Al Olama said.

Asked whether Europe could learn from the UAE, he framed it as a two-way exchange. "I think, to be honest, it's not about teaching, I think how governments interact. We learn from each other."

He said the UAE is looking at rating data centres for energy efficiency and climate neutrality, and is sharing practice with European governments. "I'm 100% sure there are excellent practices in Europe that we are actually exchanging views on with our counterparts in other governments."

Al Olama's message to Europe and the wider world was one of reliability and partnership, with the UAE committed to its energy transition.

"The UAE has and will remain a very reliable energy supplier," he said. "We believe in partnerships, in successful strategic partnerships that add value both at both ends."

The UAE plans to bring a series of large solar projects online over the next few years as it works towards net zero by 2050, the same deadline the EU has set for its own climate neutrality.
