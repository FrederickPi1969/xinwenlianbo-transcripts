Gaza's bicycle couriers refuse to stop despite danger and pittance pay
Bicycles used to deliver orders in the Gaza Strip

Between 40 and 50 workers have died in the Israel-Hamas war, yet hundreds remain on the roads, covering routes by bicycle after fuel shortages and the destruction of 74% of Gaza's road network made motorised delivery impossible.
Gaza delivery workers pedalling through rubble on bicycles can spend 40 of their 50-shekel (€12) daily earnings fixing a single puncture.

Between 40 and 50 bicycle couriers have been killed in the Israel-Hamas war, yet around 400 continue to move packages through damaged streets and active bombardment.

Fuel shortages, transport disruption and the destruction of Gaza's road network have made bicycles — known colloquially as "baskleet" — the primary means of delivery across the Strip.

Workers who spoke to Euronews at Hamama, one of the main delivery companies operating in the Strip, described a job that keeps them under relentless pressure: the physical strain of cycling through rubble, the cost of constant repairs, and the danger of passing daily through areas still under bombardment.

In the Maghazi area in central Gaza, Mohammed Abu Amra works for the delivery company Barq.

Trips he used to complete by motorbike in under 10 minutes now take him 30 or more by bicycle, with heat, rain, road conditions, and the distances involved making it much more challenging.

Tareq Abu Zekri launched Barq during the second ceasefire in January 2025, then expanded by hiring workers who used their own bicycles.

When he advertised for riders, 109 applications arrived within a day and a half, among them university graduates with few other options in what remains of the Strip's labour market.

The company now has more than 50 employees operating across three branches in Gaza City, central Gaza and Khan Younis.

Orders are transferred between areas by whatever means are available before couriers complete local deliveries, covering shop orders as well as goods sold from tents and homes.

Bicycle delivery predates the 2023 Israel-Hamas war: a pilot scheme in Rafah began in March 2021, and since October 2023, bicycles have become standard as fuel has run short and vehicles have been destroyed.

Abbas Al-Souri estimates his daily earnings at between 50 and 60 shekels (€12 to €14), depending on how many orders he completes.

A puncture repair costs between 30 and 40 shekels (€7 to €10). A worker who earns 50 shekels and pays 40 to fix a tyre is left with 10 shekels — roughly €2.

Larger repairs cost more. Abu Zekri estimates that fixing wheels, hubs and pedals runs between 200 and 500 shekels (€48 to €120), depending on what needs replacing.

Spare parts are unreliable. When a bicycle breaks down, workers strip components from bikes too damaged to ride and use them to keep others moving.

A joint assessment published in April 2026 by the World Bank, European Union and United Nations found that around 74% of Gaza's road network — approximately 4,500 kilometres — had been classified as destroyed, with a further 13% partially damaged.

Macklemore donates $1 million to Palestinian aid: 'I invite Robert Kraft to match my donation'
Macklemore donates $1 million from Ed Sheeran tour to Palestinian aid: 'I invite Robert Kraft to match my donation'

The rapper, who was dropped from Ed Sheeran's US tour for saying "Free Palestine" on stage, has challenged billionaire Robert Kraft to match his donation.
American rapper Macklemore has said he will donate his $1million (€871,000) net earnings from Ed Sheeran's tour to organisations supporting the Palestinian people.

The rapper, who has been dropped from the tour for making pro-Palestinian remarks on stage, has challenged Jewish billionaire Robert Kraft to match his donation.

"I will be donating the entire $1 million of my net earnings from Ed Sheeran's Loop Tour to six organizations working directly to support the Palestinian people," wrote Macklemore. "And I invite Robert Kraft to match my donation. Whatever we disagree about, perhaps we can agree on this: Palestinian lives are worth protecting. A Palestinian life is no less valuable than any other life on this earth."

Macklemore has shared that his net earnings will go to organizations including Palestine Children’s Relief Fund, Heal Palestine, Gaza Soup Kitchen, Medical Aid for Palestinians, UNRWA USA National Committee and Anera.

View this post on Instagram
Kraft, who owns the New England Patriots and Gillette Stadium, intervened towards Sheeran and made the decision to ban Macklemore from the Gillette Stadium because his venue would not "provide a platform for hate speech".

The tycoon and Israel donor predictably accused the Grammy-winning rapper of having "a broader history of antisemitic rhetoric" - something which Macklemore denies.

In fact, Kraft's accusation can be dismissed when looking at the footage of Macklemore on stage.

"To all of my Jewish brothers and sisters: criticism of Israel, criticism of apartheid, being against genocide in no way is a criticism of you. My message is for peace; love for all human beings to be treated with dignity, respect, and equality," said Macklemore. "So I say Free Palestine, I say Free Lebanon, I say Free Cuba, I say Free Congo, Free Sudan, I say Free all people in America living in fear of this terrorist organization, ICE. None of us will be free until we are all free."

Kraft has now responded by saying that he and Sheeran have pledged $2m (€1.7m) each "to aid in the region to fight this humanitarian crisis" - although, rather crucially, he did not specify which organisations or charities would receive the money.

"Earlier today, before Macklemore challenged me to match his donation, Ed called me and asked me to commit $2 million to match his donation to aid in the region to fight this humanitarian crisis," Kraft said in a statement. "Ed now plans to reach out to other venue owners and our goal is to continue to work together to build bridges."

"I have dedicated much of my life to building bridges between all people. I believe deeply that all lives are worth protecting," Kraft added.

Earlier this week, Sheeran said he was "appalled by the conflict between Israel and Palestine," and described the "suffering and pain caused on both sides" as a human tragedy in a lengthy Instagram statement. "I am not complicit," he added. "I have my personal views on this devastating conflict. Just because I choose not to speak publicly, it doesn't mean I don't have them, and it doesn't mean I don't care."

Sheeran also said that "Macklemore coming off tour was the promoter's decision, it was not mine", adding that the rapper's contract for the tour "was with the promoter, not me".

Despite Sheeran's attempts at damage control, the final leg of his tour remains in crisis as all the other artists scheduled to perform as part of his tour pulled out in protest.

Irish singer-songwriter Aaron Rowe, Irish folk band Beoga, Danish band Lukas Graham, and Finneas, the brother of pop star Billie Eilish, all announced they were dropping out.

Rowe said that Sheeran had been a friend to him, “but as Irish people we know all too well about genocide, forced famine and violent occupation”.

The Palestinian Authority has condemned a US decision to deny visas to President Mahmoud Abbas and dozens of officials ahead of next week's UN General Assembly, marking the second consecutive year Washington has blocked his attendance.
The Palestinian Authority on Thursday sharply criticised a decision by the United States to deny visas to its officials ahead of next week's UN General Assembly in New York, a measure that also affects its president Mahmoud Abbas.

In a statement, the Palestinian foreign ministry called it "an unjustified measure that runs counter to efforts to rebuild trust, develop Palestinian–US relations, and create the necessary political climate for implementing the two-state solution and achieving peace and stability".

The decision marks the second consecutive year in which Abbas has been prevented from traveling to New York for the General Assembly, after a similar visa denial last year forced him to address the meeting by video instead.

The State Department said Palestinian officials, including Abbas, had failed to live up to their commitments under previous agreements to seek peace with Israel. In its statement, the department said that "as a consequence of their failure to reform, contrary to their commitments to the United States, and their ongoing activities that undermine the prospects for peace, the United States will extend sanctions that deny visas to PLO members and PA officials."

The restrictions extend beyond Abbas himself. Similar rejections were issued to dozens of other Palestinian Authority officials who had hoped to travel with him. According to the Times of Israel, one notice seen by a Palestinian official cited Section 212(a)(3)(B) of US immigration law, which prohibits issuing a visa to anyone who has engaged in terrorist activities or been associated with a terrorist organisation.

Despite the ban, Palestinian representation at the summit will not disappear entirely. The Palestinians will still be represented at next week's high-level meeting by diplomats already accredited to the United Nations, and Abbas is expected to again appear by video.

The US State Department's statement also noted that it would maintain a waiver allowing members of the Palestinian Mission to the UN to continue operating from New York.

The UN General Assembly is expected to vote on a measure that would once again allow Abbas to participate remotely, a mechanism it first used last year.

The renewed visa denial comes at a politically sensitive moment as a number of key members are expected to recognise a Palestinian state at this year's General Assembly.

In 2025 France officially recognized the State of Palestine, joined by the United Kingdom, Australia, Luxembourg, Belgium, Portugal, Malta and Canada. Four of the five permament UN Security Council members now recognize Palestine.

When Washington first imposed the restriction last year, the move drew criticism from European governments including Spain's Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez who described the visa decision as "unjust", writing on X that "Palestine has the right to make its voice heard at the United Nations and in all international forums".

Thousands who had gathered in Prishtina expecting an acquittal dispersed in silence after the Kosovo Specialist Chambers found the former president guilty of murder, torture and illegal detention, but some clashed with riot police outside the EU mission later in the day.
The internationally backed Kosovo court's verdict sentencing former President Hashim Thaçi to 25 years in prison for war crimes sparked shock and anger among most Kosovars on Wednesday.

Judges of the Kosovo Specialist Chambers in The Hague found Thaçi and three other former Kosovo Liberation Army (KLA) leaders guilty of illegal arrests, torture, murder and cruel treatment during the 1998-1999 war for independence from Serbia.

But among many Kosovo Albanians, Thaçi and his co-defendants are still widely lauded as heroes of the independence movement — and their simmering anger threatened to escalate into wholesale riots.

In Prishtina, a crowd of young men marched towards the headquarters of the European Union's Rule of Law Mission in Kosovo (EULEX), waving KLA flags and chanting the guerrilla group's name.

Some scuffled with riot police and threw stones at the building. A small group remained in front of EULEX until Wednesday night, blocking traffic and defying police orders to clear the road and disperse.

The group eventually left for Prishtina airport to welcome Thaçi and Veseli's sons, Endrit Thaçi and Klestor Veseli, upon their return from the sentencing.

In The Hague, angry demonstrators who had gathered near the court briefly clashed with police, with some climbing onto a police van as officers held them back from the court building.

Earlier on Wednesday, thousands gathered in the capital Prishtina to watch the verdict on a large screen.

As the sentence was delivered, silence settled over the attendees, leaving many visibly stunned after weeks of preparations by Thaçi's supporters for a possible acquittal.

"This process was not about justice, but politics," said pensioner Tahir Bytyqi, one among the crowd that quickly dispersed after the proceedings finished.

The trial has remained deeply controversial in the Western Balkan nation of some 1.6 million, with Premier Albin Kurti calling the verdict "a great and harmful punishment" and urging it to be "corrected" on appeal.

"These are unjust findings, but they are not final and will be subject to review on appeal," Kurti said in a Facebook post.

Thaçi was sentenced alongside former senior KLA figures Jakup Krasniqi, Rexhep Selimi and Kadri Veseli. Krasniqi was sentenced to 25 years, Selimi to 13 and Veseli to 18.

The court said they had "contributed significantly" to a common criminal purpose targeting perceived opponents of the KLA's political and military goals.

All four were acquitted of charges of crimes against humanity and can appeal the sentence.

During the appeals process, Thaçi and his co-accused will remain detained in The Hague. Thaçi also faces a second trial over alleged obstruction of justice related to this case, for which prosecutors have requested a sentence of six years.

US lawmakers have approved sweeping new sanctions targeting Russia's economy and Putin's inner circle, with Trump set to sign a bill that also threatens steep tariffs on major buyers of Russian oil, including China and India.
The US House of Representatives approved a major new package of sanctions against Russia on Wednesday, clearing the way for President Donald Trump to sign into law the most significant American legislative action against Moscow since he returned to the White House.

Lawmakers passed the measure 262 to 159, following the Senate's own overwhelming approval, 86 to 11, last month. Trump has indicated he will sign it.

The sanctions target Russia's energy and defence industries, President Vladimir Putin and senior officials around him, and the so-called "shadow fleet" of tankers Moscow uses to evade existing Western restrictions.

Crucially for global energy markets, the bill also grants Trump the power to impose tariffs of up to 100% on countries that continue buying large volumes of Russian oil and gas — a measure aimed squarely at China and India, two of Moscow's biggest remaining customers.

That tariff provision proved contentious even among supporters of Ukraine in Washington. Some Democrats, while backing tougher action against Russia, objected to giving the president such broad authority over trade policy.

Gregory Meeks, the senior Democrat on the House Foreign Affairs Committee, criticised the bill's drafting, arguing it prioritised presidential tariff powers over binding sanctions commitments. He said he would have supported the legislation had the tariff measures been stripped out, but Republicans blocked attempts to amend them.

Despite these reservations, most Democrats ultimately voted for the bill, while a small number of Republicans opposed it on free-market grounds. Supporters say the added pressure is necessary as Russia's full-scale invasion enters its fifth year with no ceasefire in sight.

The vote came as Russian drone strikes hit civilian transport in southern Ukraine on Wednesday, killing five people, according to officials in Kyiv — an attack that added urgency to calls from pro-Ukraine campaigners for the bill's passage.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky had lobbied Congress directly to pass the sanctions, arguing that only sustained economic pressure would push Moscow toward negotiations. Speaking in Washington in July, he said Russia was losing around 30,000 soldiers a month but showed no sign of ending the war, adding that the initiative on the battlefield had shifted out of Putin's hands.

India responded on Thursday by signalling it would not change course, saying it would continue purchasing Russian oil "through diversified sourcing and on the basis of evolving market dynamics."

Russia remains one of India's most important strategic and military partners, complicating Washington's efforts to isolate Moscow economically.

The legislation also targets companies and foreign entities supporting Russia's military effort and extends existing sanctions related to Iran. It follows more than a year of delay, reflecting Trump's general preference for retaining personal control over sanctions and tariff policy rather than ceding it to Congress.

Ukrainian advocacy groups welcomed the bill's passage while urging swift enforcement. "Once the bill is quickly signed and becomes law, we hope for strong US enforcement to finally end the cash flows to the Kremlin's war chest," said Svitlana Romanko of the energy lobby group Razom We Stand.

Researchers said the flood was caused by a "compound crisis" shaped by long-term warming, glacier retreat, permafrost thaw and geological instability. A 2015 earthquake is also thought to have played a role.
Climate change played a significant role in last month's deadly Himalayan disaster in Nepal, thinning glaciers and thawing mountain permafrost in ways that likely destabilised the slope before it collapsed, a report released Thursday by the World Weather Attribution group said.

The collapse, near Nepal's border with China on 26 August, triggered a torrent of water, ice, boulders and sediment that swept through communities along the Trishuli River corridor, killing more than 1,300 people and leaving over 5,000 missing.

"This disaster was not an extreme weather event, but the fingerprints of climate change are still clear to see in long-term changes," said Ben Clarke, a climate researcher at Imperial College London who contributed to the report.

Climate scientists said that across the Himalayas rising temperatures are causing glaciers to retreat and permanently thawing ground that helps bind fractured mountain rock together. That means climate change can alter not only weather hazards such as heat and heavy rain but also the stability of the mountains themselves — threatening communities concentrated in narrow river valleys below.

The analysis did not determine whether the collapse would have happened without climate change, caused mainly by the release of greenhouse gases when fuels like oil, gas and coal are burnt. However, researchers concluded that warming intensified several processes that made the slope less stable over decades.

The report found that one of the clearest changes directly linked to climate change was the rising elevation of the freezing threshold.

Warming had pushed the altitude at which ground remains consistently frozen upward by roughly 100 metres per decade, according to the report.

This exposes rock at increasingly high elevations to longer periods above freezing, thawing permafrost and causing cracks within the ice, ultimately weakening the rock face under the ice.

Jakob Steiner, a geoscientist at the University of Graz who has worked in the Himalayan region since 2006 but is not an author of Thursday’s report, said monitoring instruments around 5,000 metres now show that some rock and ground no longer remain frozen throughout the year.

“This means that the ground that was sitting below ice for hundreds and thousands of years is now becoming exposed,” said Steiner.

Glaciers in the region have been losing mass for decades at a rate equivalent to more than half a metre of thinning each year, the report found. The Langtang Lirung glacier, located in the region where the disaster occurred last month, has retreated about half a kilometre since the 1990s, exposing rock once covered by ice.

The Himalayan region contains the largest volume of snow and ice outside the polar regions. More than 63,000 glaciers in the area feed at least 10 major Asian river systems and support the food, water, energy and livelihood security of billions of people. However, various studies have found that around 78% of the glacier area, located between 14,763 and 19,685 feet above sea level, is highly exposed to warming.

United Nations reports have found that between 2000 and 2019, glaciers lost 267 billion tonnes of ice per year, roughly equivalent to the mass of 46,500 Great Pyramids of Giza.

As glaciers shrink, they can remove pressure that helped support adjoining rock walls while producing additional meltwater.

According to the analysis, the weeks before the 26 August collapse were also unusually warm. July and August 2026 were the warmest such months recorded locally.

“Other than the potential geological factors like earthquakes that are weakening the bedrock, all these other factors are made worse by human-induced climate change,” said Friederike Otto, climate scientist at Imperial College London and one of the report’s authors.

European Commission chief Ursula Von der Leyen pitched the idea of associate membership for Ottawa during her annual State of the Union speech on Wednesday, with Canadian PM Mark Carney in attendance.
US President Donald Trump has threatened to cut trade with the EU or impose "very serious tariffs" after the bloc proposed to make Canada its first ever "associate member".

He called the prospect of Canada joining the EU “laughable” and called Canada a “terrible trade partner".

“If it’s a good intention, that’s fine. If it’s a bad intention, we’ll put very heavy tariffs on Europe,” Trump said.

His comments came after the EU Commission president, Ursula von der Leyen announced Canada and the EU would build on their existing free trade agreement, known as CETA, with a broader “Alliance for the Future” aimed at creating “a common prosperity and economic security space".'

The proposal comes as both Canada and the EU look to pivot away from an aggressive US under Trump's presidency.

Speaking in her annual State of the European Union address on Wednesday, von der Leyen cast that closer relationship as rooted not just in trade but in shared democratic values.

“Above all, dear Mark, Europe and Canada believe in democracy,” von der Leyen said to Carney, who received a standing ovation at the European Parliament in Strasbourg, France. He is due to address the Parliament on Thursday.

Trump’s tariffs and repeated talk of making Canada the 51st state have soured the relationship between the North American neighbours.

Canada is seeking to reduce its dependence on the United States, the destination for roughly 70% of its exports, and Prime Minister Mark Carney has called for a “unique alliance” with the EU.

The turn toward Europe had also been long coming. In a closely watched speech at the World Economic Forum in Davos in January, Carney urged middle powers to work together to increase their leverage in an era of great-power rivalry.

“When we only negotiate bilaterally with a hegemon, we negotiate from weakness. We accept what is offered,” Carney said.

Thus far, Ottawa has retaliated against US tariffs and rejected trade terms Carney says would weaken its sovereignty.

Continuing to try to exert pressure, Trump on Wednesday signed a memorandum directing the US government to remove Canadian goods from the federal procurement system, which the White House said was in retaliation for the Canadian government’s “Buy Canadian” policy, expressing a preference for goods from its own country.

The White House called that policy unfair and said in a fact sheet explaining the memo that “President Trump is taking action to hold Canada accountable for its continued unreasonable and discriminatory treatment of U.S. goods, which has burdened and disadvantaged hard-working Americans.”

For its part, the EU has taken a different course with Washington, reaching a trade agreement that set a 15% tariff ceiling on the vast majority of EU exports while Europe eliminated tariffs on US industrial goods.

Jonathan Wilkinson, Canada’s ambassador-designate to the EU, said Wednesday that Ottawa had discussed associate membership with European officials before von der Leyen’s speech and “knew that that was something that was on the table.”
