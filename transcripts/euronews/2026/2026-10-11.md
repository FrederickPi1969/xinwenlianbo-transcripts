Nepal’s authorities ordered residents along the Budhi Gandaki River to evacuate on Sunday, 11 October, after a sudden flash flood swept away homes and other buildings in the country’s mountainous north-central region.
… More
Nepal’s authorities ordered evacuations along the Budhi Gandaki River on Sunday, 11 October, after sudden flooding swept away homes and other buildings in the country’s mountainous north-central region.

Villagers fled to higher ground as the swollen river surged through narrow valleys. Aerial footage released by the Nepal Army showed floodwaters racing between mountains and residents being evacuated to safety. The National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority warned communities to remain alert until late Sunday.

No deaths were immediately reported, although several people were listed as missing. Preliminary accounts suggested the flooding was triggered when a landslide blocked the river near Chumchure, in Gorkha district, before the temporary barrier gave way.

The latest emergency comes as Nepal struggles to recover from devastating floods along its border with China in August, which killed more than 1,400 people and left thousands missing. Nearly 33,000 people were affected, with around 7,500 homes damaged or destroyed.

The government estimates reconstruction will cost $4.77 billion. Authorities are now focusing on restoring roads and bridges damaged in the earlier disaster, particularly in remote areas where disrupted transport links have made delivering aid difficult.

Turkmenistan summits focus on regional cooperation and Caspian Sea crisis
Presidents at Caspian Ecological Summit

Leaders from across Eurasia gathered in Turkmenistan for three summits focused on strengthening regional trade, transport connectivity and economic cooperation, while addressing the growing environmental crisis facing the Caspian Sea.
Ten presidents gathered in Avaza on Turkmenistan's Caspian coast on Thursday and Friday for three simultaneous summits on regional security, economic integration, and a rapidly shrinking sea that leaders warned has become as much an economic crisis as an environmental one.

The gatherings — the eighth Consultative Meeting of the Heads of State of Central Asia, the CIS Council of Heads of State and the first Caspian Ecological Forum — brought together leaders of post-Soviet states as the region marks 35 years of independence.

Azerbaijan attended the Central Asian consultative meeting as a full member for the first time, having been admitted at the previous summit in late 2025.

President Ilham Aliyev thanked Turkmenistan's leadership for supporting the accession and said Azerbaijan's trade with Central Asian countries had grown by more than 50% over the past year. Baku will chair the format in 2027.

Regional trade among participating states reached approximately €11.6 billion last year, around 8% of their combined total, according to figures cited at the summit. Leaders set a target of raising it to at least €17 billion.

Host President Serdar Berdimuhamedov opened the meeting by calling for deeper cooperation, saying the six states' mutual trust was sufficient to accomplish "all the tasks outlined."

Kazakh President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev offered the group's most pointed assessment of its track record. Since 2018, he said, more than 200 initiatives had been put forward but only a fraction carried out.

"I believe that the effectiveness of our work should be determined not so much by the number of proposals and initiatives as by their practical value and results," Tokayev said.

Leaders also discussed transport, including the Trans-Caspian International Transport Route and the East-West and North-South corridors, as ways to connect the region with international markets.

Tokayev said construction of the Aral-Caspian highway, which began in August and will run more than 1,500 kilometres, would cut the road route from China to Europe by roughly 1,000 kilometres and reduce transit delivery times to three days.

World Bank projections cited at the summit put Caspian Sea freight traffic at a potential fourfold increase by 2040 if planned infrastructure is built.

Uzbek President Shavkat Mirziyoyev proposed an Infrastructure Development Council to coordinate cross-border projects, describing the region as a potential "key land route for global trade" but warning that roads and railways alone would not be sufficient.

Other proposals included a joint low-carbon energy strategy, coordinated water and pollution management, expanded collaboration on critical minerals and connecting national data centres to support artificial intelligence development. A joint statement was signed at the close of the meeting.

The CIS Council of Heads of State met in Avaza as the organisation marked its 35th anniversary.

Tokayev called on member states to agree a development strategy through 2035 and answer a fundamental question: "how do we envision the Commonwealth in the long term, and what place should it occupy within the rapidly changing geopolitical architecture of Eurasia?"

He also proposed a CIS+ format to expand the organisation's engagement beyond its current membership.

At least 139 migrants died after a boat capsized off the coast of Djibouti earlier this week, the UN's International Organization for Migration (IOM) said on Sunday, with 84 others still missing.

The vessel had been carrying 305 people from Djibouti to Yemen when it sank near Guhere, north of the town of Obock, per the IOM. The incident occurred on the night of 8 to 9 October.

The death toll climbed from 109 after 30 more bodies were recovered. Among the 109 bodies initially recovered, 52 were men, 42 were women, and 15 were children, including nine girls and six boys, the IOM said in an earlier statement, adding that more than 80 people had survived the shipwreck - all Ethiopian nationals.

"This is a devastating loss of life. "Our teams are on the ground alongside local authorities to provide emergency assistance. This tragedy is a stark reminder of the dangers faced by migrants along this migration corridor as they seek economic opportunities."

According to the IOM, tens of thousands of migrants travel from the Horn of Africa through Djibouti each year as they look to cross the Bab el-Mandeb Strait to Yemen and eventually reach the Gulf countries.

"Poverty, insecurity and the growing impacts of climate change drive many to undertake this journey, despite mounting instability across the Middle East," the IOM said.

In March, another shipwreck off the coast of Djibouti left nine migrants dead and 45 others missing as they attempted to make the crossing to Yemen.

"Since 10 September, more than 20,000 incoming movements to Djibouti from Ethiopia have been observed by IOM’s Displacement Tracking Matrix," the IOM added on Sunday.

Denmark’s Salling Group is trialling Hive Robots in stores to handle demanding restocking, freeing staff for customer service while robots work mainly at night.
… More
Picking up products, placing them on shelves and repeating the same movements for hours. At a Føtex supermarket near Copenhagen, robots are learning to perform one of the most time-consuming jobs in retail.

Developed by Danish start-up Hive Robots and built by China's Unitree, the machines have been training since June. For now, human operators guide every movement, helping the artificial intelligence learn how to handle different products.

The ambition is to have them restocking shelves independently by early 2027, working mainly at night when the supermarket is closed.

The experiment is part of a wider trial with Salling Group, Denmark's largest retailer, which has more than 2,000 stores in six European countries.

According to Føtex, workers spend between 40 and 50 per cent of their time replenishing shelves. The retailer hopes robots will eventually take on some of this repetitive physical work, giving employees more time to assist customers.

The machines could later check expiry dates, identify empty shelves and spot misplaced goods. But they still work more slowly than people and need close supervision.

Danish law would also have to change before robots could operate autonomously among shoppers. For now, the focus remains on night-time work and tasks away from customers.

Isaias, which has since weakened into a post-tropical cyclone, first hit the city of Destin on Friday evening, bringing 105 mph (around 169 kph) winds as it struck the Florida Panhandle.

Officials have so far reported four deaths related to the storm, including two in Florida's Escambia County and two in Alabama, according to US media reports.

The US' National Hurricane Center said in an update on Saturday night that heavy rainfall was likely to continue across the southeastern US overnight and warned of possible flash and urban flooding risks as well as high winds.

"Swells from Isaias will continue to affect much of the Gulf coasts of the United States, Mexico, and western Cuba this weekend," it said, adding: "These swells are likely to cause life-threatening surf and rip current conditions."

The Center warned that tornadoes were also possible across eastern Georgia and the Carolinas.

Florida Governor Ron DeSantis said on Saturday that more than 430,000 customers remained without power in the state as the storm continued.

Tracking website PowerOutage.com shows that figure has dropped to around 323,000 as of early Sunday morning. More than 80,000 people are also experiencing outages in Alabama, over 11,000 in Georgia, and roughly 11,000 in North Carolina, per the site.

DeSantis said the main problem in Florida had been from high winds causing trees to fall, damaging buildings and scattering debris.

Video footage circulating on social media shows torrential rain in the state and extensive damage to buildings, with some having had their roofs ripped off.
