Updated: 02/10/2026 - 18:00 GMT+2
Footage shows pupils in Paris, Toulouse and Lille clashing with police as they demand better school resources, warning protests will continue nationwide.
… More
High school students in Paris, Toulouse and Lille have staged fresh demonstrations and blockades to demand better resources for schools.

Footage from 1 October 2026 shows pupils gathering outside Henri IV and Lamartine high schools in Paris, burning bins and clashing with police in Toulouse, and smashing bus shelters while facing tear gas in Lille as the protest movement spreads across France.

Across the country, pupils denounce underfunding, unsafe buildings and teacher shortages while facing a heavy police presence.

Students and staff warn that the mobilisation is unlikely to stop until their demands are met.

Updated: 02/10/2026 - 17:26 GMT+2
A fleet of 5,000 drones forms fighter jets, robots, planes and horses over Nanchang as China marks National Day with a spectacular night show.
… More
A fleet of 5,000 drones filled the night sky over Nanchang in Jiangxi province on Thursday, forming images including an aeroplane and a humanoid robot kicking a football.

Large crowds gathered along the riverbanks to watch the performance, with many spectators filming the show on their phones.

The display was part of the city's National Day celebrations and one of many events held across China to mark the 77th anniversary of the founding of the People's Republic of China.

A spectacular lightshow illuminated Dubai's Burj Khalifa on Thursday to mark the 77th anniversary of the founding of the People's Republic of China.

Tourists gathered to watch the show, with some saying the images had inspired them to visit China.

Overseas Chinese residents in the UAE also attended, sending their wishes for health and happiness to people in China during the week-long National Day holiday, known as Golden Week.

Healthcare workers in Butembo, eastern Democratic Republic of Congo, are using a mobile laboratory to test samples from suspected Ebola cases.

Technicians work inside the trucks, where a negative-pressure system helps protect staff and the surrounding environment.

The facility can be moved to other locations if an outbreak requires it, allowing testing to be carried out closer to affected communities.

The mobile unit has also cut the time needed for diagnosis. Doctors say results that once took up to 72 hours can now be available within 24 hours, and in some cases as quickly as six hours.

Faster testing helps guide patients to appropriate care and allows confirmed cases to receive treatment sooner.

The USS Theodore Roosevelt left San Diego on Sunday accompanied by a Marine expeditionary unit of more than 2,000 troops, bringing the potential total US military presence in the region to more than 60,000.
The USS Theodore Roosevelt carrier strike group left San Diego on Sunday and is heading for the Middle East, adding to the two US aircraft carriers already operating against Iran in the biggest buildup in the region since the 2003 invasion of Iraq, according to US officials.

The Theodore Roosevelt, a Nimitz-class nuclear-powered supercarrier and flagship of Carrier Strike Group 9, departed without ceremony — no sailors manning the rails, according to ship-spotter photographs.

It is accompanied by the guided-missile cruiser USS Chosin and destroyer squadrons, with Carrier Air Wing 11 embarked, including F-35C and F/A-18 Super Hornet jets, electronic warfare and early-warning aircraft.

A day later, the amphibious ready group led by the USS Makin Island also sailed from San Diego, carrying the 13th Marine Expeditionary Unit of more than 2,000 Marines aboard three ships including the USS John P Murtha and USS Anchorage.

Citing US officials, AP estimated the combined deployment at 9,000 to 10,000 additional troops, with arrivals expected by the end of November. The US already has more than 50,000 military personnel in the Middle East.

A US official told Al Jazeera that by the end of November, three carrier strike groups and two amphibious ready groups would be in place around Iran, adding that "leaders will have many options for dealing with Iran."

The USS George HW Bush and USS George Washington are currently in the Arabian Sea conducting operations against Iran, according to the US Naval Institute's report from Tuesday.

The Navy has maintained a two-carrier presence in the region since the US and Israel launched the war on Iran on 28 February. The USS Boxer amphibious-ready group is also already in theatre.

Theodore Roosevelt is scheduled to relieve USS George Washington, according to USNI News. Whether George Washington departs before or after USS Theodore Roosevelt arrives will determine whether the US briefly operates three carrier strike groups in the region simultaneously.

If all three overlap, it would be only the second such deployment since April, which was itself the first time since 2003 the US had three carriers in the region at once.

The Wall Street Journal reported that if USS George Washington remains on station until Theodore Roosevelt arrives, all three carrier groups would be in theatre together. Whether that represents a sustained increase in posture or a temporary overlap during rotation remains unclear.

Trump has not ruled out a new round of strikes against Iran as the November midterm elections approach.

Updated: 02/10/2026 - 12:01 GMT+2
A reopened investigation into an alleged sexual assault at Cornell University is putting fresh attention on how US universities respond to reports of sexual violence.

The case centres on an incident at the Chi Phi fraternity house in Ithaca, New York, in October 2024. A 20-year-old Cornell student alleges that she was drugged and sexually assaulted by seven fraternity members.

The allegations have resurfaced after the woman filed a civil lawsuit against Cornell, the fraternity and the men involved. She argues that the university did not do enough to protect her or hold those accused accountable.

The lawsuit has also brought renewed attention to messages exchanged by members of the fraternity around the time of the alleged incident.

Cornell University Police provided prosecutors with an image of a Snapchat group used by Chi Phi members. The messages, sent shortly before the alleged assault, reportedly referred to a sexual encounter with the woman in crude terms.

Additional text messages have also been published by CBS News. They reportedly involve one of the defendants and a woman who appears to be the person who made the allegations.

The case is now back in the spotlight as New York state prosecutors reopen their investigation.

For students at Cornell, the developments have raised broader questions about sexual violence and the support available to people who report it.

Jane Wang, a Cornell sophomore, said the case had made her think about the difficulties students can face after an assault and about the support available on campus.

“I think like the main similarity that I found with reading that report was just like the fact that she felt as though afterwards, because of the lack of resources that she had on campus, she had no choice but to eventually drop out, right?”

“Right now I think we all have the same feeling of where do we go with this? I had questions as a freshman, well, how do we address sexual assault as a university? And now I'm a junior and I'm still asking the same questions of how do we address sexual violence across the nation?”

Cornell has rejected the allegation that it failed to punish those involved. The university has said it supported presenting the case to a grand jury and has agreed to an independent investigation into its handling of the allegations.

The reopened investigation will now determine what further action may follow from the allegations and evidence surrounding the 2024 case.

Thousands dressed in white gather at a secret New York location for Dîner en Blanc, an annual outdoor dinner with a Parisian Belle Époque theme.
… More
Thousands of diners dressed in white gathered at a secret location in New York for the annual Dîner en Blanc on Thursday, October 1.

Participants first met at designated meeting points before being led to the venue, where they set up tables, chairs and white tablecloths for an outdoor dinner.

This year's theme, 'Une Soirée Parisienne - La Belle Époque', gave the evening a distinctly French feel.

Guests dressed up and decorated their tables with flowers, candles and elegant tableware, transforming the public space into a large open-air dining room.

The tradition began in Paris in 1988 and has since spread to cities around the world. In New York, the venue is kept secret until the last moment, adding an element of surprise to the annual gathering.

Hegseth has infused his evangelical Christianity into his leadership of the Pentagon, hosting worship services for employees and often speaking of the United States as a Christian nation.
US Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth is launching an office of religious affairs, the latest of his faith-related reforms at the Pentagon.

He announced in a speech on Wednesday that the office will help offer “first-class religious support” and will report directly to him.

“Our department is, you might say, putting on the full armour of God, because while we wage physical war, we all know the real battle is spiritual,” he said.

Hegseth has infused his evangelical Christianity into his leadership of the Pentagon, hosting worship services for employees and often speaking of the United States as a Christian nation.

The Office of Religious Affairs (ORA) will advocate for military chaplains and religious support “at the highest level of the Department,” according to a Pentagon statement.

It will not “compel religious participation, favour one faith, or diminish the rights of personnel of any faith or lack thereof.”

The Pentagon “owes it to the nation’s service members to defend religious freedom,” said Rachel Laser, head of Americans United for Separation of Church, which has sued the Pentagon over its worship services.

“Hegseth’s latest announcement violates that promise by elevating Christianity to an even higher role in shaping US military policy.”

Other federal agencies have had religious or faith-based offices, which were established under President George W. Bush and were continued in different forms under the Obama and Biden administrations.

President Donald Trump established a White House Faith Office and had a similar effort during his first term.

Shaun Casey, a religion scholar, led the State Department’s Office of Religion and Global Affairs during the Obama administration.

The office did not promote religion or spiritual well-being, he said. “It was there to understand what were the political implications of lived religion in the various hot spots around the world.”

The leader of the new office has not been named. A Pentagon memo said the religious affairs director will coordinate spiritual resources, including “faith-based homeschool support for military families.”

Hegseth has also pushed the military school system to introduce more coursework that draws from conservative and Christian influences.

In recent years, the military has become increasingly dependent on chaplains to help address the growing numbers of troops in mental health distress.

Hegseth has said he wants chaplains to focus more on God and less on therapeutic “self-help and self-care.”

The Saudi-led coalition said a drone hit the Taibah electricity distribution station, which supplies power to the Prophet's Mosque, one of Islam's holiest sites. The Houthis denied the claim, stating it was a "lie" to back previous allegations against the group.
The Saudi-led coalition accused Yemen's Houthis of striking a power station in the holy city of Medina in Saudi Arabia, a claim the Iran-backed group denied.

The alleged attack came as fighting intensified in Yemen, where forces of the internationally recognised government said they carried out attacks on Houthi targets and witnesses heard explosions in the Houthi-held capital Sana'a.

The Saudi-led coalition said on Thursday a drone struck Medina's Taibah electricity distribution station, which provides power to the Prophet's Mosque.

Medina, the burial place of the Prophet Mohammed and home to the Prophet's Mosque, is one of Islam's holiest cities alongside Mecca.

Coalition spokesman Major General Turki al-Maliki said a drone struck the Taibah electricity distribution station at 5:11 am (3:11 am CEST) on Tuesday.

"The investigation and evidence gathered by the competent authorities have confirmed the involvement of the terrorist Houthi militia in planning and executing this cowardly terrorist act, based on after-action review and the wreckage of the drone used," he said in a statement.

The coalition said one transformer was taken out of service without affecting the wider electricity network. Al-Maliki said the facility supplied electricity to the Prophet's Mosque.

"This cowardly terrorist act, targeting a civilian facility providing electricity to the Prophet's Mosque in Medina, is a continuation of this extremist militia's attempts to target the Two Holy Mosques," he said.

On Thursday, a senior Houthi official again rejected allegations that the movement had targeted Islamic holy sites, stating they were a "lie" to further back previous claims, according to Houthi-run news agency Saba.

The group had firmly rejected Riyadh's accusations in mid-September of a drone attack on Mecca, Islam's holiest site.

"Our operations target its oil facilities and military bases, which are far removed from the sacred sites," Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree said at the time, adding that "fabrications and lies propagated by the criminal al-Saud regime cannot deceive anyone."

Mecca is Islam's holiest site, home to the Kaaba — which Muslims worldwide turn toward for prayer — and the destination of the Hajj pilgrimage, one of the faith's Five Pillars or main tenets.

The accusation came as fighting intensified in Yemen, as the internationally recognised government's military said it had carried out strikes on Houthi targets in Sana'a, Saada and Taiz.

A spokesman said the military had conducted a "high-value targeting operation" in Sana'a and struck 11 targets in Saada.

He added that 27 air strikes had targeted an "integrated military system" belonging to the Houthis in Taiz.

The Houthis, who control Sana'a and much of Yemen's Red Sea coast, have repeatedly launched drones and missiles at Saudi Arabia since the kingdom intervened in Yemen's civil war in 2015.

Almost 400 teams from more than 190 countries competed over 11 rounds. Germany took bronze, while Hungary, France and Spain also finished in the top 10, alongside Azerbaijan and Armenia.
The biggest Chess Olympiad in history brought almost 400 teams from around the world to Samarkand, where host nation Uzbekistan won gold in the Open competition after defeating Ukraine in the final round.

Uzbekistan won its first Chess Olympiad title in India's Chennai in 2022 and followed it with bronze in Budapest two years later.

“This is our second Olympiad victory. Winning it at home feels different,” Uzbek grandmaster Javokhir Sindarov said. “I think we played the tournament at the highest level.”

Sindarov’s next major test will come later this year. After winning the Candidates Tournament, he will challenge India’s Gukesh Dommaraju for the world title in Geneva from 22 November to 12 December.

Both players will be 20 when the match begins, making it the youngest World Championship contest in chess history.

Organised by the International Chess Federation (FIDE), the Chess Olympiad is the leading international team competition in chess.

Unlike most major chess tournaments, players compete for their countries rather than only for themselves.

Four players from one national team face four from another at the same time, with their individual results combined to determine the result between the two countries.

Uzbekistan finished the competition with 20 of 22 possible match points, winning 10 of its 11 matches. Among the teams it defeated were defending champions India, China and the top-seeded US. Its only loss came against eventual bronze medallists Germany.

Nodirbek Abdusattorov was another key member of the winning team. Playing on Uzbekistan’s first board, meaning he generally faced the strongest player from each opposing team, he completed the Chess Olympiad without losing a game and received an individual gold medal for his performance.

“I think we can now confidently say that Uzbekistan is unrivalled in the world of chess,” Abdusattorov said after the victory.

For Germany, the result marked its first Open team medal at a Chess Olympiad since 2000, when it won silver in Istanbul.

“I think it’s by far the best Olympiad we’ve had in recent years, at least as long as I’ve been playing,” German grandmaster Vincent Keymer told Euronews.

Several European countries also finished near the top, with Hungary, France and Spain all placing inside the top 10, alongside Azerbaijan and Armenia.

In the women’s competition, China won gold, Kazakhstan finished second and defending champions India took bronze.

“There is a lot of attention from all the spectators and followers, and this is one of the widely followed tournaments from all over the world,” Humpy told Euronews.

Pike was 18 when she and her boyfriend beat and killed Colleen Slemmer, 19, at a job training camp for young people.
A condemned female murderer who survived two lethal injections during a botched execution by the US state of Tennessee is in critical condition and receiving "life-saving care," her lawyer said on Thursday.

In an extraordinary chain of events, Christa Pike, 50, was whisked from a prison execution chamber to a hospital on Wednesday night, still alive despite two deadly doses of pentobarbital.

"What we have been told is that she is in critical condition, receiving life-saving care," Randy Spivey said in a virtual news conference on Thursday afternoon, declining to provide further details.

He urged Governor Bill Lee, who had denied a clemency request earlier in the week, to scrap Pike's death sentence.

"After having endured solitary confinement for more than 20 years and a full attempt to kill her by lethal injection, Christa has endured punishment greater than any other person in the history of the American death penalty. The governor must commute Christa's sentence now."

"This failed attempt is the worst we've ever seen and unlike any other botched execution in the modern era," said Robin Maher, executive director of the Death Penalty Information Centre.

The centre counts seven inmates whose executions could not be carried out because medical staff were unable to establish IV lines, but "Pike is the first person to survive after being injected with lethal doses of execution drugs."

Late on Thursday, US President Donald Trump questioned how an execution like Pike's could fail, telling reporters that it "shouldn't be very difficult."

The case had already attracted scrutiny as Pike would have been the first woman put to death in Tennessee in more than 200 years.

Kim Chandler, a media witness with the Associated Press, told CBS News that "Pike appeared notably awake as the execution began" at Riverbend Maximum Security Institution in Nashville.

"A couple of times she looked around the room. At one point she looked towards prison officials and asked if it was supposed to feel that way," Chandler said.

Pike later began snoring and witnesses listened from the viewing area for about an hour before being ushered out.

The condemned woman was "loudly snoring, clearly very alive" as journalists left, Chandler told CBS.

Pike's legal team had previously objected to the execution IV protocol, saying it would have caused needless suffering due to her small veins and a blood condition.

"I counted at least seven needles used in her left arm alone, one of which appeared to be bent when it was pulled from her arm after an unsuccessful attempt" to start an IV line, Spivey said.

Lawyer Stephen Ferrell said medical experts had warned of needles becoming dislodged or piercing through the vein, causing lethal drugs to flow into Pike's arm instead of the bloodstream.

Madrid demonstrators bang pots and pans to demand action on Spain’s housing crisis, calling for long‑term security and support for young adults seeking homes.
… More
Hundreds of demonstrators have gathered outside Madrid's City Hall, banging pots and pans and jangling keys to denounce Spain's housing crisis ahead of a key parliamentary vote on 2 October.

Footage shows people dancing and holding signs reading 'vultures out' as they call on MPs to back measures to tackle soaring rents and evictions.

They say young adults should not have to live with their parents into their thirties and call the situation unacceptable as many cannot start a family or live independently.

Homeowners and tenants argue that temporary fixes are not enough and that people need long‑term security in their homes.

Passengers described rushing the cockpit and a fight for control after the plane suddenly plummeted, losing around 17,000 feet of altitude in the span of a minute, according to the Flightradar24 website.
US President Donald Trump warned on Thursday that Iran will be "hit very hard" if it is found to be linked to a pilot's attack on a flydubai flight to Israel.

The dramatic events a day earlier saw passengers help stop the plane from crashing, making an emergency landing in Saudi Arabia before the flight's mostly Israeli passengers were flown home to a hero's welcome.

Trump's warning came as the United Arab Emirates opened an investigation into whether the incident was linked to terrorism and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said the suspect had undergone "Islamist radical indoctrination."

"I would say the answer, based on what I'm hearing, is yes but we're working on it right now," Trump told reporters when asked at the White House whether Iran was involved in what happened on the flight from Dubai to Tel Aviv.

On whether the United States would retaliate against Iran if it was found responsible, Trump replied: "Oh, they'll be hit very hard, don't worry."

Axios reported that Washington had recently deployed additional Patriot missile batteries to Saudi Arabia and Qatar to help protect key energy facilities from possible Iranian retaliation.

The US news outlet also reported that the USS Theodore Roosevelt aircraft carrier and its strike group were heading to the Middle East, where they could join two other US carrier strike groups already in the region.

As speculation swirled about a possible motive, the UAE and Saudi Arabia began to piece together the events in the cabin.

Saudi Arabia said the co-pilot was transferred to the UAE on Thursday accompanied by an "Emirati security team," adding that its initial investigation showed he had attacked the aircraft's captain.

The alleged assault triggered a struggle for control of the aircraft before an emergency landing in Saudi Arabia. Passengers were later flown to Israel on a replacement flight.

"We know that he underwent Islamist radical indoctrination," Netanyahu told the "Fox & Friends" TV show, without elaborating.

"The attacker, he calls out to the aircrew, 'Kill me, kill me'," Netanyahu said, adding it was "clear he was suicidal."

His comments came after the UAE chief prosecutor said investigators would explore whether there had been "terrorist activity or intent."

He identified the co-pilot as an Omani national and said Israel would check how he had been cleared to work on flights to the country.

"We have constraints that say that you can't have pilots from countries that don't have diplomatic relations with Israel. Obviously he was able to go through that," Netanyahu told Fox News.

Netanyahu had earlier credited captain Smit Machchhar, an Indian national, and passengers with averting catastrophe after the co-pilot allegedly stabbed him and tried to crash the plane.

The POWs were captured by Ukrainian forces early last year after being deployed alongside thousands of North Korean troops to support Russia's war against Ukraine.
South Korean President Lee Jae-myung said on Friday that Seoul would take "further measures" if Ukraine refuses to publicly apologise over what he called its unilateral disclosure of North Korean prisoners of war being sent to South Korea.

Seoul has taken a hard stance on Ukraine disclosing the transfer of two soldiers captured fighting for Russia, warning that their families could be targeted by Pyongyang.

Both POWs are identifiable as they had been photographed by South Korean media before their arrival to the South.

The POWs were captured by Ukrainian forces early last year after being deployed alongside thousands of North Korean troops to support Russia's war against Ukraine, in exchange for economic and military assistance to Pyongyang.

Hours after Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy disclosed last week the transfer in remarks at the United Nations, Lee expressed his dismay, with Seoul claiming confidentiality was key to protecting the POWs' relatives.

Seoul later demanded an official apology from Ukraine, saying that both sides had reached a "clear understanding" during consultations to keep the transfer confidential.

"If Ukraine continues to refuse to acknowledge the facts and publicly apologise, we will take further measures," the South Korean president said on Friday, accusing Kyiv of making him out to be a "liar."

"This is a matter concerning the dignity of the Republic of Korea and its people and cannot be overlooked," Lee posted on X.

Analysts say that from South Korea's perspective, keeping the transfer of the POWs confidential may have partly been for Ukraine's sake, as making it public could have complicated Kyiv's own prisoner exchanges with Russia.

In domestic South Korean politics, however, Seoul's approach "could prove problematic," Shin Yul, a political science professor at Myongji University, said given the current administration's dovish approach to the nuclear-armed North.

"The government has acted swiftly in this case involving Ukraine, while exercising extreme caution over the North Korean landmine incident (that injured South Korean soldiers). That contrast could leave the public deeply dissatisfied."

Lee referred on Friday to an interview with Ukraine's Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha published by RBC-Ukraine and other Ukrainian outlets on Thursday.

Sybiha said he would "not like to call it an incident," describing it instead as a "diplomatic misunderstanding."

"South Korea is an important country for us. And I very much hope for momentum, continued cooperation, and concrete results that we are indeed achieving with them," he said.

Seoul's spy agency said that Zelenskyy may have disclosed the transfer of the North Korean soldiers to boost public support in South Korea for Seoul providing arms to Ukraine, a lawmaker said on Wednesday.

South Korea has in recent years emerged as a major arms exporter, particularly since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in early 2022, including through a multibillion-dollar arms deal with Poland.

The new guidance comes as the Trump administration faces mounting criticism over a string of deadly incidents involving ICE officers. The agency downplayed the new measures, insisting they’re mere reminders and that current protocols will continue.
US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) officers have been ordered not to engage in high-speed car chases and to make vehicle stops only if they have completed specific training, under new internal guidance issued after several people were shot in encounters with ICE.

A memo sent to officers in ICE’s Enforcement and Removal Operations division warned they are not authorised to chase vehicles that fail to pull over under any circumstances.

Instead, officers should take down the vehicles’ information for further investigation, according to the memo signed by ICE’s executive associate director Marcos D. Charles and dated Wednesday.

The new guidance comes after ICE-initiated vehicle stops have repeatedly escalated when drivers have refused to cooperate or tried to flee. ICE agents have fired their guns during more than a dozen such encounters since last year, killing at least four people and wounding several others.

US President Donald Trump's administration has faced internal tension over how to handle traffic stops, which are seen as particularly high-risk encounters.

After fatal shootings of drivers in Maine and Texas in July, ICE officers were directed to temporarily pause most vehicle stops.

But the next day, Trump went on social media to direct the agency to continue traffic stops, calling them “one of ICE’s most important and effective Crime Fighting tools.”

ICE has faced intense pressure from the White House to deport more people who are in the country illegally, and its arrests have reached record numbers. At the same time, the agency's rapid hiring spree last year resulted in some poorly vetted and trained officers hitting the streets.

In a statement, ICE downplayed the significance of the memo, which said the new guidance was intended to replace and amend prior directives issued earlier this year, last year and in 2018.

“Occasionally, ICE leadership issues guidance reminders to law enforcement officers,” the statement said. “Vehicle stops and arrests of illegal aliens continue.”

The new memo outlines steps intended to improve the safety of vehicle stops for ICE officers, drivers they are targeting, their passengers and the public.

The memo told ICE’s field office directors that they must ensure vehicles used for traffic stops have lights and sirens that let the public know they are law enforcement vehicles when activated.

All officers on the scenes of vehicle stops must also wear and activate their body cameras under the new guidance. In addition, only officers who have completed one of five specific agency training courses can conduct vehicle stops.

Officers who have not completed the training courses can only participate in vehicle stops “in a support capacity” as passengers and cannot be the drivers of any of the vehicles involved.

The guidance however says ICE officers can continue to use a controversial technique known as pinning, which is defined as “using a vehicle or physical barrier to trap or stop a car,” but only if they have received special training.

Similarly, only trained officers can use spike strips that deflate tires to prevent drivers from fleeing.

Top US diplomat Marco Rubio signed off on the waiver but argued that Washington’s concerns over its Middle Eastern ally’s human rights record will remain a point of discussion in full bilateral discussions.
US Secretary of State Marco Rubio has waived a restriction on $320 million (€284.4 million) in military aid to Egypt that had been previously withheld over human rights concerns, citing Cairo’s help in dealing with Iran since the conflict began earlier this year.

The US State Department said on Thursday that Rubio had signed off on the waiver “in the interest of US national security.”

"The Secretary’s determination reflects the importance of Egypt to US national security interests, and the concrete security cooperation Egypt provides to the United States,” the department said.

In a letter sent to lawmakers, Rubio cited, in particular, Egypt’s “helpful role” in the war with Iran. Egypt has been a key mediator between the US and Iran in the efforts to wind down the more than seven-month-long conflict.

The State Department maintained that the Trump administration would continue to raise human rights matters as part of its dialogue with Egypt “on the full range of issues in our bilateral relationship.”

“The waiver determination recognises the importance of maintaining security cooperation with Egypt at a time of significant regional security challenges,” the department said.

The $320 million is part of an annual US contribution of $1.3 billion (€1.16 billion) in economic and military assistance to Egypt. More specifically, it is for foreign military financing, which provides money for recipient nations to purchase US-produced weaponry.

Congress inserted the requirement over concerns about Egypt’s human rights record, which many experts regard as problematic. The State Department itself in recent years has pointed to credible reports of torture, arbitrary arrest, child labour and severe restrictions on freedom of expression.

But granting a waiver to Egypt has become routine for both Republican and Democratic administrations. The Biden administration, for example, last issued a waiver in September 2024, citing Egypt’s assistance in US efforts to bring an end to the Israel-Hamas war in Gaza.

The helicopter’s operator confirmed that the two deceased were members of its staff, the pilot flying the helicopter and an onboard nurse. Transport safety officials say an investigation into the crash remains ongoing.
A medical helicopter transporting a patient crashed off Catalina Island near Los Angeles, killing two people and leaving two others hospitalised, authorities said on Thursday.

Rescuers continued searching for a fifth person who was aboard the aircraft and went missing after the Wednesday night crash.

The Coast Guard, firefighters and divers with the Los Angeles County Sheriff's Department were among emergency crews who searched through the night and into the night on Thursday.

The cause of the accident was not immediately clear. Coast Guard Captain Stacey Crecy said the helicopter went down shortly after take-off and hit the water a short distance from the island.

“We believe that the medical aircraft was transporting a person off the island for additional medical care on the mainland,” said Crecy.

Relatives of the missing person told US media outlets that the woman was being transported after having an allergic reaction. They also said her 20-year-old son went with her in the helicopter to help translate.

Sheriff Robert Luna said at an afternoon press conference that authorities were not yet identifying victims by name. But he described the two people that died as the male pilot and a female nurse. He also said another medic and a fourth person, a man, were hospitalised.

The helicopter's operator, REACH Air Medical Services of Sacramento, confirmed that two of its crew members died and a third was recovering at a hospital. The company declined to provide more details.

“In the days ahead, our focus will remain on caring for our people, supporting their families and honouring the lives and service of those we have lost,” REACH said in a statement. A memorial service was held on the island to honor the victims.

The crash came just 11 days after a firefighting helicopter went down, killing two pilots in Yosemite National Park in California, and two weeks after a news helicopter crashed in Los Angeles, killing two others.

First responders were called to the Catalina crash shortly before 8 pm local time on Wednesday and pulled four people from the water. Two were pronounced dead at the scene, and two, in stable condition, were brought to hospitals on the mainland, the Los Angeles County Fire Department said.

The National Transportation Safety Board said it is investigating and identified the aircraft as a Eurocopter EC-135-P2.

NTSB staff were expected to arrive on the scene later on Thursday to carry out the necessary physical inspections at the crash site prior to its removal. The aircraft will be brought to a secure facility for evaluation, a board spokesperson said.

Authorities said the helicopter was submerged in about 70 metres of water off Catalina, which is located about some 35 kilometres off the mainland.

The helicopter landed on the island shortly after 7 pm, following a 24-minute flight from an airport in Oceanside, according to the flight tracking platform Flightradar24.

The investigation comes as both Lula and Flavio Bolsonaro prepare to contest each other for what is being described as a consequential election in Brazil, that could shape the developing economy’s forecast and future for decades to come.
Brazil's attorney general asked the police on Thursday to investigate allegations that US President Donald Trump's administration was using government funds to promote a "far-right agenda" in the South American country.

Brazil is three days away from a hotly contested presidential election pitting leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva against Flavio Bolsonaro, a right-wing senator – son and political heir to embattled former conservative President Jair Bolsonaro.

Lula has made Brazil's sovereignty a central campaign theme, warning against "meddling" by Trump, who has backed several right-wing candidates in recent Latin American elections.

Attorney General Jorge Messias called for the probe a day after the UK’s The Guardian newspaper published an article alleging the Trump administration planned to award $1 million (€888,800) to fund civil society groups opposing Brazil's Supreme Court.

The article "raises a serious red flag regarding Brazilian sovereignty," Messias wrote in a post on X. He added that efforts to meddle in Brazilian institutions "cannot be tolerated."

In a separate statement announcing the complaint, the attorney general's office said the Guardian report was "particularly serious, given the ongoing Brazilian electoral process and recent episodes of external pressure on the country's institutions."

Trump was a close ally of Bolsonaro's father, Jair Bolsonaro, who is currently serving a 27-year sentence for attempting a coup after he lost a re-election bid in 2022.

The US administration briefly imposed sanctions against Supreme Court Justice Alexandre de Moraes who led the trial against the former president, which Trump slammed as a "witch hunt" against his ally.
