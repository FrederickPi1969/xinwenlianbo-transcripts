Coffee with a side of alpacas? At a zoo near Islamabad, visitors can enjoy drinks and snacks while watching, feeding and petting the animals. The attraction features ten imported alpacas, a first for Pakistan according to the zoo.
… More
The attraction, aptly named Coffee with Alpaca, allows families to sit at café tables while the animals wander nearby, occasionally stretching their necks over the fence. On 25 October, children and adults were seen stroking and feeding the animals, with several young alpacas also attracting attention. White, brown and dark-coated alpacas can be seen resting, roaming and approaching visitors. The zoo says its ten imported alpacas form the only herd of its kind in Pakistan, offering guests an unusual opportunity to see the South American animals up close.

Bringing alpacas to Islamabad has also presented challenges, particularly because of the city's hot summers. The animals are native to the cooler highlands of South America and are better adapted to lower temperatures than those experienced in the Pakistani capital.

According to Multi Zoo general manager Muhammad Younas, the alpacas arrived during the summer, prompting staff to install air coolers around their enclosure to protect them from the heat.

The café attraction combines refreshments with close encounters, allowing visitors to observe the animals' behaviour and interact with them through the enclosure fence. With alpacas and llamas sharing the space, the unusual setting has become a place where a family outing can include both a cup of coffee and a meeting with some curious four-legged neighbours.

The paper is one of the few Russian outlets that has been openly critical of Russia's war in Ukraine, but it has come under increasing pressure from Moscow, which revoked its media licence in 2022 and has detained its journalists.

The outlet said its team were now also being evicted from its headquarters on Potapovsky Lane in Moscow and that the donations platform it used to receive financial support from readers had also been closed down.

"We have been stripped of all our resources. Entirely," it said, calling it a "coordinated campaign."

It added that since the start of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, it had been unable to report on the "most critical and tragic events of our time" and that censorship applied to "virtually every sphere of civic life."

Along with the newsroom, the museum of journalist Anna Politkovskaya is also closing; it was opened in her office on 7 October 2021, on the 15th anniversary of her murder.

Novaya Gazeta was founded in 1993 by Sergei Kushnerev, Pavel Voshchanov, Akram Murtazaev, Dmitry Sabov and Dmitry Muratov.

The editorial office's first computers were purchased with the help of former USSR president Mikhail Gorbachev.

At the end of March 2022, after the start of Russia's full-scale invasion, Novaya suspended its work due to warnings from media monitoring agency Roskomnadzor but later resumed operations.

After the start of the war, some of its journalists left Russia and set up Novaya Gazeta Europe, which has its main office in Latvia.

Iran’s Revolutionary Guard said Friday that two oil tankers had caught fire and been stopped in the Strait of Hormuz, as Qatari mediators returned to Tehran for another round of talks aimed at ending the war between Iran and the United States.
Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said Friday, Oct. 30, that three oil tankers had encountered incidents while attempting to navigate routes Tehran considers unauthorized in the eastern and western sections of the Strait of Hormuz. All vessels were subsequently stopped in the waterway’s southern waters.

According to Iranian state media, the Guard claimed the tankers had switched off their navigation and positioning systems and were attempting to leave the strait through routes opposed by Tehran, with the support of the U.S. military.

The Guard said the vessels continued sailing despite repeated radio warnings before encountering trouble in what it described as unsafe areas.

It provided no details about the tankers’ identities, their flags, the precise cause of the incidents or the condition of their crews.

The reports came as Qatari mediators returned to Tehran on Friday to continue efforts to broker an agreement between Iran and the United States.

In a separate incident whose possible connection to the Guard’s claims remains unclear, the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations, or UKMTO, reported that a small boat approached an oil tanker Thursday evening and attached two explosive devices to its hull above the waterline.

The devices detonated, sparking a fire in the engine room and temporarily disabling the vessel.

UKMTO said the fire had been extinguished, all crew members were safe and no environmental pollution had been reported. The tanker subsequently resumed its voyage toward its next port.

The attack appeared to mark the first reported successful use of limpet mines against a vessel in the Strait of Hormuz since the U.S.-Iran war began in February 2026.

It was not immediately clear who was responsible or whether the attack was connected to the Guard’s latest claims involving two tankers.

Separately, Oman’s Maritime Security Center reported Friday that the Royal Navy of Oman had evacuated 25 crew members from a Panama-flagged commercial vessel that came under attack and caught fire about 1.6 nautical miles off the coast of Musandam.

The center said the crew members received necessary medical care but did not identify the vessel or those responsible for the attack.

It remained unclear whether the incident was connected to other reported attacks on tankers in the strait.

The Guard’s latest statements followed claims Thursday, Oct. 29, that three tankers had struck naval mines along the strait’s southern route, triggering explosions and fires.

Iran’s regular military also reported Thursday that another tanker had caught fire in the eastern Strait of Hormuz.

According to Agence France-Presse, Iranian officials claimed that all four vessels had been operating in prohibited and unsafe areas.

However, it remained unclear whether the two tankers mentioned in Friday’s Guard statement were additional vessels or were among those involved in the incidents reported Thursday.

Adm. Brad Cooper, commander of U.S. Central Command, reiterated that the Strait of Hormuz’s main shipping lanes had been cleared of Iranian naval mines.

He also claimed that Iran’s navy had been destroyed and that Revolutionary Guard forces were no longer operating in the waterway.

Cooper said U.S. forces remained in the region to keep major shipping routes open and secure.

CENTCOM also rejected a claim made Thursday by the Revolutionary Guard that it had taken control of an American MQM-170 Outlaw drone in the western Strait of Hormuz.

Iranian sources, meanwhile, reported Friday that an American unmanned surface vessel had been destroyed in the eastern Strait of Hormuz. The U.S. military had neither confirmed nor denied that report at the time of publication.

During his first visit to Kazakhstan, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang said the country's growing computing capacity could make it a major AI hub for Europe, as Kazakhstan expands its energy and digital infrastructure.
Nvidia founder and CEO Jensen Huang has visited Kazakhstan for the first time, saying the Central Asian country could emerge as a major AI computing hub for Europe.

Huang made the remarks during a fireside chat with Zhaslan Madiyev, Kazakhstan's Deputy Prime Minister and Minister of Artificial Intelligence and Digital Development, at the Alem.ai International Artificial Intelligence Centre in Astana.

During the discussion, Madiyev said Kazakhstan plans to nearly double its installed power capacity to more than 40 gigawatts by 2035 as the country expands the infrastructure needed to support its growing AI sector.

One of its flagship projects is the Data Center Valley, a large-scale AI computing campus under development in Ekibastuz, one of Kazakhstan's main power-generating centres. Its first 50-megawatt phase is expected to begin operations in 2027, while the project's energy infrastructure could eventually scale up to one gigawatt.

Huang said Kazakhstan's energy resources and growing computing capacity could give the country a significant role in Europe's AI ecosystem.

“Kazakhstan has a lot of real estate. It also has a lot of energy,” Huang said. “You're going to have a lot of compute right here in Kazakhstan. It's going to be a hub, a computing hub, an AI factory hub for all of Europe.”

Huang described the country's abundant energy resources as a potential competitive advantage in the AI economy, arguing that electricity could increasingly be used to generate computing power and AI rather than simply being exported as energy.

“Kazakhstan has a fundamental advantage because you have so much natural resource already. You have so much energy, and instead of just exporting energy in the future, you could transform the energy into intelligence and export intelligence,” Huang said.

But energy alone, Huang argued, will not be enough. Kazakhstan will also need to retain control over another resource — its data.

Madiyev said the country is already developing Kazakh-language large language models based on open-source foundation models and asked whether Kazakhstan should eventually build its own foundation model from scratch.

Huang said building a domestic foundation model “makes complete sense”, describing it as a national security concern.

He argued that countries should retain control over both their data and the AI infrastructure used to serve their citizens, comparing it to other essential national infrastructure.

“In the future, you will have your own electric grid, your own internet, your own telecommunications and your own intelligence grid,” Huang said.

Beyond computing infrastructure and sovereign AI, Kazakhstan is also looking to position itself in the emerging field of Physical AI — systems that can interact with and operate in the physical world.

Pointing to Kazakhstan's industrial base in mining, agriculture, transport and logistics, Madiyev asked whether the country could become a hub for developing and training Physical AI models and robots. Huang called the idea “fantastic”, saying these industries could be among the first to benefit.

“Physical AI is not just robotics,” Huang said. “It includes everything from seismic understanding to mapping mines and wells, to understanding geography and geology — where to mine, how best to mine — understanding material science, the processing of chemicals.”

With the technology still at an early stage, Huang said Kazakhstan has an opportunity to help shape the emerging industry.

The discussion also turned to Higgsfield AI, a Kazakh-founded generative video startup in which Nvidia has invested.

Asked what attracted Nvidia to the company, Huang said the decision ultimately came down to its founders.

“The first thing and the last thing is you invest in people, not companies,” Huang said, describing Higgsfield's founders as “visionary” and capable of competing with leading AI companies globally.

Huang also pointed to the company's presence in both Kazakhstan and the United States, saying it could help bridge the two ecosystems.

Later that day, Huang met President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev, who described the development of artificial intelligence and digitalisation as a national priority.

Pike survived two doses of pentobarbital during her attempted execution on 30 September. She was sentenced to death for torturing and killing her classmate Colleen Slemmer in 1995, when she was 18.
Christa Pike said she has been in constant pain since surviving a lethal injection in Tennessee last month, but remains grateful for the love and support she has received.

“There hasn’t been a moment since that night when I haven’t been in pain. My body hurts. My arms hurt. My hands hurt. My throat hurts. I still struggle to speak. And the pain isn’t just physical. There is pain in my mind and in my soul,” Pike said in her first public statement, released through her attorneys on Friday.

Pike dictated the statement to her legal team from the infirmary at the Debra K. Johnson Rehabilitation Center, where she is being held.

Pike said she entered the execution chamber prepared to die peacefully, only to wake up alone and confused in a hospital bed. The 50-year-old added that she had never imagined living beyond her scheduled execution date.

“I don’t know why I survived that night. I don’t know what comes next. Some days, finding hope is harder than others. For now, I am trying to heal, one day at a time. And I am holding on to the love and kindness of so many people who have reminded me that I am not alone,” she said.

Pike also criticised the handling of her execution attempt, while expressing sympathy for her victim’s family.

"I’m deeply hurt by the incompetence that has put so many people in this terrible position. I continue to think about my victim’s family and the terrible pain and loss they have endured,” Pike said.

Pike’s lawyers have called on Tennessee Governor Bill Lee to commute her death sentence to life imprisonment.

However, Lee told reporters this week that he expected her sentence to remain unchanged unless the courts ruled otherwise.

“I suspect her sentence will be the same as before unless determined otherwise by the courts.”

The governor has ordered an investigation into the failed execution and postponed the state's other lethal injection scheduled for this year.

She was initially hospitalised following the failed execution before being transferred back to prison.

Law enforcement heads from 46 Islamic states gather in Baku to tackle cross-border financial crime, artificial intelligence and international asset recovery.
As transnational criminal networks deploy increasingly sophisticated technology to conceal illicit wealth, authorities across the globe are accelerating efforts to dismantle international financial crime.

These priorities framed the agenda in Azerbaijan as delegates convened for the Third High-Level Meeting of the Heads of Anti-Corruption Law Enforcement Agencies of Member States of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation (OIC).

According to the OIC, the landmark treaty represents the first multilateral anti-corruption law enforcement agreement tailored specifically for Islamic Cooperation member states, establishing a unified legal foundation to track illicit financial flows across borders.

Cross-border financial investigations often face significant legal barriers, a challenge highlighted alongside Malaysia’s recent asset recovery experience.

Abd Halim Bin Aman, Chief Commissioner of the Malaysian Anti-Corruption Commission, detailed his agency’s multi-year effort to trace and repatriate stolen funds following the 1Malaysia Development Berhad (1MDB) case.

"We managed to achieve our goal of tackling the 1MDB matter and as of today we managed to bring back home about 75% to 80% to outside countries, and we managed to bring back about 33 billion Malaysian ringgit worth of loss," he revealed.

Originally established in 2009 as a state-owned strategic development fund, 1MDB became well-known for being embroiled in one of the world's largest financial kleptocracy scandals, after billions of dollars were systematically siphoned off through offshore shell companies and international bank accounts.

According to the US Justice Department’s website, between 2009 and 2015, money was "laundered through major financial institutions worldwide, including in the United States, Switzerland, Singapore, and Luxembourg" and "misappropriated more than $4.5 billion (approximately €4 billion) in funds belonging to 1MDB through a criminal scheme."

The Justice Department stated it "repatriated an additional $156 million (approximately €139 million) in misappropriated 1MDB funds" to people from Malaysia and brought the total of returned funds to approximately $1.4 billion (approximately €1.2 billion).

He said that the summit provides a necessary platform to share legislation and best practices to speed up investigations irrespective of legal backgrounds.

Ivica Bošnjak, Deputy Minister of Security for Bosnia and Herzegovina, outlined the unique structural and geographical pressures facing his country.

"Bosnia and Herzegovina is complicated and complex in the political part, and therefore the organization of the entire security system is complex," Bošnjak stated.

He noted that national authorities must coordinate "16 police agencies and a whole series of other law enforcement agencies" operating across the security sector.

While the state has adopted extensive legal regulations and action plans, Bošnjak told Euronews that "much still needs to be done to refine a couple of more crucial laws" and operational strategies to tackle cross-border financial crime effectively.

Tugay Rahimli, Special Assistant to the Prosecutor General of Azerbaijan, outlined the scale and primary objective of the summit.

"Today's event brings together more than 250 participants from 46 member and service states as well as eight international organisations," Rahimli said.

"The main objective of today's event is to facilitate and foster international cooperation among member states, facilitate exchange of information and best practices, as well as to enhance the joint efforts to recover the assets obtained through crime," he added.

"Particularly important outcomes of today's event that I would like to highlight is the election of Azerbaijan to chair the bureau for the next two years," he told Euronews.

Kamran Aliyev, Prosecutor General of the Republic of Azerbaijan, confirmed the host nation's immediate legislative steps regarding the agreement, the Makkah al-Mukarramah Convention, which is an agreement designed to facilitate mutual legal assistance, the exchange of information, and the recovery of assets derived through corruption.

"This convention first was adopted in 2022 in the city of Makkah, of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, and this is a new platform in fighting corruption at a regional level," Aliyev said.

The agreement operates under a framework, which incorporates 57 nations, with the intention to bypass lengthy diplomatic channels and to allow law enforcement agencies to share intelligence quickly, conduct joint investigations, and trace, freeze, and return stolen state funds to their countries of origin.

Aliyev said that Azerbaijan has joined international initiatives and international and regional conventions against corruption and has also "implemented all provisions of the conventions into our legislation."

Updated: 30 October 2026 - 12:17 GMT+1
Nine orangutans rescued from devastating wildfires in Indonesian Borneo have returned to the forest after weeks of rehabilitation. Among them are four mothers with their young, including three females expecting another baby.
… More
Deep in the protected Batu Nyambu forest, conservationists opened the transport cages on Thursday, 29 October, allowing the animals to scramble up nearby trees and vanish into the canopy. The group included four mothers accompanied by their young, three of whom had also been found to be pregnant during their final veterinary examinations.

The orangutans had been rescued in August and September from an oil palm plantation in Ketapang, where fires had swept through their habitat. Some suffered burns to their hands and feet, while others were severely underweight and malnourished, according to Karmele Llano Sanchez, head of the YIARI wildlife rehabilitation foundation.

After spending four to eight weeks recovering at a rehabilitation centre in Ketapang, the orangutans faced a difficult journey to their new home. Conservationists transported them by vehicle, boat and on foot, carrying heavy cages through steep, slippery forest terrain to reach Batu Nyambu, a 23,000-hectare protected area chosen for its largely undisturbed habitat and abundant food.

The release comes amid growing concern over the survival of Borneo's critically endangered orangutans. Indonesia's forestry ministry says at least 51 have been rescued from burning forests in West Kalimantan since early August, while monitoring group Nusantara Atlas estimates that fires consumed more than 1.9 million hectares of land across Indonesia in the first nine months of 2026.

Flowers have been placed at Patrick Stewart's Hollywood Walk of Fame star following reports of the British actor's death at 86. Visitors gather in Los Angeles to remember his celebrated screen roles.
… More
A quiet tribute unfolded on the Hollywood Walk of Fame on Thursday as visitors stopped beside Sir Patrick Stewart's star, where flowers had been laid in memory of the British actor. His wax figure at Madame Tussauds Hollywood offered another reminder of his enduring screen presence.

Stewart died at his Los Angeles home on Wednesday, aged 86, with his wife Sunny Ozell beside him, following a brief illness involving Alzheimer's disease and Lewy body dementia, according to his family.

From the Shakespearean stage to the bridge of the Starship Enterprise, his career spanned generations. His portrayal of Captain Jean-Luc Picard made him a defining figure in Star Trek, while his role as Professor Charles Xavier introduced him to audiences of the X-Men films.

William Shatner remembered a performer who brought intelligence, grace and humanity to everything he did.

Does eating peanuts every day help to maintain memory as we get older?
A recent scientific study indicates that eating peanuts daily may help improve the brain's response to insulin in areas linked to memory in older adults.

The study was published in the journal "Food & Function", and was carried out by researchers from the medical centre at Maastricht University in the Netherlands, with the aim of examining the effect of regular peanut consumption on the brain's response to insulin with advancing age.

The participants ate 60 grams of roasted peanuts with their skins and without salt every day for 16 weeks, then the researchers compared the results with another period during which they did not eat peanuts.

The results showed an improvement in the response of areas in the right hemisphere of the brain to insulin, including parts of the hippocampus, the parahippocampal gyrus, the lingual gyrus and the posterior cingulate gyrus, which are areas involved in memory processes and other cognitive functions.

To measure this response, the researchers gave the participants insulin through the nose, then used magnetic resonance imaging based on arterial blood labelling to measure changes in blood flow to brain regions.

Insulin plays a role in a number of brain processes linked to learning, memory and communication between nerve cells.

The brain's response to this hormone may become less effective with advancing age, a change that is associated with age related cognitive decline.

However, the researchers did not demonstrate that eating peanuts prevents cognitive decline or stops dementia from developing, but they observed changes in the response of specific brain regions to insulin.

These findings build on previous research by the same team on the same dietary intervention, published in the journal Clinical Nutrition in 2025, where the results showed a 3.6% increase in total blood flow to the brain, along with a 5.8% improvement in verbal memory after the 16 week intervention period.

The researchers believe that the nutritional composition of peanuts may explain some of these effects, as they contain unsaturated fats, fibre, vitamins, minerals and biologically active plant compounds, as well as plant protein.

Peanuts are also a source of the amino acid L-arginine, which the body uses to produce nitric oxide, a compound that helps regulate blood vessel functions and may in turn be linked to learning and memory processes.

These findings still need further research to determine to what extent the recorded changes in the brain's response to insulin are reflected in memory and mental performance.

The experiment was limited to 31 people, and the indicators observed by the researchers are not enough to state with certainty that peanuts provide protection from Alzheimer's or other dementia disorders.

Conducting studies on larger numbers of participants and following them for longer periods would help to clarify this relationship and determine whether the potential benefits continue over time.

Two years after riding a wave of discontent to total control of Washington, Donald Trump and the Republicans face the same message of change that brought them to power. This time, it's aimed squarely at them.
America's midterm elections are less than one week away. It's not a presidential election year, but the result could reverberate from the White House to Brussels. On the ballot are all 435 seats in the House of Representatives and 35 seats in the Senate. Republicans currently hold a narrow 219-214 majority in the House and a 53-47 majority in the Senate. For President Trump, the stakes could hardly be higher. And the political winds that carried him back to the White House are now blowing hard in the other direction.

Two years ago, inflation and the economy propelled Trump back to the White House and handed Republicans control of both chambers of Congress.

The message was simple: change. According to 2024 ABC News exit polling, 28% of voters said the ability to bring needed change was the most important quality in a candidate. Trump won those voters 74% to 24%. The economy was the top issue for 32% of voters, and Trump won them 81% to 18%, according to NBC News exit polls.

That message carried down the ballot. Republicans flipped four Senate seats — West Virginia, Montana, Ohio and Pennsylvania — to retake the chamber, ousting long-serving Democrats such as Sherrod Brown and Bob Casey.

This time, the Republicans are the incumbents. They face the same message of change that brought them to power, and it does not appear to be a message that will favour them.

The economy is in worse shape than when Trump regained power. Inflation is not only rising, it is higher than when he was elected: 2.7% in November 2024, against 3.7% this September, up from 3.4% in August and driven largely by soaring energy prices.

Voters have noticed. Trump left office in 2021 above water on the economy, 48% to 41%, and in 2024 voters trusted him over Kamala Harris to handle it by 53% to 46%, according to NBC News exit polls. Today, just 34% approve of his handling of the economy and 65% disapprove, according to the CNBC All-America Economic Survey — his lowest mark in either term, and a disapproval rating worse than Joe Biden's worst showing.

The once-unthinkable has happened: 37% of voters now say Democrats have the better approach on the economy, against 33% for Republicans, according to the latest Reuters/Ipsos poll, which puts Trump's approval at 32%.

He repeated it relentlessly in 2024, as did his surrogates and the podcasters who backed him. On Ukraine, CNN counted at least 53 occasions on which he promised to end the war within 24 hours or before even taking office.

Today, Russia's war on Ukraine grinds on, its latest "breakthrough" a local humanitarian ceasefire in the single occupied town of Oleshky. A year after Trump declared the war in Gaza over, few of the ceasefire's promises have been fulfilled.

And in February, Trump started a new war, joining Israel in launching strikes on Iran. Nearly eight months later, there is no end in sight. At a rally in Texas on Tuesday, Trump told supporters the war would end "whenever I want it to end."

It was unpopular from day one: 56% of Americans opposed it in the first week, according to a PBS News/NPR/Marist poll. The opposition has only grown. Disapproval of Trump's handling of the war has risen from 61% in March to 65%, according to Pew Research, and in September a record share of more than 60% told Fox News it was the wrong decision.

The war has rattled the global economy, but Americans feel it most at the pump. Regular gasoline costs an average of $4.25 a gallon, up from $2.98 before the war, according to AAA, which says this is the first October that gas has averaged more than $4. Diesel has climbed from $3.76 to $6.35.

That hits everyone, but it hits Trump's rural base hardest: the farmers, ranchers and truckers who run on diesel.

Trump's response has been to insist that nothing is wrong. "Our first term, we had the greatest economy in the history of our country, and we're blowing it away," he told a rally in Tennessee this month. In March, as oil prices spiked, he went further: "When oil prices go up, we make a lot of money," he wrote on Truth Social.

But the impact on Americans is impossible to hide, or to blame on his opponents. Eight in ten rate the economy as fair or poor, according to CNBC.

The war is not the only policy hurting Trump's most loyal voters. In late August, he paused certain beef tariffs for 90 days, opening the door to cheaper Brazilian beef. Cattle auction prices fell immediately.

Then there are the data centres, which have run straight into rural communities worried about their land, water and power bills. Half of Americans now oppose one within three miles of their home, up from 28% in January, according to a Politico poll. Farm bankruptcies are up 46% this year, according to the Nebraska Farmers Union, and 57% of rural voters disapprove of Trump's handling of the economy, according to an AP-KFF poll cited by ABC Australia.

The cracks run through Trump's own movement. Marjorie Taylor Greene broke with him over the Epstein files, foreign wars and affordability, and resigned from Congress after Trump branded her a "traitor." Tucker Carlson broke with him over Iran and now says he regrets supporting him.

Joe Rogan, whose podcast helped deliver young men to Trump, put it bluntly: "He ran on no more wars... And then we have one that we can't even really clearly define why we did it."

Rather than mend those fences, Trump spent primary season going after some of his party's most steadfast conservatives.

In Kentucky, Congressman Thomas Massie, a fiscal hawk with a lifetime Heritage Action score of 83%, lost his primary 55% to 45% to a Trump-endorsed challenger backed by many of the president's biggest donors, in a race that drew more than $32 million in advertising. His crime was not a lack of conservatism. It was a willingness to tell the president "no."
