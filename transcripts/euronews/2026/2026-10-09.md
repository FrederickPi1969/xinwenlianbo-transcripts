US President Donald Trump said on Friday that Russian leader Vladimir Putin had agreed to release diesel into US and global markets.
Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has hit out at what he called "gifts to Putin" after the US announced a deal to buy Russian diesel.

US President Donald Trump said on Friday that Russian President Vladimir Putin had agreed to release diesel into US and global markets, a move he said would help ease soaring energy costs amid the war with Iran.

"Russia will immediately supply over 300,000 tons of Diesel Fuel to the American and Global Marketplace, another 500,000 tons during the month of November," Trump said following talks with Putin.

Responding to the announcement in a post on X, Zelenskyy wrote: "Gifts to Putin will not bring peace or any benefit to the civilized world."

"Allowing Russia to sell petroleum products is an investment in a war that must be ended, not prolonged. What is needed is real de-escalation with Russia on a reciprocal basis," he added.

The US Treasury Department also said it was issuing a temporary licence allowing Russian diesel to enter global markets.

The White House did not immediately clarify who would pay for the Russian diesel or when supplies under the agreement would become available. It also did not say whether further deliveries expected next month would arrive before the 3 November midterm elections.

Trump's announcement comes just a month after he signed sweeping sanctions against Russia aimed at targeting its energy sector and other industries helping finance its war in Ukraine.

The move raises questions over whether the White House will impose further sanctions on Russia this month, as required by bipartisan legislation targeting major importers of Russian oil and gas.

Trump made no mention of the sanctions or Russia's war in Ukraine, instead saying: “Lower prices for Americans, especially our Great Farmers, Ranchers, and Truckers, is my Greatest Priority.”

Russian presidential envoy Kirill Dmitriev reposted the Treasury Department’s announcement on X Friday and said that “Russia-US cooperation on diesel and energy will benefit the world.”

The quake's epicentre was located near the country's southern tip, around 12 kilometres west-southwest of the remote community of Pitaloza Arriba, the USGS said.

A tsunami warning was issued on Friday for the eastern Pacific, but this was later lifted.

Rene Garcia, the head of emergency operations at the country's National Civil Protection System, told the AFP news agency that there were so far no reported fatalities. At least 27 people have reportedly been injured.

Tremors were felt in the capital Panama City, where buildings shook and residents rushed into the streets as they awaited updates.

Omar Smith of the National Civil Protection Service told local broadcaster Telemetro Reporta that damage had mainly been reported in the central provinces of Veraguas, Herrera and Los Santos. He said no damage had been reported in the capital.

Panamanian President Jose Raul Mulino urged calm following the earthquake, writing on X: “What happened should not be used to cause alarm or disorder.”

Panama is located along the Pacific "Ring of Fire", a 40,000-kilometre stretch of volcanoes and fault lines where most of the world's earthquakes occur.

The Omani co-pilot was previously barred from flying in Oman over extremist views.
The co-pilot who attacked the captain of a flydubai jet on 30 September had planned to crash the aircraft into Tel Aviv's international airport, according to the United Arab Emirates' top prosecutor.

The Omani co-pilot was inspired by Al-Qaeda's September 11, 2001 attacks on the United States and intended to cause "the greatest possible loss of life", according to the prosecutor's findings published by state media.

"The findings showed that he had met the professional and security requirements for employment, including holding a pilot's licence, providing a certificate of good conduct and completing the required checks."

Passengers described storming the cockpit and struggling to regain control after the aircraft suddenly dropped around 5,000 metres.

The plane's Indian captain was injured when the co-pilot attacked him with an axe, but managed to open the cockpit door, allowing passengers and crew to subdue the attacker. The aircraft then made an emergency landing in Saudi Arabia.

On Thursday, Israeli President Isaac Herzog awarded the Presidential Award for Civilian Heroism to six Israelis who helped foil the recent attempted flydubai hijacking.

“The civilian bravery and resourcefulness demonstrated by the heroes present here cannot overshadow the obligation to obtain full answers. Everyone, all of us, deserves clear and satisfactory answers,” he said as reported by The Jerusalem Post.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu praised the passengers and the pilot for their bravery in thwarting the attack and ordered a "thorough review of all security aspects of foreign civil aviation to Israel".

Ukraine’s counter-offensive on the Lyman axis collapsed the northern arm of a Russian pincer movement aimed at surrounding Ukrainian-held Donetsk, liberating up to 176 square kilometres and forcing Russian units onto the defensive, the Washington-based think tank said.
Ukrainian forces have derailed Russia’s plan to encircle and occupy the remaining Ukrainian-controlled territory of the Donetsk region this year, the Washington-based think tank Institute for the Study of War (ISW) concluded in an assessment based primarily on open-source intelligence.

The Russian campaign centred on a deep pincer movement targeting what the report calls the “fortress belt” — a defensive and logistical network protecting the Ukrainian-held portion of the Donbas — advancing from Lyman in the north and Dobropillia in the south-west, with both arms intended to link up near Barvinkove in the Kharkiv region.

Completing such an encirclement would have gone a long way towards Moscow’s longstanding objective of occupying all of Donetsk region and would have given the Kremlin something to show Russian society for the costs of the war.

Ukraine’s counter-offensive on the Lyman axis, dubbed Operation Vivaldi, dismantled the northern arm of that pincer.

Ukrainian forces advanced along the Ridkodub–Nove–Novomykhailivka line in the north and the Yarova–Drobysheve–Stavky line in the south, collapsing the salient Russia had built to threaten Sloviansk from the north-east and forcing Russian units onto the defensive.

Commander of Ukraine’s 3rd Army Corps Andrii Biletskyi said the first three phases of the operation in mid- to late September liberated 176 square kilometres of territory.

Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy put the figure at between 125 and 140 square kilometres as of 3 October, adding that Russian losses had risen sharply over the preceding two to three months.

ISW suggested the operation may have been more successful than Kyiv’s official figures reflect.

The defeat of the northern pincer forced Russia to abandon its plans to advance on Sloviansk from the north-east and deprived it of positions intended to threaten northern approaches to the fortress belt.

Ukraine’s retained control over the Lyman area also preserved key supply routes. Russian units on other parts of the front may have been redeployed to reinforce the northern sector.

Russia’s south-western advance from the Pokrovsk–Dobropillia area — the surviving arm of the original encirclement — now faces far more ambitious objectives on its own.

ISW regards the collapse of the northern pincer as an operational failure, as Russia has not just lost ground around Lyman but also a key component of the campaign plan for the fortress belt.

Russian forces retain limited gains around Kostyantynivka and east of Sloviansk but have struggled to sustain the pace of advance in both areas.

ISW argued that their reliance on infantry infiltration, drones and bombardment can produce gradual attrition but cannot generate the fast mechanised manoeuvres required to collapse Ukraine’s defences at the scale Moscow needs.

The situation remains fluid, the institute added, and Russian forces will probably adapt over time, but for now, Ukraine appears to have halted the advance.

A further Russian mobilisation and technological adaptation could alter the balance, although neither would necessarily enable a breakthrough.

Iran's Revolutionary Guard warned that vessels “violating” its designated routes would be pursued and "punished" anywhere in the region, as maritime agencies reported two further strikes on commercial ships in the Persian Gulf.
Iran's Revolutionary Guard claimed responsibility on Friday for setting an oil tanker ablaze in the Strait of Hormuz and threatened to pursue vessels it deems in violation beyond the strait and across the wider region, as maritime security agencies reported two further strikes on commercial ships in the Persian Gulf.

The IRGC Navy said it targeted the engine room and propulsion system of the MV Sun Shine, a tanker belonging to the company Natwit, after the vessel attempted to use what it described as an unauthorised route through the strait's southern section. The attack caused a fire on board.

In a statement, the IRGC warned that action against "violating" vessels would no longer be confined to the Strait of Hormuz and that any ship using what it deems unauthorised routes would be pursued and "punished" anywhere in the region.

Companies it accuses of co-operating with the US would be sanctioned and all their vessels targeted, it added.

The IRGC laid blame for the incidents in the strait on the US military, accusing Washington of interfering in the decisions of shipping and insurance companies and paying "large bribes" to induce compliance.

"Direct responsibility for these incidents and the escalation of tensions in maritime transport in the region lies with the aggressive US army," the statement said.

Tehran's Friday prayer leader separately told his weekly sermon that the strait would remain closed until Iran's conditions were fully met.

Hojjatoleslam Mohammad Javad Ali-Akbari said Iran had set out "seven conditions" and would decide its next steps once they were met, adding that military readiness had improved, weak points had been addressed, and Iran's strategy had shifted from defence to offence.

Iran has said it controls the Strait of Hormuz and will not permit military forces from hostile countries in the area, vowing to respond "decisively" to any attempt to disrupt its management of the strait.

US Central Command said on Friday it had cleared the strait's main shipping lanes, removed naval mines from principal transit routes, and declared them open to all vessels not in violation of US maritime sanctions against Iran.

The competing claims came as the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre reported that an LPG carrier was struck by unknown projectiles on Friday morning, 13 nautical miles west of Al-Jazira in the United Arab Emirates, causing a major fire that was later brought under control.

UKMTO had reported the previous day that an oil tanker north of Qatari waters was struck by several projectiles resulting in casualties, in the first such attack in six months.

In a separate incident on Thursday, the maritime intelligence company Vanguard reported that a tanker had caught fire roughly 40 kilometres off Fujairah in the Gulf of Oman, identifying the vessel as probably the Dalkut, sailing under the Marshall Islands flag.

At least 12 attacks, attempted attacks or harassment incidents targeting oil tankers and gas carriers were recorded in the strait between 28 September and 5 October, the highest weekly figure since the war began on 28 February, according to media reports.

The Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil and gas shipments normally pass, has been effectively closed since the outbreak of the war, following Iranian missile and drone strikes on commercial vessels and a US Navy blockade of Iranian ports.

The latest data indicate that oil exports from other countries in the region, excluding Iran, have returned to pre-war levels in recent days.

Updated: 09/10/2026 - 16:01 GMT+2
A young orangutan has been rescued from wildfires in Borneo. Named Novita, the three- to four-year-old female was found clinging to a tree in Central Kalimantan on 26 September, with burns to her hands and feet after crossing the flames.
… More
Rescuers from the Borneo Orangutan Survival Foundation tranquilised her and gave her oxygen and intravenous fluids before taking her to the Nyaru Menteng rehabilitation centre in Palangkaraya.

On Thursday, officials said her burns were improving, but warned she might not make a full recovery.

Novita is among dozens of orangutans rescued from fires across Sumatra and Borneo in recent months. The fires have intensified during Indonesia's dry season amid drought linked to El Niño. Government figures put the area burned between January and July at more than 202,000 hectares; satellite monitoring by Nusantara Atlas estimated nearly 896,000 hectares had burned by the end of August.

The fires are destroying habitat and worsening regional haze. Smoke has also caused respiratory symptoms among orangutans at the BOS Foundation's care facility in Central Kalimantan.

Updated: 09/10/2026 - 14:45 GMT+2
Australia's scientists have trained seals and sea lions to take hearing tests at Sydney's Taronga Zoo, hoping to better understand how human-made noise affects marine mammals.
… More
Two sea lions and two fur seals learned to press a paddle whenever they heard a tone, receiving fish as a reward for correct responses. The study, reported on 9 October, found the animals were most sensitive to sounds around 3.2 kilohertz. Researchers say the results provide a starting point for assessing noise from ships, aircraft and coastal construction.

The findings are particularly important for the endangered Australian sea lion, whose population is estimated at just 12,000. These animals rely on hearing to communicate, find food, navigate and detect danger.

Lead researcher Christine Erbe, from Curtin University, said scientists first needed to establish which sounds the animals could hear before measuring their impact. The results could help guide environmental assessments and noise controls as maritime industries expand. Researchers now plan to investigate how underwater noise affects the animals' hearing and behaviour.

Tens of thousands marched in Madrid to mourn pensioner Maricarmen Abascal, whose eviction made her a housing crisis symbol and sparked fresh protests.
… More
Crowds filled the streets of Madrid on Thursday, 8 October, in solidarity with 87-year-old María del Carmen Abascal, known as Maricarmen, whose eviction has sparked widespread anger over Spain's housing crisis. She died in hospital on Wednesday, two weeks after being removed from her home. Her death reignited anger over housing costs and evictions, amid a political crisis that led to a snap election being called for 29 November.

Thousands marched from Puerta del Sol to her former home, carrying her portrait and a funeral wreath. Many walked in silence; at Cibeles, they observed a minute's silence.

Hundreds have been camping in Puerta del Sol, the centre of the housing protest movement. Madrid Tenants' Union spokesperson Alicia del Río praised Abascal's determination to defend her rights. Demonstrators said her struggle had inspired people across generations and vowed to keep campaigning for affordable housing.

The Russian president met Pezeshkian in Turkmenistan and pledged to do “everything in our power” to end the ongoing war between the US and Iran, despite Russia not being among the official mediators.
Vladimir Putin pledged to do "everything in our power" to help end the war between Iran and the US on Thursday, positioning Russia as a would-be Middle East mediator while continuing his own full-scale invasion of Ukraine, now in its fifth year.

The Russian president made the remarks at a bilateral meeting with his Iranian counterpart Masoud Pezeshkian in Avazeh, on Turkmenistan's Caspian coast, praising Tehran for making what he called "genuine efforts" to resolve the war.

Pezeshkian said Iran and the US had exchanged proposals to end the war and reopen the Strait of Hormuz, and that Tehran was reviewing and amending them before consolidating them through mediators. Despite ongoing US attacks, he said Iran would not leave the negotiating table.

"We are talking, but every time we negotiate with the United States, they attack again," Pezeshkian said. "Nevertheless, we will not abandon the negotiating table and dialogue."

He said the revisions, once finalised, would be put to mediators. "We are supposed to sit down through the existing mediators and consolidate them," he said.

It was the seventh meeting between the two presidents since Pezeshkian took office, following a phone call on 7 October — Putin's birthday — in which the pair discussed regional developments and the Iran-US negotiations.

The two previously met in person on 1 September on the sidelines of the Shanghai Co-operation Organisation summit in Bishkek.

Russia is not a mediator in the US-Iran negotiations. So far, Qatar and Pakistan have led the talks.

Military, diplomatic and economic relations between Iran and Russia have strengthened since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in early 2022.

The Iran war, which began with joint US-Israeli strikes on 28 February, has sparked more frequent consultations between Moscow and Tehran on political and material support and financial co-operation.

Since 2022, Iran has supplied Russia with Shahed drone technology — which Moscow has since copied to create its own Geran-type variant, a key tool in its daily attacks on Ukrainian civilian and infrastructure targets — and short-range missiles.

Moscow also faces accusations of providing Tehran with targeting information for attacks on US interests in the Middle East.

Trade between the two countries rose by more than 21% in 2025 and continued on the same trajectory through the first seven months of 2026, Putin said, adding that relations were developing under a comprehensive strategic partnership agreement signed early last year.

Pezeshkian described the current situation as an opportunity to deepen co-operation and said Tehran and Moscow should coordinate "with greater speed and strength."

Iranian state-run media reported in the days before the meeting that Russia was ready to extend a $1 billion loan to Tehran, described as the first tranche of a $20 billion package agreed at a meeting between Supreme National Security Council secretary Ali Larijani and Putin.

The semi-official IRGC-affiliated Fars news agency said arrangements to disburse the sum were complete, but disagreements among Iranian authorities over how to spend the money prevented Tehran from providing the account number needed to complete the transfer.

Former UN human rights commissioner Navanethem “Navi” Pillay has won the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize for her efforts to promote peace and international law, the Norwegian Nobel Committee announced.

The 85-year-old said her first reaction to hearing the news was to think it must be a joke. “Being a lawyer and a judge, I still want to verify this,” she said.

Originally from Durban, South Africa, Pillay has spent decades working in international law and human rights. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, she presided over the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda, which investigated and prosecuted those responsible for the 1994 genocide.

More recently, she served on the Independent International Commission of Inquiry on the Occupied Palestinian Territory, established by the UN Human Rights Council in 2021.

Two missile strikes on King Khalid International Airport forced diplomats to shelter in place and delayed dozens of flights, as Yemen’s government claimed progress in its Red Sea counter-offensive. Houthi forces said they were holding their ground.
Three people including an airline pilot were killed in attacks on Riyadh's international airport on Thursday, Saudi Arabia said, after Yemen's Houthis claimed a series of attacks targeting the facility.

The Saudi civil aviation authority said two missile attacks on Thursday on the Saudi capital's airport and a Saudia Airlines aircraft there "resulted in the martyrdom of three citizens".

The national flag carrier on Friday confirmed the death of the plane's captain Hamoud Ali Alkalthami in an incident at King Khalid International Airport, expressing "profound sadness" and "heartfelt condolences," and stating it honoured "his memory with deep respect and appreciation."

The missile strikes on Thursday forced diplomats stationed in the Saudi capital to shelter in place and international schools to take safety measures, as explosions echoed across the city.

Numerous flights at Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport were delayed or cancelled, while the airport advised travellers of the "necessity" to contact airlines before their trips.

The kingdom's civil defence agency also said "interception debris" had damaged a kindergarten and a medical complex in Riyadh. It did not say if there were casualties there or provide details of the damage.

Admissions by Saudi authorities of civilian targets being hit are relatively rare. There have been few statements on Houthi attacks in recent weeks.

The Houthis have also warned workers to evacuate Saudi Arabia's oil facilities, having days before hit a major refinery south of the capital.

The Houthis, whose heartlands are in Yemen's northwest, had already controlled the capital Sana'a and most of the country's major population centres since the start of the war in 2014.

Yet Saudi Arabia and the Yemeni government forces it backs have struggled to contain the Houthis' latest offensive, despite a pushback aimed at recapturing Red Sea territory including the area around the Bab el-Mandeb strait that is crucial to global energy flows.

After declaring its counter-offensive against the Houthis, Yemen's government claimed to have pushed the fighters out of strategic areas on the Red Sea coast and the port city of Mocha.

But the group formally known as Ansar Allah insists it is holding its territory, and military sources on both sides have not reported any major changes on the ground.

Claims by the warring parties have been impossible to independently verify, with the situation moving fast and access for journalists difficult.

In Yemen, one of the world's poorest countries, ordinary civilians are paying the price for the renewed fighting, with more than 200,000 people displaced since July, according to UN data.

In Yemen's Taiz, the Houthis are laying siege to the government-controlled city in the country's west, depriving residents of their main supply route to the government's temporary capital in Aden.

"There is a crisis in everything: water, gas, vegetables, everything essential", said Taiz resident Um Rian.

Hitler Mussolini Simeón Flores won by 25 votes in Queropalca, a district of just over 730 people in the Huánuco department, running for the centre-left Ahora Nación party. He is not Peru’s first elected Hitler.
Peruvian man named Hitler Mussolini has been elected mayor of a small Andean district after winning a local election by a narrow margin on a left-wing party ticket.

Hitler Mussolini Simeón Flores, 45, won the mayoral race in Queropalca, a district in the province of Lauricocha in the Huánuco department north of Lima, running for the centre-left Ahora Nación party.

He received 229 votes — 34.5% of the 663 valid ballots in Queropalca — ahead of his closest rival, Gaspar Benilde Osorio Zambrano of the Popular Renewal Peru party, who took 204 votes, or 30.8%.

The district has an estimated population of just over 730, according to 2024 official data.

Simeón Flores also manages the Simeon Sac investment group in Pillco Marca, in the same department.

The vote was part of regional and municipal elections held across Peru following right-wing firebrand Keiko Sofía Fujimori's victory in the country's presidential election.

This is not the first time a Hitler has won over the voters in the South American country.

Centre-right We Are Peru party's Hitler Alba served as mayor of another small Andean town of Yungar between 2011 and 2014.

In 2018, he went up against Lennin Vladimir Rodriguez Valverde, who tried to challenge the validity of his candidacy. Alba won against other candidates, garnering 47.7% of the vote.

Alba, who dubbed himself "the good Hitler," campaigned under the slogans "Hitler returns" and "Hitler with the people".

He said at the time that his father did not know about the Nazi German dictator's legacy when he named him.

Lawmaker and surgeon Hitler Saavedra served in Peru's Congress as a member of We Are Peru from 2021 until his death in 2024 at the age of 46.

It is not uncommon for Peruvians to choose historic or otherwise unusual names for their children regardless of negative or controversial connotations.

South Africa saw violent anti-immigration protests in Soweto and Durban on Thursday, 8 October, after a Constitutional Court ruling eased restrictions on asylum applications.
… More
Police said 10 vehicles were set alight during clashes in Soweto, near a Home Affairs office in Orlando West, Johannesburg. Shops owned by foreign nationals were also looted, while firefighters tackled burning cars and police closed nearby roads. Similar unrest broke out in Durban, where riot police were deployed after reports of looting and vehicle fires; police said 14 vehicles were torched there.

The violence followed a July Constitutional Court ruling removing barriers that prevented some migrants from applying for asylum, including those who entered South Africa irregularly. Authorities said refugee reception offices had been overwhelmed by applicants. The government pledged to expand processing capacity and strengthen identity checks, stressing that the ruling did not automatically grant refugee status.

Police reported no injuries in Soweto and warned that those responsible for violence and destruction would face prosecution.

Updated: 09/10/2026 - 9:40 GMT+2
Torrential rain caused severe flooding in Santiago, Chile, leaving homes, clinics and university buildings under water.
… More
Floodwaters surged through the streets of Santiago, carrying away cars and forcing bus passengers to climb onto the roof for safety. Las Condes, in the east of the capital, was among the worst-hit areas as mud and debris rushed through residential neighbourhoods.

Emergency crews rescued people trapped in vehicles and buildings, while soldiers helped evacuate vulnerable residents. At least six people were injured, and 11 people were moved to safety from a care home for older people.

Two private clinics were flooded, with mud covering treatment rooms, stretchers and medical equipment. A nearby university campus was also affected.

Chile's disaster agency issued a red alert for 10 districts around Santiago, warning that further mudslides could threaten homes and roads.

President José Antonio Kast declared a state of catastrophe in Las Condes, enabling the deployment of additional rescue teams and military equipment. Schools closed in affected areas as authorities urged residents to limit travel.

The storm came after days of heavy rain across central Chile. Officials were also investigating a death south of the capital, although its connection to the flooding had not been established.

Musk received the award alongside Google cofounder Sergey Brin, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, and AMD chief Lisa Su.

"I want to thank everybody for being here as we celebrate the exciting advance of science and technology in the golden age of America," Trump said in his opening remarks, before turning his attention to Musk, who he called an "industrial titan" and "our modern-day Thomas Edison."

"Few Americans in history have done more to advance America's national interest than Elon in one field after another. His genius and determination have made science fiction into reality," Trump said.

"Elon, I want to congratulate you. You're my friend and all of that, but you are a very, very special guy. Thank you very much and congratulations Elon," he added.

Despite his glowing words, Trump and Musk have navigated a rather volatile relationship in recent years.

Musk joined Trump in the latter stages of his presidential campaign and went on to take a much-publicised role as defacto leader of the White House's cost-cutting Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE) following Trump's return to the White House in January 2025, spearheading efforts to make the government leaner.

But the pair's relationship fractured after Musk criticised Trump's spending bill, the "One Big Beautiful Bill," prompting the start of a bitter and public feud.

However, Musk has found himself back in Trump world in recent weeks, with the Tesla CEO set to take on a role as co-director of "Project Meridian."

The project, commissioned by the Department of Defense, will study the future of warfare, helping identify the weapons and technologies the US military will need to build and master and proposing "actionable solutions to start developing, testing and fielding these capabilities."

A White House press release said Musk was awarded the National Medal of Science "for engineering achievements of extraordinary ambition and scale that have pushed the frontiers of space exploration, intelligent systems, communications, and beyond, renewing American technological leadership and opening a new chapter in American manufacturing."

Convicted military base shooter Nidal Hasan's execution will take place on 3 December at Fort Hood military base, where he committed the shooting. It will be the first US military execution carried out by a firing squad since World War II.
The firing squad execution of Fort Hood shooter Nidal Malik Hasan will be livestreamed, the Pentagon said Thursday, a controversial revelation about plans for the first military execution by firing squad since World War II after Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth had described it as being "public."

"The livestreaming of an execution is tantamount to a public execution and would breach the absolute prohibition of torture," said the UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights.

The UN body said it had made similar statements about public executions in Afghanistan and Iran, saying they "constitute a form of cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment".

Nidal Hasan, a US Army psychiatrist, shot and killed 13 people and injured 32 others at Fort Hood, Texas, in 2009.

"We are going to make sure that people are able to watch it, that it's public", this is how US Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth announced the "public" execution of convicted military base shooter Nidal Hasan.

"People need to understand that... there's serious consequences for these types of things", said Hegseth.

His execution by firing squad will be livestreamed on 3 December; it will be the first execution by the US military in more than 65 years, and the first carried out by firing squad since World War II.

"I kind of like the mentality we had in World War II, which was win, which was understand when the enemy attacks you, you need to stand your ground", Hegseth said.

Officials in the Pentagon clarified Thursday that the execution would be livestreamed and that more details would follow.

The revelation from Hegseth about the public nature of the execution has raised further questions about how the death penalty is carried out in the US, especially after the botched lethal injection of Christa Pike in Tennessee.

In an interview with right-wing influencer Jack Posobiec, the Pentagon chief was asked if Hasan had made any final request. "Don't care. We'll see him on December 3", Hegseth said — the day Hasan is slated to die.

Hasan could choose to resist execution and file a habeas petition — a legal request to review the legality of a detention — in civilian court, which could significantly delay the execution, experts said.

"It frankly might outlast the current administration, and who knows if the next one will have the same penchant for executions", said Eugene R. Fidell, a visiting lecturer at Yale Law School specialising in military law.

Deborah Denno, a death penalty expert and law professor at Fordham University, said livestreaming an execution could make the process "much more transparent" and inform the public about all that capital punishment entails.

Those are benefits that she said would be useful at a time when Pike’s botched execution has raised fresh questions about the manner by which criminal defendants are put to death for their crimes. "I think it could possibly be a good idea because it would be transparent and the public would be fully aware that it’s happening", she said.

When many states have had difficulty carrying out lethal injections due to difficulty accessing drugs or finding medical professionals willing to participate in executions, firing squads are coming to be seen as a less inhumane alternative, said Brenner Fissell, a professor at Villanova Law and vice president of the National Institute of Military Justice.

Russia slashes cancer care as defence spending hits post-Soviet record
Russia cuts cancer care spending amid war with Ukraine

The federal cancer programme budget falls from 44.9 billion to 3.87 billion roubles in 2027 — a nearly 40-fold reduction against 2025 levels — as defence spending reaches 35% of all federal expenditure, the highest share since the Soviet Union collapsed.
Russia is cutting funding for its national cancer programme nearly twelvefold in 2027, from 44.9 billion roubles (€470.5m) to 3.87 billion roubles (€40.5m), according to the draft federal budget, as defence spending reaches its highest share since the collapse of the Soviet Union.

The reduction is even sharper compared to last year: the programme received 148.5 billion roubles (€1.5bn) in 2025, meaning federal spending on cancer control would have fallen roughly 38-fold in two years if the budget passes as proposed.

Funding is projected to recover partially thereafter — rising to 5.76 billion roubles in 2028 and 12.78 billion in 2029 — but both figures remain far below current levels.

Since its launch, the federal cancer project has funded diagnostic equipment, cancer centres, and early screening programmes.

Under the new 2027-2029 budget, the remaining money is expected to go mainly to diagnostic and radiology equipment, while specialised units in smaller towns and rural areas face cuts, a distribution likely to deepen an already significant gap between cancer care in major federal centres and outlying regions.

Officials have said some costs will be transferred to the compulsory health insurance system (OMS) and other budget lines rather than eliminated entirely.

Patient organisations and some experts have questioned whether the new mechanisms could fully offset the federal reduction, and noted that shifting costs to regional budgets and the OMS ties the quality of care more directly to the financial strength of individual regions.

The proposed cuts come as new data show Russian cancer patients are already bearing a growing share of treatment costs.

A study by the NGO Movement Against Cancer found that between 2019 and 2025, patients bought roughly one in every six or seven packs of cancer medicines out of pocket. In 2025 alone, household spending on anti-tumour drugs rose from 8.8 billion to 11.4 billion roubles, and the number of packs bought independently increased from five million to 5.8 million.

Around 94% of those drugs are on the list of essential medicines and should be provided free of charge.

The study's authors attribute the out-of-pocket spending not to patients choosing more expensive options — most purchases involved generics — but to supply disruptions and gaps in provision.

Russian law guarantees cancer patients free access to the medicines they need, but in practice, patients and doctors have long reported procurement delays and regional budget shortfalls.

State procurement remains substantial: official figures show the government bought 32.8 million packs of medicines worth 336 billion roubles through the OMS system in 2025. But the trajectory in out-of-pocket spending suggests the burden on patients is already rising ahead of the planned federal cuts.

Defence spending in the 2027 draft budget is set to reach 17.1 trillion roubles, or roughly 35% of all federal expenditure — a post-Soviet record.

More than four years of Moscow's all-out war in Ukraine have progressively shifted Russia's public spending, with civilian programmes including healthcare and medical infrastructure taking a backseat to continued expansion of defence outlays.

Authorities have placed 10 other Chilean districts on high alert as risks of potential mudslides and rain-related emergencies threaten to batter the region.
Heavy rains battered Chile’s capital Santiago and its surrounding regions on Thursday, triggering mudslides that swept cars through streets, flooded homes, forced residents to evacuate and injured at least six people, according to authorities.

Chilean President José Antonio Kast declared a state of emergency in Las Condes, a wealthy suburb east of Santiago, giving government agencies more power to respond as torrential rains turned key roads into impassable waterways.

The district's mayor, Catalina San Martín, said 14.4 centimetres of rain had fallen in recent days — about nine times the average October rainfall — leaving the area “totally drenched.”

Chile’s disaster-response agency put 10 other districts on high alert for more landslides and ordered schools to close on Friday.

Rain storms earlier this week had already damaged a few dozen homes across central Chile and left nearly 12,000 customers in Santiago without power, officials said. The downpours were expected to ease on Friday.

No deaths were reported on Thursday as a result of the heavy rains. The injured however included a one-year-old child, who suffered broken bones. Firefighters said they'd rescued or evacuated more than 1,000 people.

The naturally recurring warming of tropical Pacific waters scrambles rainfall patterns worldwide and often brings wetter springs to the Southern Hemisphere.

The World Meteorological Organization said on Thursday that this year’s El Niño was expected to peak in December and could be among the most intense since at least 1950.

Eyewitnesses say they saw a few masked officers wearing Department of Homeland Security branded clothes surrounding a man on the ground who appeared injured. They say they heard three or four gunshots being fired.
A federal immigration officer shot and wounded a Dominican man while arresting him in New York City on Thursday, during what the Department of Homeland Security called a “targeted enforcement operation.”

Protesters and local officials decried it as an example of the dangers of the Trump administration’s immigration crackdown.

The man survived and is being treated at a hospital, the agency said. It did not describe exactly what led to the shooting, saying only that “during the incident, an ICE officer discharged his weapon” and that the man was injured.

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani and New York Governor Kathy Hochul said they had received reports the agents fired at the man while he was inside a vehicle with a 5-year-old child, who was apparently unhurt.

Hochul said she had urged US President Donald Trump and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin by phone to halt immigration enforcement actions in New York.

Mamdani said in a statement he also wanted ICE actions to stop in the city, calling the shooting “unconscionable and incredibly serious.”

The gunfire erupted around 4 pm local time in Marble Hill, a small part of Manhattan that is attached to the Bronx borough.

Pedro Almaraz, an employee at a nearby restaurant, said he heard about three or four gunshots and saw a man on the ground surrounded by several officers wearing face masks and vests with Department of Homeland Security branding. Witnesses, he said, were screaming.

Bystander videos and photos posted on social media showed a man sitting on the ground with his hands behind his back, in the immediate aftermath of the shooting. Another video shows New York City police officers picking up an apparently injured man and carrying him into a police vehicle.

Nearby, a damaged car sat blocking the roadway with small holes in two of its windows. The videos show agents wearing ICE badges on the scene, including one carrying a rifle.

Protesters at the scene chanted, “ICE off our streets now!” and then marched to the hospital where the injured man was being treated.

A sizable crowd blocked an intersection at the hospital entry, and as police began to set barricades so that ambulances and other vehicles could pass, some protesters shoved past the barriers and hurled water bottles at officers.

DHS complained that its officers “are forced to make arrests at large in the community in unpredictable and dangerous environments” when “dangerous illegal aliens" get released.

The agency blamed “reckless sanctuary policies” in Democrat-led areas. There is no clear definition of sanctuary jurisdictions, but the term is generally applied to state and local governments that limit cooperation with federal immigration authorities.

The agency said the man who was shot had a prior grand larceny conviction and a history of other arrests. New York police arrested him in December 2023, and he was released, DHS added.

ICE agents have fired their guns during more than a dozen vehicle stops since last year, killing at least four people and wounding others.

Kyiv says it is open to “constructive dialogue” with Seoul despite its decision to recall its ambassador. South Korea’s decision comes just days after its president threatened to take punitive action against Zelenskyy’s violation of the non-disclosure agreement.
South Korea said on Thursday it will recall its ambassador to Ukraine, stepping up its protest against President Volodymyr Zelenskyy’s recent revelation of a supposedly secret transfer of two North Korean prisoners of war to Seoul.

The South Korean announcement came days after its President Lee Jae Myung threatened to take unspecified punitive steps if Ukraine refuses to apologise over the revelation.

The Foreign Ministry in Seoul did not say when ambassador Park Ki-chang will be brought back home but acknowledged in a statement that it will continue consultations with Ukraine, adding that it hopes the dispute between the two countries would be resolved smoothly.

Ukrainian Foreign Ministry spokesperson Heorhii Tykhyi said Kyiv remains open to “constructive dialogue” despite Seoul’s decision to recall its ambassador.

South Korea and Ukraine became embroiled in a diplomatic row after Zelenskyy spoke about the transfer of the North Korean soldiers during his UN speech on 23 September.

Seoul has accused Kyiv of violating a non-disclosure agreement on their transfer in mid-September, but Kyiv has denied agreeing to such a deal.

The soldiers were among thousands of North Korean troops deployed to support Russia’s war against Ukraine. Since their capture in early 2025, the soldiers had expressed their desires to resettle in South Korea, because they could face persecution if repatriated home.

South Korea had said it would accept them, because it legally regards the North as part of its territory and has a policy of accepting and financially supporting North Korean defectors.

South Korean officials said the non-disclosure agreement was made because they were concerned about the safety of the North Korean soldiers and their relatives in the North.

But many experts say South Korea's liberal government likely also worries the disclosure of their arrival may hurt its efforts to improve ties with North Korea.

North Korea's state media hasn't commented on the soldiers' transfer, but the North previously reacted with fury to major defections of its citizens, accusing South Korea of kidnapping them.

Pyongyang has shunned any talks with Seoul and the US and focused on bolstering its nuclear programme since 2019.

It's unclear what other steps South Korea would take. Experts say maintaining good relations with Ukraine is in South Korean national interests because Kyiv could provide Seoul with intelligence about North Korea's expanding cooperation with Russia and its battlefield experiences.

They say cooperation with Ukraine is important for South Korea's partnerships with many Western countries supporting Ukraine over the war.

Observers say Zelenskyy disclosed the transfer of the North Korean POWs to expose the danger of North Korea's military support to Russia as a way to win more international assistance.

South Korea has provided $445 million (€396 million) in humanitarian assistance to Ukraine since 2022. In July, Lee pledged $100 million (€89 million) in aid to Ukraine in a meeting with Zelenskyy.
