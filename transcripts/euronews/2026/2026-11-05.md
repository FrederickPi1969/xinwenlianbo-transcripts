Russell Brand was convicted of four sexual offences, one rape and three sexual assaults, involving four women between 2005 and 2009 in different locations.
British comedian Russell Brand has been found guilty of rape and three sexual assaults by a London jury on Thursday, the offences involving four women in total.

Eyes closed when the verdict was delivered, Brand showed no emotion as he was also cleared of two rapes and one count of attempted indecent assault.

The 51-year-old actor who turned into a conservative, religious online guru faces "significant custodial term," as said by Judge Joel Bennathan.

Brand's wife Laura watched from the public gallery as the jury at London's Southwark Crown Court delivered their four unanimous guilty verdicts. He was remanded in custody and will be sentenced on Monday. The maximum sentence in the UK for rape is life imprisonment.

Briefly married to US pop star Katy Perry, Brand reportedly also faces accusations of raping two other women, including a 16-year-old schoolgirl. These allegations were revealed during previous hearings of his four-week trial, thus not included in the trial.

After the verdicts, the Metropolitan Police paid tribute to the "extraordinary courage, resilience and determination of the women who came forward and reported what happened to them."

"Brand used his influence, status and fame to exploit and sexually offend against women," the Met's Detective Superintendent Andy Furphy said in a statement.

"As his profile grew, so too did his sense of entitlement. I hope this conviction sends a clear message that no one is above the law, no matter how famous or powerful they may be," Furphy added.

Senior prosecutor David Roberts said outside the court Thursday that Brand's behaviour was "predatory" and that he "used his celebrity status to commit sexual crimes."

"He was a manipulator with an insatiable desire to get what he wanted regardless of whether consent was given or not," Roberts added.

His offences involved four women between 2005 and 2009 in different locations, a radio station, the set of a DVD recording and Brand's home. He had pleaded not guilty to all the charges saying that although he used to be promiscuous he had always had consensual sex.

The prosecution had earlier described him as a "ruthless, calculated and manipulative" sexual predator. Following the guilty verdicts, Brand's lawyer Ian Winter urged the judge to renew his bail over the weekend as "an act of humanity" and to "put his affairs in order and in particular to say goodbye to his three young children."

But Judge Bennathan refused to grant bail, saying Brand had "considerable resources" and whatever his good intentions might be, there was "fear he might in the end not feel able to come along and face his sentence."

From the dock, Brand signalled to his defence team that he was okay, adding: "I'm good, I'm good, I'm good" before being led out of the dock.

Thousands of pupils, students and teachers marched through central Athens on Thursday in a nationwide push for better-funded schools, joining a protest wave that has spread from France across southern Europe.
… More
The rally began at 11am outside the University of Athens, then wound to Syntagma Square, where crowds gathered in front of parliament behind police lines. Protesters carried banners declaring "From Greece to France, we fight for free public education" and placards bearing the French slogan "Étudier ou survivre?" ("Study or survive?"), borrowed from France's school student protest movement. Outside parliament, some dumped sacks of rubble from deteriorating schools to highlight the conditions pupils face, from leaking roofs and crumbling plaster to broken radiators.

Police put turnout at about 4,000 across Athens and Thessaloniki combined, well below the 20,000 who marched in 50 Greek cities on 21 October; organisers disputed the figure.

The demands are concrete: more state funding, urgent inspections and repairs of school buildings, permanent posts to fill thousands of vacancies, smaller classes, and withdrawal of new national exams and of a proposal to remove Article 16 of the constitution, which protects public higher education. The secondary teachers' union, OLME, staged a four-hour walkout from 10am so staff could join.

Behind it lies the cost of living. Students describe rents of 800 euros a month and say many can no longer afford to study at all, with unions demanding a monthly housing allowance and rent caps.

The decree transferred JTI’s four factories, around 4,000 employees and roughly 40% of the Russian cigarette market to a state-linked holding company. The latest seizure added to a list that includes Nestlé, Auchan, Leroy Merlin and Metro.
Russian President Vladimir Putin placed Japan Tobacco International's (JTI) operations under state control on Thursday, ordering their transfer by decree to a state-linked holding company AO Promyshlennye aktivy.

The order covers JTI's factories in St Petersburg, Rostov-on-Don, the Lipetsk region and the Leningrad region.

The company employs around 4,000 people in Russia and holds roughly 40% of the Russian cigarette market, making it the country's largest manufacturer.

Its Russian product range includes the international brands Winston, Glamour, Camel, LD and Sobranie, and the domestic labels Pyotr I, Russky Stil and Donskoi Tabak.

JTI, whose headquarters are in Geneva, entered the Russian market in 1999 and acquired local producer Donskoi Tabak in 2018.

JT Group has invested $4.6bn in its Russian operations over more than 20 years, according to a company profile updated in November 2025.

After Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, the company did not leave the country but suspended new investment in Russian production and marketing.

The seizure follows Russia's arrest last month of Yuki Imai, a journalist with the Japanese newspaper Hokkaido Shimbun, on espionage charges.

The Russian Foreign Ministry has accused Tokyo of systematically using journalists to gather intelligence inside the country. Japan has rejected the accusation.

Japan, which has a long-standing dispute with Russia over the Chishima Islands in the Pacific Ocean, has also joined some Western sanctions over the ongoing all-out war in Ukraine.

JTI joins a list of European companies whose Russian assets have been transferred to state management under the same mechanism, including Swiss food group Nestlé, French retailers Auchan and Leroy Merlin, and German retailer Metro.

Moscow has presented the external management process as a response to sanctions imposed after the 2022 invasion.

Restrictions introduced for companies from states deemed "unfriendly" include caps on dividend payments, which trap profits inside Russia, and substantial fees on sales of Russian business assets.

Putin said on 1 October the mechanism also gave Russia the ability to prepare retaliatory measures against any possible future nationalisation of Russian assets abroad, while leaving open the possibility of returning control to former owners if "conditions improved".

External management involves appointing state-linked administrators to run a company’s Russian assets without the foreign owner’s consent or compensation, transferring effective control to the Russian state regardless of who retains nominal ownership.

The accounting black hole at Ueno Bank wipes out public funds, leaves thousands without access to their deposits and drives Paraguay into its worst institutional crisis in years.
A sudden blank screen startled the almost three million customers of Ueno Bank in the early hours of Tuesday 3 November. At around 03:24, the app of the Paraguayan institution, a key player in the system that manages state funds worth millions and has grown at breakneck speed in its three years of existence, stopped displaying users' accounts and showed a single message: "We cannot display the balance".

At the same time, the Central Bank of Paraguay issued a tough statement on its social networks: the institution was being taken over and its liquidation process was beginning. This "resolution procedure" for Ueno Bank was triggered by a broader financial alarm after a critical collapse in its solvency ratio was detected, which had fallen below 50%.

The impact was immediate and set off an unprecedented political earthquake that has ended the government of Santiago Peña. Since Tuesday, crowds of angry users have gathered around the headquarters in Asunción and the country's 75 branches in an attempt to save their money.

The scene outside the branches, closed and guarded, has turned into one of extreme tension and desperation. The distress in front of the branches translated into daily emergencies that quickly became unsustainable. Some people, in tears, begged to withdraw their money to pay for medicine for a seriously ill child; a few kilometres away, a construction worker was just as desperate when he realised that, without access to his savings, he would not even be able to buy food for his children that day.

The bank held around 850 million dollars (about 758 million euros) in public funds, including money from the Social Security Institute that underpins pensions and healthcare for workers. The spectre of an Argentine-style corralito immediately set off alarm bells among the population. "Who will answer for a lifetime of savings? Will there be impunity?" read the placards held by demonstrators standing their ground outside the bank's offices.

The whole country held its breath ahead of the press conference by the Central Bank of Paraguay (BCP). The official confirmation was categorical: "We proceeded to close the institution; neither the app, nor the cards, nor any service is active from today," said board member Liana Caballero. It was of no use that the authorities tried to calm tempers by reminding people that the law provides for a guarantee fund to reimburse each client up to 75 minimum wages (around 228 million guaraníes or 37,700 dollars, about 33,620 euros); mistrust and suspicion had already taken hold on the streets.

The opposition points to the BCP leadership as accomplices by omission in the fraud, but the coup de grâce is aimed directly at the head of state. Santiago Peña has found himself in the eye of the storm: at the request of 35 lawmakers, the Chamber of Deputies was to meet tomorrow in an extraordinary session to vote on whether to open impeachment proceedings against him, but President Santiago Peña has decided to resign beforehand.

The indictment accuses Peña of "poor performance of his duties", backed by serious charges ranging from conflict of interest and alleged embezzlement of pension funds to giving institutional protection to the Central Bank. In the midst of the storm, the president cancelled his entire official agenda on Wednesday and chose to remain silent until late in the afternoon, when he reappeared online to announce the departure of the head of the central bank and to call on the courts to establish responsibility.

The legal net closed in on Wednesday with the arrest of eight senior executives of the bank, including its chief executive, Miguel Vázquez. At the same time, the knock-on effects quickly hit the real economy: the Association of Importers and Traders warned of an immediate 35% slump in sales at the country's major retailers. With money blocked and out of reach, domestic consumption ground to a halt overnight.

In a country of just over six million inhabitants, the Ueno Bank app went far beyond the financial sphere. Owned by the influential Grupo Vázquez and instantly recognisable for its fluorescent green look, the bank boasted that it had buried the heavy bureaucracy of the country's traditional agrarian republic. Its presence was ubiquitous in the media and in the sponsorship of the country's most important football clubs, such as Cerro Porteño and Olimpia, as well as the iconic Defensores del Chaco stadium.

Only a few days earlier, the bank had been flaunting an ambitious 100 million dollar capitalisation plan. However, behind the advertising façade and sporting patronage, the Superintendency of Banks uncovered a grim and unsustainable accounting reality: the institution was unable to meet a legal reserve requirement of 70 million dollars (62.427 millones de euros) demanded by the central bank. The financial collapse was a matter of numbers, and the ensuing explosion, pure political dynamite.

An economist with a technocratic profile and a former official of the International Monetary Fund, Santiago Peña came to power in 2023 promising to turn the country into a magnet for foreign capital. Although under his mandate Paraguay has posted one of the strongest rates of economic growth in South America, the sudden banking collapse has plunged the president into the most critical moment of his term.

Seoul said pirates have recently ventured further from the Somali coast and added that it wanted to send a patrol aircraft to protect merchant ships off Oman while keeping its forces out of the ongoing Iran war.
South Korea plans to extend its naval patrols from the Gulf of Aden to waters near Oman after a rise in piracy off Somalia's coast, its defence ministry said on Thursday.

The navy’s Cheonghae Unit has escorted merchant ships off Somalia since 2009. Under the plan, it would also patrol near Duqm, a port in Oman about 650 kilometres from the Strait of Hormuz.

The patrols would stay outside the strait, and the unit would remain under South Korean command.

South Korea’s defence ministry said 36 piracy incidents had been reported off Somalia so far this year, up from 11 in all of 2025. It said pirates had ventured further towards Oman as international naval forces shifted to the Red Sea and Hormuz.

The navy would send a P-8A Poseidon patrol aircraft, a bomb disposal team and uncrewed craft to clear sea mines. The plan would raise the personnel limit from 320 to 395.

The navy’s current mandate expires on 31 December. Deputy ministers backed the plan on Thursday and the cabinet is due to review it next week.

If the cabinet and President Lee Jae-myung approve it, the National Assembly will vote on whether to expand the patrol area and extend the mission until 31 December 2027.

South Korea sourced about 70% of its oil from the Middle East in 2025. Most of that oil passed through the Strait of Hormuz, according to industry ministry figures.

US President Donald Trump has criticised Seoul for what he sees as insufficient support for the Iran war. Lee said in September that South Korea had a duty to protect its citizens, merchant ships and oil shipments, but ruled out a deployment that could draw the country into the war.

Seoul has backed a British and French-led plan to protect merchant ships and clear mines in the strait once conditions allow. Its defence ministry said sending mine-clearing equipment to waters off Oman would give South Korean crews experience they could use in a future mission.

A South Korean defence official told Yonhap that Seoul had agreed to take part if asked, once the war ended or Washington and Tehran reached a deal allowing crews to operate safely. Any deployment inside the strait would still need parliament’s approval.

From 2027, passengers would pay 500 roubles on domestic flights and 1,000 on international flights to fund the lease and operation of 258 Russian-built aircraft, as sactions over Moscow's all-out war in Ukraine continue to bite.
Russia is planning to impose a levy on airline tickets from 2027 to fund its struggling civilian aircraft programme, with domestic passengers paying 500 roubles (€5.4) and international passengers 1,000 roubles (€10.8) per ticket, according to the State Duma's budget and tax committee.

The Ministry of Industry and Trade is developing the measure on President Vladimir Putin's instructions. The funds would cover the costs of leasing and operating 258 aircraft under the Comprehensive Programme for Expanding the Production of Aircraft, including the MS-21, Tu-214, SJ-100, and Il-114-300 models.

Russia's civilian aircraft industry has produced no new serial aircraft since the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, repeatedly missing production deadlines.

Between 2022 and 2025, Russian airlines received 13 new aircraft against a planned 120, many assembled from pre-sanctions components.

The production timeline has been revised repeatedly. In summer 2026, the large-scale production target was pushed back from 2030 to 2035.

Certification and the start of serial production of the MS-21 and SJ-100 are currently scheduled for 2027, while deliveries of the Il-114-300, which has passed certification, are promised from December 2026.

The Russian airline fleet has shrunk during Moscow's war in Ukraine as ageing foreign-built aircraft are retired without replacement. Spare parts are scarce and increasingly expensive to source, and Russia has turned to Vietnam to obtain aircraft for lease.

Western sanctions after the February 2022 full-scale invasion cut off Russia from purchasing or leasing Western aircraft and from obtaining spare parts or maintenance services.

However, Moscow passed a law allowing airlines to re-register more than 400 foreign-leased Western aircraft domestically rather than return them to their owners.

Under international aviation law, dual registration of a single aircraft is prohibited, meaning Russia's re-registrations are illegal, but the International Civil Aviation Organisation (ICAO) has no enforcement mechanism to compel their return.

Lessors have filed insurance claims totalling around $8 billion (€7.1bn) to recover their losses, with many cases still ongoing.

Before the full-scale invasion of Ukraine, roughly 850 of Russia's 900 commercial aircraft were US and European-made Boeing or Airbus planes, of which around 85% were leased from foreign companies.

With Western spare parts and technical services now sanctioned, airlines have been cannibalising the seized fleet for components.

The main production capacity of Russia's aerospace sector has since shifted toward military aircraft. Official output figures are classified, but industry observers estimate production in the dozens per year.

In a rare event, three American astronauts voted in Tuesday's US midterm elections from the International Space Station, using advanced links to send electronic ballots to officials on Earth.
NASA astronaut Anil Menon announced that he and his colleagues Luke Delaney and Jessica Watkins had cast their votes from the space station, stressing that the process took place via a secure electronic system that ensures ballots reach election officials.

He attached a 29-second video in which he shows an “I voted” sticker and gives a tour of what he described as his own “polling station”, which in reality was his private cabin on the International Space Station. He explained that he had filled out the form on his computer and sent it to Harris County in Texas, where his vote was officially recorded.

Harris County is the usual electoral destination for NASA astronauts because it is home to the Johnson Space Center in Houston, the agency’s main centre for managing human spaceflight missions.

Despite the distance, the voting process relies on the same infrastructure that NASA uses every day to exchange data and messages with the station through its space communications and navigation programme.

According to the agency, the procedure starts with the astronaut requesting an absentee ballot using the “Federal Post Card Application”, a form also used by US military personnel serving far from home.

Once completed on board, the ballot is transmitted via NASA’s “Tracking and Data Relay Satellite System” to a ground antenna at the agency’s White Sands facility in Las Cruces, New Mexico.

From there, it is transferred to Mission Control at the Johnson Space Center before being forwarded to the relevant county election official to be registered and counted.

NASA stresses that the process is subject to strict security measures to protect the secrecy and integrity of the vote. The ballot is encrypted, and only the astronaut and the authorised election official can see its contents.

Although voting from beyond planet Earth is highly unusual, the practice itself is not new. It began in 1997, when the Texas legislature passed a law allowing NASA astronauts to vote in US elections while in orbit.

American astronaut David Wolf was the first to do so, casting his vote that same year from the Russian space station Mir, almost three years before the International Space Station welcomed its first permanent crew in November 2000.

The three astronauts who voted this year arrived on two separate missions. Delaney and Watkins launched on 1 October last year as part of SpaceX’s “Crew-13” mission, while Menon arrived in mid-July aboard a Russian Soyuz spacecraft.

They are living alongside four other crew members: Canadian astronaut Joshua Kutryk and Russian cosmonauts Anna Kikina, Pyotr Dubrov and Sergei Teteriatnikov. Kutryk and Teteriatnikov are also part of “Crew-13”, while Dubrov and Kikina arrived with Menon on the Soyuz.

Updated: 5 November 2026 - 8:02 GMT+1
Scientists at CERN have found evidence of an extraordinarily rare particle decay, offering a glimpse into a largely unexplored world known as the 'zeptoverse'.

The discovery was made using the Large Hadron Collider, which smashes protons together to produce particles that scientists can study.

Researchers focused on beauty particles, short-lived particles created in these collisions. They found evidence of an extremely rare process in which one of these particles transforms into other particles, something predicted to happen just once in every 10 billion cases.

This incredibly small scale is what scientists call the 'zeptoverse': an unexplored frontier of the subatomic world, where researchers hope to discover new fundamental particles.

Scientists believe exploring this tiny world could also help answer some of the biggest questions in physics, including the nature of dark matter and dark energy, which together account for around 95% of the universe.

In Ukraine, in Gaza, in the Sahel, children caught in the world's conflicts ask for remarkably little: a classroom, a teacher, a desk. Today, the education of 258 million school-aged children worldwide is disrupted by crises. For these children, missing out on quality education means missing out on chances in life.

Meanwhile, the aid the world provides for education is projected to fall by 24 per cent this year. In the very emergencies where education matters most, it is being neglected. This is a political failure before it is a financial one.

With major consequences: When we fail to educate children in crises, we do not merely postpone their futures – we mortgage our own. Children that are denied learning are more vulnerable to violence, recruitment into armed groups, exploitation and early marriage.

Girls are often the first to be denied access to education – and the last to return to school. And the societies that lose a generation to illiteracy do not quietly disappear; they export instability, forced displacement and conflict across borders. Excluding children from school today will deepen the security, economic and social challenges of tomorrow. In the short term, school is the most effective lifeline for children.

A classroom is a place of safety, of meals, of psychosocial support, of normality in the abnormal. Getting children back to learning within days of a disaster is the difference between trauma and recovery – and it is also the difference between hope and the loss of it.

In the long term, school is the most effective development policy there is. It is essential for peace and stability. Every additional year of education makes a lasting difference: for literacy, for livelihoods, for girls who marry later and lead sooner, for teachers who train the next generation, for communities that can absorb a flood or a war and rebuild.

That is why the work of Education Cannot Wait (ECW), the global fund for education in emergencies, is so important. Over a decade of action, ECW and its partners have enabled education for more than 16 million crisis-affected children across 33,000 schools, even in the toughest places.

Nearly 300.000 teachers – over half of them women – have been trained. And ECW has an ambitious goal: to improve access to education for another 10 million of the world's most crisis-affected children over the next four years.

As an international community, we can make it happen if we all contribute. That is why, at a time when others are stepping back from multilateralism, Germany is stepping forward. We have committed €20 million per year to ECW for 2027-2031 – contributing to its US$600 million replenishment target.

Knowing that budgets are tight, we make this commitment with open eyes. Because the evidence is unambiguous: there is no more effective way to build peace, no faster way to restore dignity, and no smarter investment in our own security than a child in a classroom.

With this commitment, Germany is sending a clear message: we strongly believe in multilateral partnerships. And we remain a reliable partner. Only through collective action can we find solutions to global challenges and ensure stability worldwide. Now we ask our partners in the G7, in Europe and beyond to do the same.

We must not let education become the silent casualty of our times. Together, we can make a difference – so that every child, everywhere, has the chance to learn and build a better future.

Hong Kong democracy activist Joshua Wong was sentenced to 10 more years in jail in the Chinese territory on Thursday for colluding with foreign powers.

Wong, who became internationally renowned during the "Umbrella Movement" that convulsed Hong Kong in 2014, has already spent almost six years behind bars and is now not due for release until late 2036.

The former student leader, who turned 30 last month, was previously expected to be freed in January after serving four years and eight months for "subversion" under a Beijing-introduced national security law.

But Hong Kong authorities used the same law to charge Wong again in June last year, accusing him of collusion with other countries and entities to impose sanctions against the finance hub and China.

Pro-democracy tycoon Jimmy Lai, founder of the now-defunct Apple Daily newspaper, was given 20 years in February under the same legislation.

The court on Thursday described Wong and fellow student leader Nathan Law, who is in self-imposed exile and wanted by authorities, as "two ring-leaders or masterminds" of a conspiracy to endanger national security.

Wong was potentially facing a life term as his offence was deemed to be "of a grave nature", judge William Tam said.

But he decided an "appropriate starting point" for Wong's offence was 13 years, reduced to 10 because of his guilty plea.

With a heavy police presence outside, Wong entered the dock looking calm in a blue shirt and black glasses, nodding to supporters.

Wong said in his mitigation letter to court, seen by the AFP news agency, that he had hoped to leave prison in 2027, but the new charge made him felt he was back at the beginning.

He added that his future would not be defined by things that happened to him before 2020, and that the years he has spent behind bars have been the "most profound lessons" of his life.

After the sentencing, Wong thanked and waved to his supporters as he left. Some wept and were heard saying: "We'll be waiting for your release."

Wong's fate shows that Beijing "is keeping a tight rein over Hong Kong and there's no scope for turning back to the more relaxed days before 2019", Steve Tsang, director of the China Institute at SOAS University of London, told AFP before the sentencing.

Human Rights Watch said his sentence "cruel and unjust", and urged foreign governments to "impose targeted sanctions on officials responsible for Hong Kong's growing repression".

Steve Li, chief superintendent of the police's National Security Department, welcomed the sentencing, which he said "evidently proved" Wong's efforts to rouse foreign sanctions were "futile".

And China issued a warning on Thursday against "irresponsible" remarks on Hong Kong's judicial affairs, shortly after the United States condemned Wong's sentencing.

"Relevant parties should earnestly respect China's sovereignty, respect Hong Kong's legal system, stop making irresponsible remarks regarding the trial of HKSAR judicial cases," foreign ministry spokesman Guo Jiakun told a news briefing, using the city's official acronym.

Wong rose to prominence during Hong Kong's 2014 protests, known as the Umbrella Movement, and has been in and out of jail since 2017.

Authorities said Wong started international lobbying as early as 2016, when he promoted a bill -- the Hong Kong Human Rights and Democracy Act -- that would allow the US president to impose sanctions in response to rights violations in the city.

During Hong Kong's massive pro-democracy protests in 2019, Wong was part of a delegation to the United States to lobby for the bill, which eventually passed.

The following year, Beijing imposed the sweeping national security law on Hong Kong that effectively quashed dissent, prompting opposition groups to disband and activists to flee the city.

He was among dozens of pro-democracy figures jailed for "subversion" in 2024, in what was Hong Kong's largest national security trial. Some of them have since been released.

At its AGM held this week in Doha, the Arab Air Carriers’ Organization raised concerns over EU carbon regulations, as airlines look to boost European connectivity amid ongoing regional uncertainty.
Following a turbulent year, Arab airlines are projected to suffer a multi-billion dollar loss, according to a report issued by the Arab Air Carriers' Organization (AACO).

Meeting this week in Doha for its annual general meeting (AGM), AACO is an association of 39 Arab airlines based in the Middle East and North Africa, including carriers such as Qatar Airways, Emirates, Etihad Airways and Gulf Air.

It provides a joint framework for cooperation among member airlines in areas like aeropolitical affairs, digital transformation and sustainable aviation fuel.

In its 2026 annual report, AACO highlighted that operational pressures related to “geopolitical uncertainties” and “fluctuating financial conditions” have hit the balance sheets of regional airlines.

According to the findings, it’s forecast that AACO members will record a net loss of $4.3 billion (€3.82 billion) in 2026 and a 5.2% decline in air cargo traffic. Globally, the industry’s net profit is projected to fall to around $23 billion (€20.5 billion), its “lowest level in 12 years excluding the COVID-19 period and its aftermath”.

The report added that performance among members is “likely to vary considerably depending on hub locations and exposure to conflict-affected markets”.

Despite the pressures, AACO said that the airline industry is “expected to remain profitable" in 2026, "albeit at a slower pace as higher energy costs and ongoing operational disruptions are placing additional pressure on margins”.

Fuel is expected to take 31.4% of industry operating costs, and specifically for AACO members this comes to 37.3% – “up from 25.4% and 27.9% recorded respectively in 2025".

In Doha, concerns were raised over the EU's proposed Emissions Trading System (ETS), warning it could infringe on regional airspace and create unfair additional costs to Arab airlines.

Abdul Wahab Teffaha, AACO's Secretary General, told Euronews Travel that ETS raised questions of “sovereignty”.

“The EU has decided that 'we want to have targets which are beyond the targets which were agreed upon by the ICAO (International Civil Aviation Organization)’ – and we have no problem with that, because they are exercising their sovereign rights," he said.

In 2005, the bloc introduced ETS proposals, with the goal of requiring polluters to pay for their greenhouse gas emissions. The aviation sector was brought into the EU ETS on 1 January 2012, however, the application for flights to and from non-European countries was suspended later that same year.

During the AGM, the Resolution on Aviation and the Environment was adopted, opposing the extension of the ETS to flights departing from the European Economic Area (EEA) and landing in third countries within a 5,000-kilometre radius of Frankfurt Airport by 2029.

"This is extra-territoriality, trespassing on the sovereignty of others,” said Teffaha, who added that the move would undermine the Chicago Convention. “And that applies for all the countries within the 5,000 kilometres, which are basically, we start from Turkey, through Iraq, down till Dubai, up till Sudan, Egypt, up to Morocco."

Otherwise known as the Convention on International Civil Aviation, Article 1 of the Chicago Convention says that all co-signing states recognise that each country has "complete and exclusive sovereignty over the airspace above its territory".

Beyond sovereignty, Teffaha also told Euronews that if the ETS were to become law, Arab carriers could unfairly face double costs for their emissions reductions, given that the existing Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation (CORSIA) framework already requires airlines to offset a proportion of their international flight emissions.

As such, Arab carriers may end up paying double if the two frameworks do not recognise each other’s charges.

The three-day event was hosted by Qatar Airways with Hamad Al-Khater, Qatar Airways Group Chief CEO, as the session's chairman. It brought together more than 200 executives and stakeholders within the industry, including AACO member airlines, partner carriers as well as representatives from Amadeus and SITA.

"This AACO gathering brings together the airlines of the Arab world, which collectively play a vital role in connecting people, businesses and economies across six continents,” Al-Khater said.

“As our industry faces a very difficult period, with geopolitical developments continuing to disrupt global connectivity, regional cooperation has become our greatest strength."

The Qatar Airways chief added that the sector supports as many as four million jobs across the Middle East and injects $290 billion (€258 billion) into the regional economy. This figure is set to rise to $730 billion (€649.9 billion) by 2043.

During the event, AACO and SITA inked a memorandum of understanding, making the latter's WorldTracer Bag Mate, SITA's scheme for mishandled bags, available to AACO member airlines.
