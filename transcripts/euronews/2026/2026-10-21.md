Macron calls for extreme caution in Gulf, secures critical gas deal in Qatar state visit
French President Emmanuel Macron during his two-day state visit to Doha, 20 October 2026

French president concludes his state visit by reinforcing the strategic partnership with Qatar. He declares his Gulf tour objectives achieved after securing a crucial gas deal with Qatar as he seeks to lower tensions over living costs in France.
French President Emmanuel Macron issued a stern warning on Wednesday in Doha about the Gulf's fragile security situation, urging “extreme caution” to avoid escalating tensions that could reignite conflict, after talks with Qatar’s Emir Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani.

At the end of his two-day state visit to Qatar, Macron told the media that “no one can ignore that we are in a period that despite everything can lead to tensions: elections in Israel, the midterm elections in the United States, and so during this time efforts and initiatives must be doubled to avoid that these tensions don’t result in a resumption of conflict.”

The French president said he discussed at length Qatar’s mediation efforts with Sheikh Tamim and that “in the next hours or days we will have Iran’s answer,” on the ongoing negotiations with the United States at a time when “there is a lot of uncertainty and measures taken by one side and the other that I would not comment on. “

Hailing France’s relationship with Qatar as “partners in trust who helped the security and stability” of the region, Macron said he was “very cautious because no one can tell how the situation will evolve in the next days and weeks.”

Macron said that the two leaders also discussed ways to secure the transit of ships through the blocked Strait of Hormuz, with the “biggest difficulty now being the cost of transport multiplied by 10 because of too few ships come for transit as they are not insured.”

France and the Gulf countries are working on a mechanism to address this reality, on “diversification in infrastructure” and ultimately on reducing the dependence on Hormuz, the French president said.

Macron announced that he had secured a gas agreement with Qatar which would cover France’s needs for the winter period and which will contribute to lowering fuel and transport costs, “reducing pressure on prices from November” and “avoiding difficulties.”

"We secured the equivalent of two terawatt-hours per month of LNG for the winter period, which was important for us and which will allow us to move forward," the French president told the media.

“Our objectives are achieved because we received guarantees on volumes where we had pre-existing uncertainties and all these agreements reassure us for the months to come, to have better conditions for our citizens and to avoid the shortfalls which were predicted by certain (energy) operators,” he added as he sought to lower prices of energy following violent protests over the rising living costs in France.

He explained that France’s energy priority needs were diesel, which he secured in the United Arab Emirates and Bahrain, and then gas, which he obtained in Qatar, as “guaranteed volumes on which we depend, which posed a risk for the next months.”

Macron was accompanied on his Gulf tour by French business leaders including the chief executives of France's largest energy companies, TotalEnergies and Engie. TotalEnergies already has substantial interests in Qatar, including a partnership with QatarEnergy in the North Field East LNG expansion project, one of the world's largest.

France secured commitments for an additional 200,000 tonnes of diesel per month from the UAE and Bahrain amid soaring fuel prices.

France and Qatar hailed their defence partnership during Macron’s state visit, as France was one of Qatar's main defenders during the Iran war.

Emphasising how “the partnership showed its strength” during the war, Macron announced new agreements to continue supplying Qatar with the new 5th-generation Rafale fighter jets, along with radars, air-defence systems, and armoured vehicles.

Macron said that “all the countries in the region are looking for more autonomy and to develop their own capacity for defence.”

Qatar’s Rafale fighter jets escorted Macron’s plane on his arrival in Doha on Tuesday, both as part of the state visit honours, but also to showcase the defence cooperation between the two countries.

After departing from Qatar, which the Elysee announced as the last country to visit during his Gulf tour, Macron made a surprise stop in Saudi Arabia in a show of support for Riyadh’s efforts to combat the Houthis in Yemen.

During his visit to Doha, he said that France has been working with Saudi Arabia in a “defensive capacity” to secure global energy supplies disrupted by the conflict with the Houthis.

Qatar’s emir Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al-Thani praised the “fruitful” talks with Macron during the first state visit by a French president to Doha.

In a post on X, Sheikh Tamim said that the two leaders “explored ways to strengthen our strategic partnership and expand areas of cooperation between our two countries, alongside exchanging views on the most prominent regional and international issues”.

“We take pride in the progress our relations have achieved and in our shared commitment to entrenching security and stability while serving the mutual interests of our two peoples,” he added.

Around two dozen Americans are still being held in Russian prisons for various reasons, according to the Foley Foundation, a hostage advocacy group.
Russia released retired American teacher Stephen Hubbard on Wednesday after he was jailed for allegedly fighting as a mercenary in Ukraine, US envoy Steve Witkoff said during a meeting with President Vladimir Putin in St. Petersburg.

The release came a day after US President Donald Trump said he had spoken at length with Putin and urged him to free Hubbard. The White House said that, unlike in previous releases, the US was not required to give up anyone in return.

Witkoff, who has made multiple unsuccessful visits to Russia in order to reach a settlement in its war on Ukraine, thanked Putin, telling him, “Because of you, he's going home to his family.”

Hubbard was first imprisoned by Russia in 2022 on charges that Hostage Aid Worldwide, a hostage advocacy group, has called bogus. He was sentenced to nearly seven years in prison following a closed-door trial in 2024.

Russian prosecutors accused him of joining the Ukrainian military after Moscow launched its full-scale invasion in February 2022 and fighting alongside Ukrainian forces until his capture two months later.

He is the second American released by Russia in roughly two months, following the release in August of former US Marine Robert Gilman, who had been seriously ill and was jailed on contested charges of assaulting a police officer.

His release coincided with Witkoff's visit and came as Trump has taken several steps favourable to Moscow, amid growing tensions between Washington and Kyiv.

In an open letter to Trump published by Hostage Aid Worldwide in August, Hubbard's sister, Patricia Hubbard Fox, praised the president but also claimed her brother had endured "constant beatings, torture and starvation" while in detention.

Officials in Busan made a third attempt to guide a trapped shark out of a canal and back to sea. The animal has drawn over a million visitors.
… More
Authorities in the south-eastern South Korean city of Busan made a third attempt on Wednesday to send a trapped shark back to the open sea.

City and Coast Guard officials scanned the water from the shore and from a makeshift bridge, while divers went in.

The team used a net secured to a pontoon to guide the animal towards open water. At several points, it was seen swimming close to the shore.

Onlookers gathered along the banks to watch. The shark first appeared in the waterway on 18 September, and more than a million visitors have come to see it since.

Eurostat data show 876,600 Russian nationals held valid EU residence permits at the end of 2025, with Germany, Spain and Estonia hosting the largest communities.
The number of Russian citizens legally residing in the EU has grown by 105,000 since Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in early 2022, reaching 876,600 by the end of 2025, an analysis of Eurostat data shows.

The figure represents a 13.6% rise from the 771,800 Russians holding valid temporary or permanent residence permits across EU member states in 2021. Germany hosts the largest community, with 218,000 residents, followed by Spain (116,800) and Estonia (78,400).

Southern Europe has seen the sharpest growth: Spain issued residence permits to 44,400 Russian nationals over the period, France 13,700, while Cyprus and Portugal saw their Russian-born populations increase 2.5 and 3.5 times, respectively.

Several EU states have moved in the opposite direction. Latvia, Estonia and the Czech Republic have recorded the largest drops in Russian residents, driven by tough migration policies and, in the Baltic states, security concerns over large Russian-speaking communities.

Significant communities have also formed outside the EU. In Serbia the Russian population rose from roughly 5,000 before the invasion to 61,900 by the end of 2025. By spring of this year, Kazakhstan had absorbed more than 107,000 Russian migrants, while Armenia and Georgia hosted 43,500 and 70,000, respectively.

Turkey drew large numbers in the immediate post-invasion period but later tightened residency rules. By June, valid Russian permits there had fallen to 75,900, half the 2023 peak.

Researchers tracking emigration patterns have identified at least four distinct waves since February 2022. The most recent, running from this spring, has been sparked by renewed claims of impending mobilisation for Moscow's all-out war in Ukraine.

"Everyone now understands that they have no future in Russia. There is no image of the future, no understanding of what will happen in six months or a year," said Anastasia Burakova, a lawyer and founder of Kovcheg, a legal assistance project for Russians abroad. "What is more, there are risks linked to mobilisation and conscription, which people feel very personally."

Ivan, 19, left Russia this spring to avoid conscription and now works in construction in Yerevan, posting videos on YouTube. A year earlier he had been a mobile phone store manager.

"I thought: this is it, this is life, everything is going the way it should," he said in a YouTube video. "Now I'm sitting on a bench in Yerevan, with no title and no steady job. My wife is next to me. Back then I was a manager and felt trapped. Now I have nothing, and I feel free."

Surveys by independent researchers show the Russian emigrees include predominantly young couples, often without children, with both partners under 36.

They tend to have high levels of education and are concentrated in professions either vulnerable to political repression — journalists, human rights workers, political analysts — or in high international demand, particularly IT, engineering and academia. Around 20% of those who left do not hold university degrees. Some 18% are studying or plan to study abroad.

They fall into three broad groups: those who have settled and expect to remain abroad long-term, those who have already moved once and are preparing to relocate again, and those still in Russia who intend to leave within six months.

The economic impact on host countries has been visible yet uneven. In Armenia and Georgia, the sudden influx of people and capital contributed to GDP growth of between 9% and 9.4% at its peak between 2022 and 2024, according to analysis published by the CASE Center research institute.

Average Russian household incomes in Serbia ran to around €3,500 per month against a local average of €900. In Georgia the figures were €3,300 against €650. The effect has since moderated as host economies adapted, with growth settling back to 5–7% annually.

Rather than competing for local jobs, most Russian migrants have worked remotely or set up their own businesses.

In IT, up to 41% have maintained income from foreign clients, while in the media sector around 21% have done so, channelling money into host economies through taxes and everyday spending.

Meanwhile, Russian authorities and independent researchers have documented some returns, but the scale remains disputed.

The independent OutRush sociological project estimated that no more than 8% of emigrants had returned to Russia by summer 2024, with around 5% more intending to do so.

The Institute of Applied Economic Research at RANEPA, a state-linked academic institution, put the overall return rate from the 2022–2023 wave at roughly 10%.

Estimates from consulting firms and recruitment agencies are considerably higher, ranging from 16% among IT workers to 40–45% of those who left at the peak.

Thousands protested in Lisbon’s Rossio square over housing costs after police removed a camp, and voted to return the next day to camp out again.
… More
Thousands of people gathered in Rossio square in Lisbon on Tuesday to demand affordable housing.

They chanted, lit flares and held up keys and signs, one of which read ‘a house cannot be a privilege’. Similar demonstrations took place in other Portuguese cities.

Earlier in the day, police forcibly cleared the camp that demonstrators had set up in the square. The crowd called a new gathering, and many came back that evening.

They are also against recent government plans to ease the cap on yearly rent rises and to make evictions easier for tenants who fail to pay.

The prime minister says the changes will build trust between landlords and renters. Critics say they leave tenants more exposed.

Torrential rain flooded eastern Spain, leaving at least four dead. Crews carried out more than 400 rescues in the Valencia region, officials said Monday.
… More
Torrential rain hit eastern Spain and left at least four people dead, authorities said on Monday. Streets filled with water, and many people had to stay indoors. In parts of the Valencia region, up to 700 litres of rain per square metre fell in 24 hours.

The water swept away parked cars, covered roads and left some residents cut off. Emergency crews carried out more than 400 rescues by land and air, and some people were airlifted out.

Two of the deaths occurred in the resort of Oliva, and a third in the same region. A fourth person died in Catalonia after falling into a swollen stream.

Scientists say climate change is making the autumn storms that hit the Mediterranean coast more intense.

Moscow says it is prepared to consider economic proposals from Washington but will not make political concessions, while the Kremlin insists that a lasting settlement on Russia's terms, rather than a temporary energy ceasefire, remains its objective.
Russia has ruled out making political concessions to Washington ahead of talks between President Vladimir Putin and US envoy Steve Witkoff, signalling that Moscow remains unwilling to soften its maximalist negotiating position despite renewed American efforts to end the war in Ukraine.

Witkoff arrived in Saint Petersburg to meet Vladimir Putin, following what US Secretary of State Marco Rubio described as "positive" talks with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy in Oslo on Tuesday.

Russian Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov said Moscow would examine proposals brought by Witkoff and Donald Trump's son-in-law Jared Kushner, but made clear that any economic or financial benefits would not be accompanied by political concessions.

"We'll see what they propose. If they offer something that benefits us economically and financially, then why not?" Lavrov said, according to remarks reported by Russian media.

"But this must be done without any political concessions on our part," he added, stressing that Russia should rely on itself rather than expect favourable treatment from Washington.

His comments underscore the difficulties facing the latest diplomatic initiative, which comes after Trump reached a diesel agreement with Putin and criticised the impact of Ukrainian strikes on Russia's energy sector on global fuel prices.

Lavrov also cautioned against expecting a fundamental change in US-Russian relations under Trump, describing the United States as "at best a competitor" and warning that future American administrations could reverse any economic concessions.

The Kremlin, meanwhile, reiterated its opposition to any temporary halt in attacks on energy infrastructure, despite Trump's earlier announcement of an energy ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine.

"Nothing has changed. We need peace, not a ceasefire," Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov told reporters on Wednesday.

The statement comes as Russia continues its attacks on Ukraine's energy infrastructure ahead of winter, raising further questions about the prospects for a limited agreement to protect power facilities.

Peskov also acknowledged that a comprehensive settlement could not be reached without Ukraine's participation, even as Moscow prepares to discuss the latest proposals directly with Washington.

"Of course, it is impossible to fully resolve the Ukrainian issue without someone from Kyiv being present," he said, adding that someone in the Ukrainian leadership would have to take what he described as a responsible decision.

The Kremlin spokesman also cast doubt on the contribution Finnish President Alexander Stubb could make to the diplomatic process, suggesting that merely repeating the broader European position on Ukraine would do little to advance negotiations.

Stubb took part in Tuesday's talks in Oslo alongside Zelenskyy, Witkoff and Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre, with discussions focusing on proposals for ending the war and possible security guarantees for Ukraine.

The latest diplomatic push comes amid deepening tensions between Kyiv and Washington, following Trump's criticism of Ukrainian strikes on Russian energy infrastructure and reports of delays in US-made Patriot interceptor missile deliveries already paid for by European countries.

Following his meeting with Witkoff, Zelenskyy thanked Washington for resuming intelligence-sharing on incoming Russian strikes, describing early warnings of ballistic missile attacks as essential to protecting civilians.

"We are thankful that intelligence works," Zelenskyy told reporters in Oslo, calling the warnings "very, very important for our people, for children, adults, civilians".

Trump, meanwhile, renewed his calls for Ukraine to reach an agreement with Russia, telling reporters at the White House on Tuesday that Kyiv needed to "make a deal".

Zelenskyy has insisted that Ukraine must be directly involved in any negotiations determining its future.

"If there are issues about Ukraine, our future, it can be acceptable only when we are at the table of negotiations," he said on Tuesday.

Roughly three in four voters rejected starting a path toward separation, as Trump’s trade war and annexation threats push Canadians closer together.
Voters in Alberta, Canada’s oil-rich province, on Tuesday overwhelmingly chose to remain in the country, giving Prime Minister Mark Carney a boost in his push for national unity as US President Donald Trump’s trade war and annexation threats rally Canadians around their flag.

With about 90% of voting areas counted, 75% backed staying in Canada, according to unofficial results from Elections Alberta.

“Don’t mess with Canada. President Trump is watching this,” said Thomas Lukaszuk, a former Alberta deputy premier who led the Forever Canadian campaign against separation.

The referendum was not a direct vote on independence. A “yes” result would have set Alberta on a path toward a later vote on leaving Canada.

“It’s a great result for Canada that Albertans have chosen unity,” Carney told reporters in Ottawa before a Cabinet meeting on Tuesday.

He argued that the quarter of voters who supported starting the separation process did not all necessarily want independence. In his view, only a smaller “hard core” favours leaving. Some voters have said they backed the question to send Ottawa a message rather than to break away.

Carney said he spoke with Alberta Premier Danielle Smith on Tuesday morning about the results and about closer federal-provincial cooperation, including on immigration.

Some voters said they backed the separation question without wanting Alberta to leave. Edmonton voter Shawn Campbell said he voted to begin the process to send a message: “It sends a message to Ottawa that Albertans are not happy.”

Others criticised the vote itself. Dave Malka, 59, of Edmonton, objected to spending money on a “referendum on a referendum” rather than on healthcare. With Trump threatening Canada, he said, “It seems to be happening at the absolute worst time when we should be united. Instead, we’re fighting among ourselves.”

In a televised address Tuesday night, Smith welcomed the outcome and promised not to call another separation referendum while she is premier.

“Yesterday, Albertans voted overwhelmingly in favour of remaining a province of Canada,” Smith said. “The voter turnout was historic and a powerful testimony of the passion Albertans feel for our country and our province.”

She said Alberta would keep pushing Ottawa for more provincial autonomy, but that disputes would be resolved through Canada’s political and legal institutions, not through separation.

Thomas Lukaszuk, a former Alberta deputy premier who led the Forever Canadian campaign against separation, told supporters: “Don’t mess with Canada. President Trump is watching this.”

“Independence feels far away, and we should not pretend this was not a defeat,” he wrote on social media.

Still, separatist leaders insisted they would carry on. At a referendum-night gathering at an Edmonton restaurant, the crowd fell silent as the results came in.

“We’re not going anywhere,” said Jeff Rath of the Alberta Prosperity Project. “We’ve dominated the conversation in the last year. We’ve been educating Albertans on the benefits of independence, and we’re going to continue moving forward.”

Rath had said before the vote that he would be thrilled with 30% support. He also said he wanted Smith removed as leader of the United Conservative Party (UCP) and believed the party could splinter, possibly producing a new pro-independence party.

Alberta is home to the world’s fourth-largest proven oil reserves. It is cowboy country and a conservative stronghold, with a political culture that has long had more in common with the American West than with much of Canada. Resentment toward the federal government in Ottawa runs deep, but it has not traditionally translated into broad support for independence.

Former Premier Jason Kenney noted that separatist parties historically drew only 1% or 2% of the vote. He said anger over COVID-19 restrictions helped mobilise activists who later embraced the separatist cause.

Trump’s trade war and his threats to make Canada the 51st U.S. state have helped Carney build support in Alberta, a province where Liberal prime ministers have historically been unpopular.

Independent diplomat Luca Niculescu wins parliamentary backing for a minority government, but analysts warn the calm may not last.
Romania on Tuesday swore in a new cabinet following a vote that ended a months-long political impasse.

Prime Minister Luca Niculescu, an independent diplomat, won 280 votes in favour of his proposed cabinet, with 58 against. He plans to start work immediately, with a large budget deficit at the top of his agenda.

President Nicușor Dan nominated Niculescu, 55, a diplomat and former journalist, on 5 October, the president’s fourth pick for the job, after three earlier nominees failed to form a government.

Niculescu was widely expected to succeed after securing pledges of support for a minority government from the Social Democrats and other parties. He has reportedly reached a deal with several parties for six months of backing while a longer-term agreement is negotiated.

“I present a governing programme, a team, and a commitment to work toward several clear objectives,” he said. “And I want to tell the Romanian people, from the very start, what kind of government this will be. A government that brings funds and investments into the country.”

The political crisis has gripped Romania since May, when then-Prime Minister Ilie Bolojan was ousted in a no-confidence motion. The motion was filed jointly by the Social Democratic Party, which had been part of his governing coalition, and the Alliance for the Unity of Romanians party. Bolojan’s government had pursued strict austerity measures.

Bolojan took office in 2025 with the aim of ending one of Romania’s worst political crises since the end of communism and of reducing a massive budget deficit.

Romania has faced a long stretch of instability since the annulment of a presidential election in December 2024. The country has also struggled with rampant inflation and a technical recession. Last year, it recorded one of the highest budget deficits in the European Union.

Tuesday’s vote came weeks after lawmakers rejected a cabinet proposed by Dan’s third pick, Siegfried Muresan. That failure prolonged the crisis and raised the prospect of early elections, at a time of regional security concerns linked to Russia’s war in neighbouring Ukraine. Romania is a member of both NATO and the EU.

Experts warn that tensions could flare again soon. Niculescu’s support rests on a six-month arrangement with several parties, and a longer-term deal has yet to be agreed.
