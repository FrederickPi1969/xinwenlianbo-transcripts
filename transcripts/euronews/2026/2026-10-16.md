Updated: 16/10/2026 - 16:16 GMT+2
Foxes, owls and deer made from pumpkins have taken over Groot-Bijgaarden Castle near Brussels. The Pumpkimania festival features a 772kg giant, pumpkin boat rides and Halloween activities until 1 November.
… More
Thousands of pumpkins have transformed the grounds of Groot-Bijgaarden Castle near Brussels into a colourful autumn wonderland for the annual Pumpkimania festival. This year's theme, “The Magic of the Forest”, features towering sculptures of foxes, owls and deer covered in pumpkins of every shape and size, sourced from Belgium, Germany and Portugal.

The star attraction is a giant pumpkin weighing 772 kilograms, grown for the castle's traditional weigh-off on 26 September. Visitors can also watch giant pumpkins being turned into makeshift kayaks for rides across the castle lake.

Organisers expect around 50,000 visitors before the festival closes on 1 November. Families can take part in pumpkin-carving workshops at weekends, while the Halloween programme will see the castle open daily until 7pm from 24 October. The festivities will end with a giant pumpkin-smashing event.

Despite a difficult growing season following a hot summer, the displays have filled the estate with thousands of pumpkins. Located in Dilbeek, about 10 kilometres from central Brussels, the festival costs €16 for adults and €9 for children aged 5 to 14, with free entry for younger children.

Russian strikes ignited massive fires at businesses in Odesa and Zaporizhzhia as President Zelensky met European leaders for talks.
… More
Russian strikes sparked major fires in Ukraine's Odesa and Zaporizhzhia regions on Friday 16 October, leaving industrial premises engulfed in flames.

In Odesa, firefighters battled a blaze at a factory producing household goods, dragging hoses towards the burning building as products lay scattered across the ground.

Further east, in Zaporizhzhia, emergency crews used an elevated platform to tackle a fire covering 500 square metres at a private business. Footage released by Ukraine's emergency services shows firefighters directing jets of water into the flames at both sites.

The attacks came as Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky joined European leaders for talks. Separate footage shows him arriving alongside European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and European Council President António Costa. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni and Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk were also among the leaders filmed at the gathering. The scenes underline the continuing impact of Russian strikes on Ukrainian businesses as Kyiv pursues diplomatic discussions with its European partners.

The decree covers a flooring manufacturer, a paper packaging firm, a hygiene products maker, and two metal- and plastic-packaging companies, all of which continued operating in Russia after the all-out invasion of Ukraine in early 2022.
Russian President Vladimir Putin has signed a decree ordering the compulsory seizure of Russian assets belonging to French flooring manufacturer Tarkett and four other European companies, whose combined annual revenue exceeds 50 billion roubles (€532 million).

The assets have been placed under the interim control of a management company called Centre Capital, registered in February 2026.

Its chief executive is listed as Marat Mirkhaydarov, former head of the administration of the regional governor of Zabaykalsky, who stood down in 2025 following an injury sustained in Russia's ongoing war in Ukraine.

The decree also covers paper packaging producer Kappa Rus, formerly part of Ireland's Smurfit Kappa, hygiene product maker Ontex RU, part of the Belgian Ontex group, metal packaging manufacturer Eviosys Packaging Kuban, part of US-based Sonoco Products Company, and Menshen Packaging CIS, owned by German plastics producer Menshen.

All five companies continued operating in Russia after the country's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022.

Tarkett, which ranks third globally by floor covering sales and leads the market in vinyl flooring according to its 2024 annual report, kept two plants in Russia out of its 34 production sites worldwide and continued paying taxes to the Russian budget.

The latest seizures follow a series of similar moves in September, when Putin placed the Russian assets of Swiss food giant Nestlé, German hypermarket chain Metro, French retailer Auchan and home improvement chain Leroy Merlin under temporary administration.

Those assets were transferred to a company called LEV Management, established shortly before the decision and reported by Russian media to have links to the country's security services. A manufacturer of plastic dispensing systems owned by French holding Aptar Europe Holding was also placed under temporary administration shortly afterwards.

A presidential decree signed in April 2023 established the mechanism for such seizures, authorising the imposition of temporary administration over certain Russian assets of foreign companies.

Western governments and international legal experts have described the move as unlawful expropriation. Companies whose assets are seized can, in principle, pursue compensation through international arbitration under bilateral investment treaties Russia has signed with EU member states, although enforcing judgments against the Kremlin and its entities is nearly impossible.

The first targets under that decree were Danish brewer Carlsberg and French dairy group Danone.

Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov directly linked the transfer of Nestlé's and Auchan's assets to the fact that they are companies from what Moscow terms "unfriendly countries".

“Unfriendly countries” is an official Russian government designation applied to all EU member states, the US, the UK and other states that imposed sanctions following the 2022 full-scale invasion, in an attempt to dissuade them from aiding Kyiv.

Russia has framed the asset seizures in part as a response to the EU’s decision to freeze approximately €300 billion in Russian sovereign assets following the all-out invasion.

At least 12 people died after a boat carrying migrants sank off the coast of Gambia, the Red Cross said.

The incident occurred off of Jinack Kajata in the country's North Bank region, the Gambia Red Cross Society (GRCS) said on Thursday. The migrants were reportedly heading towards Europe via the West African Atlantic Route.

"The number of people missing remains uncertain, and further updates are expected as response efforts continue," the GRCS said in a statement, adding that it was "working closely with relevant authorities and response partners as the situation unfolds."

According to Gambian media, the village of Jinack Kajata on Jinack Island has become an increasingly popular launch site for migrants looking to make the journey to Spain's Canary Islands in the Atlantic Ocean.

The United Nations' International Organization for Migration (IOM) says the West African Atlantic Route is a key pathway for West African migrants travelling to Europe, despite it becoming increasingly dangerous.

"Data from the West African Atlantic Route show that while arrivals to the Canary Islands fell considerably since the 2024 peak (62 per cent decrease from 2024 to 2025), recorded deaths and disappearances remained broadly unchanged, falling 4 per cent from 2024 to 2025 (1,215 to 1,172), highlighting how the route has become much more dangerous for migrants," the IOM said earlier this week.

It added that increased controls in traditional departure spots in Morocco, Mauritania, and Senegal have pushed some migrants seeking to reach Europe further south, towards countries such as The Gambia.

"These alternative departure points can add up to 750 kilometres to the crossing, extending some journeys to approximately 2,200 kilometres and exposing people to longer periods at sea, more remote and less monitored coastlines, and greater risks of shipwreck, dehydration and disappearance," the IOM said.

In July, dozens of migrants were reportedly dead or missing after a vessel travelling from The Gambia was shipwrecked off the coast of Mauritania.

A Ukrainian company has demonstrated an unusual new battlefield rescue system: a drone capable of carrying a wounded soldier inside an enclosed cabin. Developed by SkyFall, the aircraft, named StarGate, was filmed taking off, flying and landing with a person on board.
… More
Built to reach casualties in areas inaccessible to ground vehicles, the remotely operated machine can reportedly lift 150 kg and travel at speeds of up to 105 km/h. Its ability to fly over minefields, rivers and damaged roads could help shorten dangerous journeys from the front line.

In the footage, a SkyFall representative steps out of the cabin after landing, while an operator remains at a distance. The manufacturer says StarGate could cut evacuation times from hours or days to just minutes, potentially allowing wounded troops to receive medical attention much sooner.

The aircraft uses Starlink and SkyFall's L-Link system, together with AI-assisted technology designed to operate despite satellite navigation interference. It was also shown deploying a net to intercept a hostile drone.

A Ukrainian combat brigade has now received the prototype for testing under frontline conditions. SkyFall has yet to publish the findings, and the drone's performance in real combat evacuations remains to be independently confirmed.

A senior official in Peshawar told the AFP news agency that initial reports suggested the roof collapse was due to "weak infrastructure".
The families of children killed in a roof collapse at a school in northwest Pakistan called for "justice" on Friday, a day after more than 30 pupils and a teacher were crushed to death.

A woman teacher and 34 students, mostly younger than 14, died in the building collapse in the city of Peshawar on Thursday morning.

"The parents of all these children must receive justice," tailor Muhammad Wahab, who lost his 11-year-old nephew Hammad in the accident, told the AFP news agency on Friday.

"I appeal to our rulers," he said, adding that Hammad had been "doing well in school" before the tragedy.

Dozens of people gathered outside the school on Friday to mourn the children who were killed in the collapse, which officials blamed on dilapidated infrastructure.

One grief-stricken father showed AFP a picture on his mobile phone of his young daughter, dressed in red and gold, who lost her life.

Another parent, Asadullah, said he lost a son, nephew and three other close relatives in the tragedy.

"The children were good students and often achieved top positions in their classes," he told AFP. "They happily went to school every day."

Residents and rescuers described chaotic scenes in the aftermath of Thursday's collapse, as people scrambled to pull children out from under the rubble.

A man named Sohail told AFP that "everyone began screaming in panic" when his niece, who had escaped the building, arrived home to tell them it had collapsed.

"When I arrived at the scene, I saw around 10 to 15 children trapped beneath the rubble, with debris piled up to their necks. We quickly pulled them out," he said.

Images released by emergency services showed rescue workers in high-visibility vests scrambling to move debris, rubble and dirt.

A senior official in Peshawar told AFP that initial reports suggested the roof collapse was due to "weak infrastructure".

"The school owner has been detained," the official said on condition of anonymity because he was not authorised to talk to media.

Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif expressed "deep sorrow and grief over the loss of precious lives" in an X post.

Building collapses are common in Pakistan, largely due to poor safety standards and substandard construction materials in the South Asian nation of more than 240 million people.

In June, more than a dozen children were killed when a roof collapsed at a tutoring centre in the eastern city of Lahore.

In 2025, 27 people were killed and 10 injured when a five-storey building collapsed in the impoverished Lyari area of the southern city of Karachi.

Based on the calculations behind the proposed legislation, prices for a standard pack of cigarettes are expected to rise to €8.77 from 2027, €9.56 from 2028, €10.42 from 2029, and €11.36 from 2030.

Some premium brand cigarettes could cost as much as €14, whilst hand-rolling tobacco is also set to become significantly more expensive.

Germany's cigarette prices are therefore gradually catching up with those in other European countries, but they are still expected to remain below prices in some of the continent's most expensive markets, including Ireland, France, and the Netherlands.

With the planned increase, the German government aims to raise an additional €12 billion over four years.

Additional revenue of around €1.5 billion is expected for 2027, rising to €4.5 billion by 2030. Last year, the government generated €17.6 billion in revenue from tobacco duty.

The tobacco industry, however, doubts that the government will actually raise as much revenue as it hopes, with industry representatives warning that higher taxes could also drive smokers increasingly towards the black market or towards cheaper cigarettes from other EU countries. Legal tobacco retailers could be among those hit hardest.

CDU MP Carsten Brodesser has called for more moderate tax increases, arguing that steeper hikes could risk both lower tax revenues and adverse effects on the legal tobacco trade. However, he was unable to get his proposal adopted.

During a debate in the Bundestag, SPD MP Ingo Vogel acknowledged that reform was partly intended to help balance the budget but that it also had a public health objective - making nicotine less attractive.

Vogel welcomed news that taxes on cigars and cigarillos would not rise as sharply as had previously been planned. Taxes on e-cigarette liquids, however, were increased slightly more than had been expected.

"Inexpensive products, in particular, should not become an easy gateway to nicotine consumption, which is why we are raising the price threshold in this area," Vogel said, adding that concerns raised by customs officials and their union about a possible rise in the black market were being taken "very, very seriously."

Should demand shift to illegal channels, then "we will not only lose out financially, but we will also have to deal more with criminal networks," he said.

The far-right AfD party has criticised the increase, with MP Christian Douglas pointing to what he sees as a contradiction in the government's approach: it justifies higher tobacco taxes on public health grounds whilst also expecting tax revenues to increase, suggesting the coalition does not actually expect tobacco consumption to fall.

The Greens, on the other hand, support a higher tobacco tax in principle but consider the planned increases to be too small.

According to the 2025 microcensus, around 19% of people aged 15 and over in Germany smoke. Some 14% regularly and 5% occasionally.

The German Cancer Research Centre said around 131,000 people died from tobacco-related illnesses in 2023. Health experts regard higher prices as an effective means of encouraging people to give up smoking.

On Friday, the Bundesrat will vote on the amendment to the tobacco tax law. With no objections currently anticipated, the tax increase is expected to be approved.

Ali Kayali, 70, known as Mihrac Ural, commanded al-Assad-affiliated militia forces that carried out summary executions of more than 450 civilians in Bayda and Baniyas in May 2013.
Syria's General Intelligence Directorate has arrested Ali Kayali, the al-Assad era militia commander linked to the 2013 Bayda and Baniyas massacres, as he attempted to flee the country, state-run SANA news agency reported.

Known by his alias Mihrac Ural, Kayali is accused of commanding armed groups that carried out killings alongside forces of ousted dictator Bashar al-Assad, including in the coastal province of Tartus, and in rural areas of Latakia, Aleppo and Idlib.

The Syrian Observatory for Human Rights cited unnamed sources who said Kayali was taken from his home in the Mansourieh area of Lebanon, not at a Syrian border crossing.

According to those sources, armed men with Lebanese accents entered the property, apprehended Kayali's wife and brother-in-law, and removed him from the premises. Lebanese authorities have not commented on this report.

Kayali, 70, has been linked to the killings in Bayda and Baniyas in May 2013, when regime forces and allied militias stormed residential areas in Tartus province and carried out summary executions of civilians.

The Syrian Network for Human Rights documented 264 deaths in Bayda, including 36 children and 28 women, and 195 in Ras al-Nabeh, including 56 children and 43 women.

A Human Rights Watch report published in September 2013 documented at least 248 deaths across the two locations, noting the real figure was likely higher.

Witness accounts described bodies left in streets, shops and basements, executions carried out with firearms and bladed weapons, and homes set alight alongside the dead.

Born in the Turkish city of Antakya in 1956, Kayali was active in armed organisations including the Popular Salvation Front in Turkey and the Front for the Liberation of the Iskenderun Brigade, both designated terrorist organisations by Ankara.

He fled to Syria in 1982, obtained Syrian citizenship and changed his name. He reportedly cultivated close ties to the al-Assad family through his wife.

When the Syrian civil war began in 2011, he took command of the Popular Front for the Liberation of the Iskenderun Brigade, which fought alongside regime forces, primarily along Syria's coast.

Kayali's detention follows that of Jamal Trabelsi, described in reports as his deputy, who was arrested by Syria's Internal Security Forces on 4 October.

Turkish authorities announced the separate arrest of Omar al-Khatib earlier this month as he attempted to reach Europe by sea. Media reports describe him as one of the planners of the Reyhanli bombing and say he coordinated with members of al-Assad-era intelligence services.

Syria's Interior Ministry has said it is continuing to pursue individuals accused of involvement in abuses under the former regime as part of efforts to achieve transitional justice.

Damascus has stepped up efforts to restore trust in Syria's government and its services following a 13-year-long war in the country that resulted in the deaths of hundreds of thousands and saw millions displaced and seeking refuge outside the country.

Trust in authorities in neighbouring Lebanon is among the lowest in the world, with 56% of Lebanese citizens believing the government does not care about them at all, about double the global figure (29%), according to the latest research by Gallup for Lloyd's Register Foundation's World Risk Poll.

The deadliest school shooting in US history took place at Virginia Tech on 16 April 2007, when South Korean student Seung-Hui Cho killed 32 people and wounded 17 others before taking his own life.

There have been more than 360 mass shootings in the US so far this year, according to the Gun Violence Archive, which defines a mass shooting as four or more people shot.

"Our hearts are with the terrified children and the families anxiously waiting to hear that their loved ones are safe," GIFFORDS, a gun safety organisation, said on X.

Thursday's shooting was followed by a painfully familiar chorus of calls for action on gun control, pleas that have resulted in little meaningful change.

In the face of decades of massacres akin to Thursday's, the powerful US pro-gun lobby has given millions of dollars to primarily conservative politicians in a successful bid to keep gun laws loose.

"They're killing our babies. Shredding our kids with lead. Forcing us to live in fear," said Janelle Bynum, an Oregon congresswoman, in a statement.
