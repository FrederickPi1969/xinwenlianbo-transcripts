Saudi Arabia vowed it would not hesitate to take "deterrent measures" against the Houthis after alerts were issued over possible drone attacks near Islam's holiest site, the first such alerts since the Iran war began in February.
Yemen's Houthis on Wednesday said they shot down a Saudi fighter jet, claiming they used munitions made by the group to target the warplane.

"The Yemeni Armed Forces, with Allah's aid and grace, succeeded in shooting down a Saudi F-15 fighter jet while it was carrying out hostile operations," Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree said on social media, while accusing Saudi Arabia of launching 450 strikes.

The spokesman also dismissed Saudi claims that the Houthis had targeted Islam's holy city of Mecca.

"Our operations target its oil facilities and military bases, which are far removed from the sacred sites," he said, adding that "fabrications and lies propagated by the criminal al-Saud regime cannot deceive anyone."

The Saudi coalition vowed retaliation, warning that the security of holy sites was a "red line".

"The security of the Two Holy Mosques and the pilgrims is a red line, and the Joint Forces Command of the Coalition will not hesitate to take the necessary and deterrent measures against the terrorist Houthi militia," the coalition said in a statement shared by spokesman Turki al-Maliki on X.

Saudi Arabia had issued brief alerts on Tuesday over possible attacks in Mecca and across swathes of the kingdom's south, which borders Yemen.

The alerts, including the first one issued in Mecca since the Iran war erupted in February, came a day after a wave of ballistic missile and drone attacks launched from Yemen wounded 13 people in the kingdom's south.

Pakistan's Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif condemned on Wednesday "in the strongest possible terms the dastardly and heinous" attack on Mecca.

The Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, a 57-member bloc of Muslim-majority countries, also condemned the "heinous" attack.

The Houthis are Zaydi Shia Muslims, a specific branch of Islam historically rooted in northern Yemen that is theologically distinct from both the Twelver Shia Islam dominant in Iran and Saudi Arabia's Wahhabi-influenced Sunni tradition.

Mecca is Islam's holiest site, home to the Kaaba — which Muslims worldwide turn toward for prayer — and the destination of the Hajj pilgrimage, one of the faith's Five Pillars or main tenets.

Any confirmed attack on the city would put the perpetrators beyond the pale in the eyes of virtually the entire Muslim world, Shia and Sunni alike.

Formally known as Ansar Allah, the Houthis have been engaged in renewed fighting with the internationally recognised government forces since Yemen's civil war reignited in July.

The fighting in the Arabian Peninsula's poorest country has forced 82,000 people to flee their homes since the start of September, according to the UN.

Yemen's permanent representative at the UN Abdullah al-Saadi said his compatriots were fighting to restore "national institutions across every inch of their land", and called on the international community to take action against the Houthis.

The status announced by von der Leyen during the State of the Union is an entirely new one, with details yet to be made clear. The European Commission’s President also proposed a new alliance with Ottawa covering tech, critical raw materials, energy and defence.
European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen announced on Wednesday that Canada has been invited to become the first “associate member” of the EU.

She announced the move during her 2026 State of the Union address in Strasbourg, to an audience including not only MEPs but also Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, who attended her speech in the midst of a trade war with the US.

Since late August, the Canadians have been pushing hard for the Europeans to build a closer relationship with them, though full EU membership is not an option as Canada is not a European state.

During her speech before the MEPs on Wednesday, von der Leyen invited the country to become “the first associate member of the European Union".

“We share one ocean, one set of values, one way of seeing the world. And we will now build our shared future as well,” she added.

Expectations had been high over the last week after Carney spoke about building a “unique alliance” with the EU.

Canada is not a European state geographically, but it shares the same values as the EU – human rights, democracy, rule of law – which are necessary criteria for becoming a member state.

Several options already exist for countries that don’t have full membership. Norway, for instance, is part of the single market, the EU’s borderless area of free movement of goods, persons, services and capital. Oslo and Brussels also collaborate on joint defence initiatives.

Switzerland also has access to the single market through several agreements – and in the years since Brexit, the United Kingdom and the EU have secured the largest and broadest trade and cooperation deal in the bloc's history, coverering a wide range of sectors. The UK is now taking part in several EU programmes such as Horizon Europe, the EU’s research and innovation programme.

Euronews has learned that Ottawa is already in talks with Brussels to join the Erasmus+ exchange program, Horizon Europe research grants, and mutual recognition of workers’ qualifications.

However, “associate member” is a completely new status, and key questions have not yet been answered – chief among them, whether Canada will have voting rights in EU institutions. Despite their regulatory harmonisation with the bloc, Norway and Switzerland have no say on EU legislation.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz floated the idea last May of an “associate membership” for Ukraine, which is urgently seeking to enter the EU. In a letter sent to EU leaders, he argued that this status would grant Ukraine access to the decision-making bodies – the European Council, the European Commission and the European Parliament – without voting rights or a dedicated portfolio.

It would also allow the country to tap into certain EU-funded programmes on a "step-by-step" basis.

It’s hard to see Canada in the same category as Ukraine, a country which is at war, but von der Leyen's announcement on Wednesday nonetheless sent a strong political signal in a volatile world where historical alliances are shifting.

“Europe and Canada believe in democracy. That power does not belong to the strongest, the richest, or the loudest – but to all of us,” she said. “Democracies have the freedom to choose with whom to work.”

She also called for a new alliance with Ottawa, which could include tech, defence, Arctic joint projects, but also energy, critical minerals and artificial intelligence.

The Al-Zaraa mine is near El-Nahud in Sudan’s West Kordofan region. The mine began to collapse on Tuesday after tunnels were dug very close together
At least 60 small-scale miners are dead, with many missing, after the collapse of a gold mine in Sudan's West Kordofan region.

The mine reportedly began to collapse on Tuesday after two shafts that had been dug close together began to give way.

One surviving miner Khair al-Sayyed Jabara told AFP there had been an overnight rescue operation since the collapse, and that fellow miners were having to dig using the same rudimentary tools they use to mine.

"Everyone's working very hard to retrieve the bodies or any survivors, but it's difficult. We need heavy machinery to clear the rock and soil."

Heavy machinery is often not readily available at small-scale mining sites. Sudan’s gold industry has become increasingly important since the civil war began in April 2023, providing a significant source of revenue in an economy devastated by conflict.

The civil war began in April 2023 between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces. As a result, the country is facing the world’s largest displacement crisis, with nearly 20 million people experiencing crisis-level hunger.

Updated: 16/09/2026 - 10:19 GMT+2
Technology companies gathered in Tokyo on 16 September 2026 for a one-day summit focused on physical AI, showcasing robots designed to perform tasks in the real world.
… More
Robots are getting to grips with the physical world in Tokyo, where technology companies are showcasing machines designed to handle objects, perform industrial tasks and work alongside humans.

The demonstrations offer a glimpse of how AI could move beyond screens and software, giving machines the ability to interact with their surroundings and carry out physical tasks.

Among the prototypes on display is a machine developed by THK, which picks up bottles, bags and boxes under the remote control of a single operator. Its articulated arms and grippers also allow it to hammer a nail into a piece of wood, demonstrating the precision and dexterity needed for more complex tasks.

Nachi, HatsuMuv and ugo are also presenting their latest robotic systems. Ugo is showcasing its semi-humanoid ugo Nova, which performs a drilling task under remote control.

Designed to work in environments built for humans, the robot has two seven-axis arms as well as additional joints in its head and waist. It is also designed to collect real-world data that can be used to train physical AI models.

But the demonstrations also reveal the technology’s current limits. Many of the robots still depend on human operators, with developers now working to give them greater autonomy as investment in AI-powered machines grows in Japan.

The nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office reports that up to $3 billion more is being spent on the war each month, depending on the intensity of fighting.
The US had spent more than $38 billion (€33 billion) on its war with Iran by the beginning of August, according to the country's Congressional Budget Office (CBO).

The estimate covers costs incurred by the US Department of Defence from the start of the conflict on 28 February to 1 August, just over five months later.

The Defence Department did not provide information to the CBO for its assessment, which instead relied on government databases and publicly available reports. The CBO said its estimates were therefore subject to “considerable uncertainty”.

Nevertheless, the figure broadly aligns with an estimate given by Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth in July, when he said the war had cost $37.5 billion.

The largest proportion of the cost is reportedly the $21.7 billion that has been spent on replacing munitions.

The CBO also warned about the depletion of missile-defence interceptors. It estimates the US has probably used between half and two-thirds of its inventory of those weapons since June 2025, leaving stocks depleted for several years.

On Monday, a Pentagon inspector general report warned that the US-led military campaign against Iran, known as Operation Epic Fury, had resulted in “strategic inventory shortfalls”.

The CBO also warned that the conflict is having a wider impact on the US economy, largely because disruption to oil and gas shipments through the Strait of Hormuz and Red Sea has pushed up energy costs.

The estimate was prepared in response to a request for information from Representative Brendan Boyle, the top Democrat on the House Budget committee.

Boyle criticised the amount spent: “this nonpartisan CBO report makes clear that the war has also cost American taxpayers tens of billions of dollars and counting, while continuing to drive up costs.”

“Donald Trump and Republicans have spent years telling Americans that we cannot afford to help families here at home,” Boyle said in a statement, "but apparently they can find tens of billions of dollars, and potentially much more, for a reckless war that is leaving Americans to pay the price.”

In June, the White House requested an additional $87.6 billion (€76 billion) in supplemental funding, much of it for the war effort. Congress has not approved the request.

Yemen has seen a new wave of displacement as fighting between Houthi rebels and Saudi‑backed forces intensifies, forcing families from their homes in Taiz province. On Tuesday, 15 September, displaced people arrived at makeshift camps with few belongings, while others built shelters from wooden branches and plastic sacks.

The US is planning to deliver a package of 40,000 powerful bombs to Israel as part of an upcoming arms deal. The $2.8 billion (€2.42 billion) deal includes 2,000-pound (900 kg) Mk84 and BLU-117 aircraft bombs, according to two US officials and a person familiar with the plan who spoke on condition of anonymity.

The same weapons prompted concerns about the possibility of mass casualties in Gaza, and their delivery was put on hold by the the Biden administration two years ago. Trump lifted that hold when he took office again.

Congress has been informally notified about the pending deal, most of which is to be paid for with foreign military financing — in which US taxpayer dollars are given to Israel to turn around and buy the weapons from the United States.

The sale signals that while the relationship between President Donald Trump and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu is reportedly strained, the US-Israel military relationship has not significantly weakened.

The two countries launched the war against Iran in February. Trump had expressed frustration with Netanyahu tied to the Iran conflict before their latest meeting in July at the White House.

Last year, the Trump administration bypassed a normal congressional review process and approved a nearly $3 billion sale to Israel that included more than 35,500 of the bombs. The administration then approved a new series of arms sales to Israel totalling $6.67 billion in January.

A new GSMA report finds that 3.4 billion people remain offline despite living within mobile broadband coverage, as rising component costs push smartphones further out of reach for the world's poorest.
Global telecoms industry body GSMA is calling for urgent action to make smartphones more affordable for people living in low- and middle-income countries so that everyone can “benefit from the AI revolution”.

The call to action comes as the organisation launches its State of Mobile Internet Connectivity Report 2026, an annual study of global mobile connectivity.

The report found that while 4.8 billion people now use mobile internet on their own device, 3.4 billion do not, despite 90% of them living within mobile broadband coverage. GSMA refers to this as the “usage gap”.

Growth in the world’s digital population is also slowing, with roughly 160 million new users coming online in 2025, compared with 190 million in 2024.

Commenting on the report, GSMA Director General Vivek Badrinath said: “Artificial intelligence has the potential to improve lives on an unprecedented scale, but AI is meaningless if people cannot get online in the first place.”

“The greatest risk is not simply an AI divide between countries, but between people who can afford to participate in the digital economy and those who cannot.”

GSMA says it is important to “avoid a large portion of the population in emerging economies being completely left out of the digital economy” and is calling for a number of actions.

A key factor behind growing global digital inequality, according to the report, is the “sharp increase in the cost of smartphone memory and chipsets, driven by global demand for AI infrastructure and data centres”.

Memory prices have skyrocketed recently, more than doubling between the third quarter of 2025 and the first quarter of 2026, followed by a further 80-90% increase during the second quarter of 2026.

These rising component costs are then passed on to consumers, with prices for entry-level smartphones becoming unaffordable for many people around the world. This dynamic, GSMA says, threatens to undo the “years of progress made on closing the mobile usage gap”.

“Unless we protect the affordability of entry-level smartphones, billions of people risk being excluded from the next generation of digital services before they have even had the opportunity to experience the internet,” said Badrinath.

This year’s report found that, by the end of 2025, entry-level smartphones cost the poorest 20% of people in low- and middle-income countries up to 44% of their average monthly income and up to 76% in sub-Saharan Africa, with prices expected to continue rising.

GSMA believes the sharp rise in smartphone prices will translate into the “largest annual decline on record” in smartphone shipments globally, with emerging markets the most vulnerable.

To address the world’s growing digital inequality, the organisation is calling for manufacturers to reduce the price of entry-level smartphones to $30, arguing that this could “make devices affordable for almost 1.6 billion people” while a $20 price point could make them affordable for around 2.2 billion people currently living within mobile broadband coverage.

The telecoms organisation is also calling on chipset and memory manufacturers to “take meaningful steps to increase availability of affordable components for entry-level handsets," arguing that this would help smartphone manufacturers make their entry-level devices more affordable.

GSMA also recommends addressing “other key barriers to digital inclusion" such as “low literacy and digital skills, safety and security concerns, and availability of relevant content and services”.

As part of the plea agreement, Saab agreed to cooperate with ongoing federal investigations in the United States and to hand over 195 million dollars (some €168.9 million) derived from the corruption scheme.
Alex Saab, a close ally of ousted Venezuelan president Nicolás Maduro, pleaded guilty on Tuesday to a money-laundering charge linked to an alleged bribery scheme to secure lucrative public contracts in Venezuela.

As part of the plea agreement, Saab agreed to cooperate with ongoing federal investigations in the United States and to hand over 195 million dollars (some €168.9 million) derived from the corruption scheme.

Saab, 54, was deported to the United States in May by Venezuela's interim president, Delcy Rodríguez. US authorities have been investigating the Colombia-born businessman for more than a decade.

The money-laundering charge carries a maximum sentence of 20 years in prison. However, prosecutors have agreed to recommend a term at the lower end of the guideline range and to seek further reductions if his cooperation proves useful, including assistance in investigations targeting four co-defendants whose names have not been disclosed.

Saab had already been charged in 2019, during Donald Trump's first administration. He was arrested in 2020 during a refuelling stop in Cape Verde, in what the Venezuelan government claimed at the time was a high-level humanitarian mission bound for Iran.

During his court appearance on Tuesday, Saab told Judge Kathleen Williams that he takes the antidepressant Zoloft every day along with other medication for what he described as "post-traumatic stress disorder" following his 2020 arrest.

The then president Joe Biden pardoned Saab in 2023 in exchange for the release of several US citizens imprisoned in Venezuela. The deal was part of a failed attempt by Biden's White House to persuade Maduro to hold free presidential elections.

The decision was sharply criticised by Republicans and by senior federal law-enforcement officials, who immediately opened new investigations into Saab over other alleged offences not covered by the pardon, whose scope was limited.

US authorities have for years described Saab as Maduro's 'bag man', a term used to refer to someone tasked with handling or moving money for another person. They could now call him to testify about the character of the former Venezuelan president, who is awaiting trial in Manhattan on drug-related charges after being captured in January during a US military operation.

The new legal proceedings against Saab and Maduro come as the Trump administration reshapes the United States' relations with Venezuela and focuses on tapping the country's vast oil reserves.

This month, Trump announced a partnership with Venezuelan oil tycoon Alejandro Betancourt under which the US government will secure a stake and access to cost-price crude shipments from fields with an estimated production potential of 65 billion barrels.

The helicopter crashed near a storage building in the San Fernando Valley, as news crews were covering a car accident that left at least two dead and six others injured
An NBC news television helicopter crashed into a car park in Los Angeles on Tuesday, killing at least three people.

The helicopter crashed near a storage building in the neighbourhood of Chatsworth in the San Fernando Valley, as news crews were covering the bus crash that left at least two people dead and six others injured, according to the Los Angeles Fire Department. A fourth person was taken to hospital, though their condition was unknown.

Colleen Williams, an NBC4 Los Angeles anchor, confirmed during a live broadcast that the station’s chopper went down just before 7 pm.

“We are going to take a couple of minutes to think about that and process it,” she said, her voice shaking with emotion. Moments later, coverage was abruptly cut off.

The Los Angeles Fire Department said it was still working to determine how many of the victims had been on board the aircraft.

At least one person killed was on the ground outside, spokesperson Captain Branden Silverman told reporters.

The crash occurred near the scene of a separate deadly bus accident, though the connection between the two incidents, if any, was not immediately clear.

Photos and videos online showed around half of an SUV lodged into the side of an MTA bus and a large group of fire crew working around the crash.

Two people were reportedly killed and six others were injured after an SUV crashed into a city bus in Los Angeles, officials said.

According to the fire department, more than 50 firefighters responded to the helicopter crash. The fire had damaged four vehicles and two storage containers but was out by Tuesday evening, according to officials.

Silverman described the helicopter as “pretty well destroyed” and said they’re going to have to go through the rubble to see how many victims were on board.

According to media reports, the helicopter came down into a park serving a large single-storey building, striking or landing near several parked vehicles.

The National Transportation Safety Board and the Federal Aviation Administration will be handling the investigation.

Most of the Kennedy Centre in Washington, DC will close for repairs following a board vote Tuesday, hours after a federal judge again blocked the landmark venue from being renamed after President Donald Trump.

“Simply put, Defendants cannot install memorials for President Trump or anyone or anything else at the Kennedy Center without Congress’s blessing,” US District Judge Christopher Cooper said in the latest ruling Tuesday.

Trump, in response to the ruling, said the $257 million (€223 million) allocated by Congress for the repairs would only be released if renaming was permitted. Federal courts have twice ruled cannot be met without Congressional approval.

Judge Cooper ruled in May that the Kennedy Center illegally added Trump’s name to the building, ordering it removed. The institution’s leaders complied in June but left a tarp and scaffolding in place where Trump’s name was once displayed.

Trump, who was named the Kennedy Centre’s chairman last year by loyalists on the board, participated in the virtual board meeting and said on social media he’d given $17 million to an endowment intended to support the institution.

The meeting included testy exchanges at points between Trump and Rep. Joyce Beatty, an Ohio Democrat who has led the campaign to block efforts to memorialise the president at the Kennedy Centre.

The developments have thrown into limbo the fate of one of the US's most prominent cultural institutions, a place where presidents, lawmakers and celebrities of all political ideologies have gathered for decades to recognise and celebrate the best in American arts and culture.

The venue’s leaders have long argued that the building, which began construction in 1965, was in dire need of renovations. A partial ceiling collapse in the building’s main hallway earlier this month intensified the calls for renovations.

“The building is not currently built to last; the chairman knows that because he is among the best builders in the world,” Brian Ballard, Kennedy Centre board member, said in a statement.

But Trump’s insistence on a physical recognition of his work, reminiscent of his tendency to add his name to buildings as a real estate developer, has been a persistent sticking point.

It's the latest setback in Trump’s second-term bid to dramatically remake the US capital in his gilded tastes. He’s demolished the East Wing of the White House and is replacing it with a ballroom and plans to build a triumphal arch near Arlington National Cemetery. He’s also eyeing a golf course renovation along the Potomac River.

The Kennedy Centre board voted in August to inscribe his name on the Kennedy Centre’s facade so it would read “The John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts Restored and Renovated By President Donald J. Trump.” If the Trump Kennedy Centre Fund reached $100 million, (approximately €86.66 million), another inscription would be added reading “Endowed by the Trump Kennedy Center Fund.”

There are concerns that the administration may seek to demolish the Kennedy Center. According to a filing last month, administration lawyers told the court that absent recognition of the president, the Kennedy Center would struggle to raise money for renovations.
