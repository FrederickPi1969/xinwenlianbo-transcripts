Brazilians are voting across the country, with polls open from 8:00 to 17:00 Brasília time. As voting is digital, first results should appear within three hours of closing.
Brazilians began voting this Sunday in the first round of the presidential election, in which President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva of the Workers' Party (PT) is seeking re-election against Senator Flávio Bolsonaro of the Liberal Party and son of Jair Bolsonaro.

The election is attracting international attention, as it will determine whether this Latin American economic powerhouse remains under left-wing rule or joins the rightward shift sweeping South America, championed by US President Donald Trump.

Soon to turn 81, veteran leftist Lula da Silva is running for a fourth and final term, warning voters that Brazil's sovereignty is at risk because of pressure exerted by Trump. Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, 45, is making his debut in the presidential race, in which he aims to follow in his father's footsteps, Jair Bolsonaro who is currently serving a 27-year prison sentence, who governed the country between 2019 and 2022 and pursued policies of close alignment with the United States.

According to opinion polls, which put the two leading candidates in a statistical tie, Lula da Silva (45% of voting intentions) faces a tough battle against the right-wing senator Flávio Bolsonaro (43%), aged 45, who has been emphasising his proximity to the White House and insisting that Brazil needs to strike better deals with the Trump administration, particularly when it comes to the economy of rare minerals.

Polls also suggest that neither candidate will cross the 50% threshold needed for an outright victory this Sunday. A second round of voting is therefore expected on 25 October.

Almost 160 million citizens are eligible to vote in these elections. According to the Superior Electoral Court (TSE), 158 745 463 voters are expected to cast their ballots this Sunday. Voting is compulsory and those who do not vote must provide a justification.

Brazilians will also elect 27 state governors, state deputies, all 513 federal deputies in the Chamber of Deputies and 54 of the country's 81 senators in Sunday's vote.

Overseas, voting is only for the President and Vice-President, and around 918 000 registered voters scattered around the world are expected to take part.

In some places, voting has been under way for several hours, as is the case in Portugal, home to the second-largest Brazilian electoral constituency abroad, while in others polls have already closed.

According to provisional results published so far, Lula da Silva is ahead in Malaysia, Singapore, China, South Korea, Australia and New Zealand. Flávio Bolsonaro is winning in the Philippines and Japan.

With more than 99.9 per cent of votes counted, Prime Minister Andris Kulbergs' United List party was in the lead with 35.4 percent, according to state TV.
Latvia's Prime Minister Andris Kulbergs' centre-right United List party has won the Baltic state's parliamentary election, preliminary results showed Sunday.

With more than 99.9 percent of votes counted, the United List led with 35.4 percent, according to state television.

In the 100-seat parliament, Kulbergs' United List party secured 42 seats, a record for the post-independence history of the former Soviet republic, local media reports.

Kulbergs, who became prime minister only in May, said the result was higher than expected and matched the lead shown in exit polls. "That means people truly want change," he said, adding that this meant "economic growth" and "a future for (Latvia's) children."

His coalition, which pre-election polls had suggested would weaken, will not survive in its current form. One member looked set to lose its seats, and others fared worse than they had hoped.

Populist parties, however, also performed strongly, helped by support from Latvia's Russian-speaking minority, after a campaign dominated by fears over neighbouring Russia and the cost of living.

More than four years after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, security remains central to Latvian politics. A member of NATO and the European Union since 2004, this small Balkan state sees Russia as its main security threat and has sharply raised defence spending in response.

"Because we are the external border of Europe, we must have a strong economy... a strong border region," Kulbergs said. "That is the main task."

Rising living costs, low wages, a strained healthcare system and long-term population decline also featured prominently in campaign debates.

Most parties expected to enter parliament support both high military spending and continued aid to Kyiv. The exception is the Sovereign Power party, which opposes military aid to Ukraine and further increases in defence spending. Having campaigned on cost-of-living issues, it looked set to finish third.

However, it has been criticised as pro-Russian by its detractors, and most major parties have ruled out working with it.

Latvia First, a conservative party run by a property magnate often compared with US President Donald Trump, looked set for second place.

Both populist parties were seen as benefiting from changes in voting patterns among the Russian-speaking minority who make up a third of the population.

They had long rallied around the Harmony party, which is broadly seen as pro-Russia and was at one time the country's biggest party.

However, Harmony's support collapsed dramatically in the last election, held shortly after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, and it lost its parliamentary representation.

Japan’s military said the missile landed in waters outside its exclusive economic zone, while South Korea's military, which often accuses the North of exaggerating its capabilities, said it travelled more than 700 kilometres.
North Korea announced Saturday that it had test-fired an intermediate-range ballistic missile, claiming the weapon can manoeuvre in flight to evade missile defences in South Korea and Japan.

The launch comes as tensions between the two Koreas run high over landmine explosions along their border.

Kim Yo Jong, the influential sister of leader Kim Jong Un, said her brother oversaw the test. She called the missile "strategic", a term that suggests it is meant to carry nuclear warheads. She also claimed it can alter its trajectory at low altitudes and has unspecified artificial intelligence capabilities, which she said would make it extremely hard to intercept.

Japan’s military said the missile landed in waters outside its exclusive economic zone, while the South Korean military, which often accuses North Korea of exaggerating its military capabilities, said the missile travelled more than 700 kilometres.

According to South Korea's Joint Chiefs of Staff, the North Korean missile was fired early Saturday from the eastern coastal Wonsan area. Japan's Defense Ministry later put the distance at roughly 680 kilometres.

In her statement, Kim Yo Jong pointed to the gap between the two US allies' figures as proof that they could not follow the missile in flight.

"Whenever we applied the wavelike trajectory mode to missiles, the enemy failed to track the targets from their first skip points," she said. "There is no way to avoid this attack."

The launch followed a day after she mocked Seoul's demand that Pyongyang apologise for the 21 September landmine explosions. The blasts went off about 10 metres south of the military demarcation line and wounded three South Korean soldiers.

A joint inspection by South Korea and the US-led United Nations Command concluded that North Korea had planted the mines, and the command later found that Pyongyang had breached the armistice that halted the 1950-53 Korean War.

Seoul has also asked the North to stop fortifying the border with mines and anti-tank barriers. That work has sped up since Kim Jong Un dropped the goal of reconciliation in 2024 and branded Seoul a permanent adversary.

Kim Yo Jong, who may be the second most powerful person in Pyongyang, called Seoul's demands "utterly pathetic" on Friday and rejected any North Korean responsibility for the blasts, warning of retaliation if the South fires on troops working along the border.

The Wonsan launch is the newest in a string of recent military displays, which also include missile tests, live-fire drills and the commissioning of a new warship. Kim Jong Un is showing off his expanding nuclear programme even as Washington urges a return to diplomacy.

Wonsan also hosted the Sept. 20 test of what state media described as a hypersonic missile system. It belongs to a family of weapons built to overwhelm missile defences in South Korea and Japan, including solid-fuel intermediate-range missiles first revealed in 2024.

South Korea's military said it had raised its surveillance level for further launches and was sharing information closely with Washington and Tokyo. The US Pacific Command said the launch "does not pose an immediate threat to American personnel or territory or to our allies."

Kim has mostly turned his back on diplomacy with the US and South Korea since his nuclear talks with President Donald Trump broke down in 2019. He has shifted his focus to Moscow, sending thousands of troops and large shipments of weapons to back Vladimir Putin's war in Ukraine. After years of heavier testing, his arsenal of nuclear-capable missiles now includes long-range systems that could potentially reach the U.S. mainland and shorter-range ones that threaten South Korea and Japan.

In August, Trump scaled back a large joint exercise with South Korea, apparently hoping to restart talks.

Around 3.4 million voters in Bosnia are choosing the three members of the country’s multi-ethnic presidency, lawmakers for the national Parliament, and heads of institutions for the country’s two entities.
Polls opened Sunday in Bosnia's general election, where some 3.4 million voters are choosing the three members of the multi-ethnic presidency, national lawmakers, and leaders of the country's two entities: the Muslim-majority Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina and the Serb-dominated Republika Srpska.

The complex system of institutions was created by the US-brokered 1995 peace agreement to manage divisions among Muslim Bosniaks, Orthodox Serbs and Catholic Croats.

Early reports show voting was delayed at several polling places due to problems with new digital voting technology, which was introduced to address earlier concerns about vote tampering. Polls close at 7 pm. local time.

The election comes as many citizens in the multiethnic Balkan state face everyday problems, while some leading politicians stoke the war-era tensions that have kept the country divided since the 1992-95 conflict.

For the first time, authorities are using electronic voter identification and ballot scanning to curb the irregularities and organised manipulation that undermined confidence in earlier elections.

Most of the 75 parties running are organised along ethnic lines. The Bosniak nationalist Party of Democratic Action has campaigned on keeping Bosnia united against Serb separatism, while many Bosnian Croats back ethnic parties such as the local branch of Croatia's ruling Croatian Democratic Union.

Izetbegovic, who was beaten by Becirovic in 2022, is seeking a third term as president of the centre-right SDA, the largest Bosniak party in the nation.

Many disillusioned citizens say politicians use nationalism to avoid tackling real problems like low living standards and the exodus of young people. Mostar voter Ivan Silic said nothing has improved since he first voted and that politicians live well while everyone else's situation stays the same.

Despite not being on the ballot, the campaign has been dominated by Milorad Dodik, the pro-Russian, separatist former president of Republika Srpska. Dodik is banned from public office after being convicted of defying the international envoy who oversees the peace agreement.

Fresh from meeting Russia's president Vladimir Putin in Moscow, Dodik leads a party, the Alliance of Independent Social Democrats, that is likely to win in Republika Srpska.

At a final rally Friday in Banja Luka, attended by Russia's ambassador, Igor Khalabukov, Dodik declared Bosnia is "dead" and "an impossible country". "It doesn't make any sense," he stressed.

Off the ballot for the first time in almost 30 years, he is still in command and is present across the country's media, says Dragan Bursac, an analyst. In Bursac's view, the ban is not working, and Dodik is exploiting it.

Bosnia has been peaceful in recent years, but its politicians have kept ethnic divisions alive. Bosnian Serb leaders such as Dodik have pushed for their entity to break away, raising Western fears of renewed instability.

The Balkan nation remains one of Europe's poorest countries, still recovering from a war that began after it declared independence from Serb-led Yugoslavia and ethnic Serbs took up arms to carve out their own state. That conflict killed more than 100,000 people and displaced millions.

The country is formally seeking European Union membership, but progress has stalled despite EU efforts to push its leaders toward reform.

Major powers continue to influence socio-economic and political life in the small Balkan nation. Russia is holding down the EU's efforts to expand its membership throughout the Balkans, including Bosnia, while continuing to back the Republika Srpska.
