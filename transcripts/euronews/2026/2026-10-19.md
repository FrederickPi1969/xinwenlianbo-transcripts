Macron increasingly isolated amid unrest across France

Saturday’s violent protests and looting across France have deepened Macron’s political isolation, raising questions over his authority.
With only months left in his presidency, Emmanuel Macron faces mounting social unrest and political criticism that could ultimately shape his legacy.

After student protests over living conditions erupted into violence last Saturday, Macron has come under fire from across the political spectrum over his handling of the security situation and his response to grievances over education, the cost of living and inflation.

The unrest comes as France grapples with record public debt, soaring fuel prices and escalating international conflicts.

The Yellow Vests movement, which has helped coordinate the protests, announced a “second act” on Monday, with further demonstrations planned for next weekend.

Addressing the nation, Macron condemned violence and announced new operational procedures for security forces.

The Paris prosecutor’s office reported 855 arrests linked to the demonstrations, with 557 people placed in police custody, 433 of whom were adults, representing 78% of those detained.

“Looking at these images, we have all asked ourselves: What was going through their minds? How can French people do this to France?” Macron said. “Nothing justifies it, nothing explains it, nothing excuses it. Tonight, we all feel anger and shame.”

The response is likely to add to the pressure on Macron, whose performance was viewed unfavourably by 82% of respondents in an Ipsos-BVA poll conducted in October.

The French president is currently on a diplomatic trip to the Gulf, which the Élysée says aims to secure energy supplies as prices rise. However, his decision to leave Paris amid the crisis has drawn criticism, with some accusing Macron of evading the situation.

Across the political spectrum, several potential presidential candidates have argued that Macron has lost authority and that his response came too late.

“We have the feeling that the government is collapsing under its own weight, that it is completely overwhelmed,” said Jordan Bardella, president of the far-right National Rally, which is leading in polls ahead of France’s 2027 presidential election.

Speaking at a press conference at the party’s headquarters on Monday, Bardella pledged tough measures to restore order if the National Rally came to power. “France is caught in a vice between powerlessness and violence,” he said.

Polls put Marine Le Pen ahead of all her potential rivals in the 2027 presidential race. She faced Emmanuel Macron in the 2017 and 2022 run-offs, losing both times.

In the aftermath of the looting, Le Pen said France had been “ravaged” by “an alliance of criminals from the banlieues and politicised professional rioters”.

Le Pen is expected to focus her campaign on migration and law and order, issues that have traditionally resonated with far-right voters, while playing down foreign policy issues such as the war in Ukraine and wider geopolitical tensions.

Macron has also faced criticism from his former prime ministers, Édouard Philippe and Gabriel Attal, both of whom are running in the 2027 presidential election.

“Like millions of French people, I would have preferred to see decisions and initiatives aimed at restoring order,” Philippe wrote on X.

Attal called on the state to employ all means "so justice is done" including AI facial recognition and deploying the army "if necessary". Macron did not announce extra security measures on Sunday, asking local authorities to coordinate with the police.

The divided French left, riven by internal tensions, accused the government of failing to protect citizens.

“This is unprecedented in its gravity,” Raphaël Glucksmann, a French MEP and social-democratic contender in the 2027 presidential election, told RTL radio. “I had the feeling that France was dissolving, that the Republic was disintegrating, that the state no longer existed.”

Bruno Retailleau, another presidential hopeful representing the conservative Les Républicains party, described Macron as “completely out of his depth, incapable of deploying the full force of the state against the chaos threatening the Republic”.

On Monday, Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez admitted there had been a “lack of anticipation” in preparations. He offered to resign “because I felt responsible for everything that happened”, but Macron rejected his request.

Justice Minister Gérald Darmanin likewise acknowledged that the authorities had “undoubtedly failed” in their response, citing “shortcomings in intelligence gathering”.

Qatar is preparing a new regional defence partnership while keeping channels open with Tehran, arguing that dialogue and economic ties offer a route to lasting peace. Doha also warned of an energy disruption “peak” with more difficulties on the horizon “in the coming days and months”.
Qatar will soon announce a new defence partnership, Deputy Prime Minister Sheikh Saoud bin Abdulrahman Al Thani said on Monday, while also calling for talks and closer economic ties with Iran to prevent further conflict.

Sheikh Saoud, also minister of state for defence affairs, told the Global Security Forum in Doha that Qatar was using lessons from the Iran war to better protect itself and develop new defence plans with its neighbours.

The planned defence partnership would help Qatar counter ballistic missiles, drones and cyberattacks, he said, also focusing on water security.

He also said Qatar would soon announce a local defence production hub, with local manufacturing or investments in defence companies, working with a regional partner and allies including the United States, Britain and France.

He said that “more than 90% of (Iran’s) attacks” on the region were countered successfully by the Gulf region due to cooperation with the US, through decades of military investments, training and shared experience and that developments of Al Udeid airbase — the biggest US base in the Middle East — would continue in line with their needs.

Sheikh Saoud told the Global Security Forum that Doha had stayed in contact with Tehran at different levels despite Iran’s attacks. “We never stop our communication,” the deputy prime minister said.

The deputy prime minister called for talks between Iran and the six Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries, as well as Pakistan and Turkey, once the US and Iran reach a settlement. International allies should also be consulted, he said.

Until then, he said Qatar was working with Pakistan and Oman on mediation, comparing it to a long-distance race that requires determination and resilience.

Sheikh Saoud said that the Gulf countries could emerge from the conflict with stronger economies and new partnerships, as they had after previous crises.

“Iran is our neighbour,” he said, arguing that closer economic ties could help prevent another conflict and that the talks should build trust and address security concerns on both sides, with a non-aggression agreement among the possible next steps.

He pointed to France and Germany’s joint work on Airbus as an example of countries building economic links.

Qatar’s deputy prime minister said that Qatar and Iran also share a major offshore gas field, giving both countries an interest in avoiding further disruption.

He also warned that ongoing disruptions in the energy sector continue to send shockwaves worldwide, and that crises in the region can disrupt the “chain of supply from east to west and also from west to east”.

“We haven’t reached the peak with respect to energy,” and warned of more difficulties “in the coming days and months”.

He also rejected transit fees in the Strait of Hormuz and said uncertainty over shipping would raise costs for consumers.

Sheikh Saoud said energy disruptions and other regional conflicts pose an ongoing threat to Qatar and the Gulf countries, and that regional instability can affect Gulf investment toward Europe.

Demonstrators blocked the entrances to the Louvre in Paris on Monday, as tensions remained high following a weekend of protests, clashes and looting in France.
… More
Protesters blocked the entrances to the Louvre museum in Paris on Monday, following a weekend of demonstrations over the cost of living that escalated into violence and looting in several French cities.

A sign on the museum gates read “Entrance closed”, while demonstrators handed out leaflets and spoke to passers-by about their action.

The Louvre protest was linked to a wider movement initially led by high school students, but the weekend unrest extended beyond student demonstrations.

A first-year student at the École du Louvre said the action aimed to bring the cultural sector into the movement, citing budget cuts and worsening working conditions at the museum.

She also criticised the police response to Saturday’s protest as repression of a peaceful demonstration.

The World Food Programme will stop monthly assistance to half a million people in Gaza because of a funding shortfall, the UN agency said on Monday.

The cuts leave 1.1 million Palestinians, around half of Gaza’s population, receiving food or cash each month. Since the October 2025 ceasefire, the WFP had been reaching between 1.5 million and 1.8 million people a month.

“This has been a lifeline. But now, rather than being able to sustain and scale up that assistance, we’re actually having to reduce it,” Shaun Hughes, the WFP’s country director, told AFP in east Jerusalem.

"And that’s not because we don’t have humanitarian access to deliver food; that’s because we don’t have funding."

Families receiving food will now get one parcel instead of two and a smaller bag of wheat flour. The 75,000 families who receive cash will see monthly payments fall from 1,250 to 750 shekels ($400 to $240).

“People are not safe. Airstrikes continue on a daily basis. People continue to be displaced,” he said. “Almost everybody remains without a permanent home to live in. And the humanitarian conditions are brutal.”

According to the latest assessments, 1.4 million people, two-thirds of the population, remain “acutely food insecure” and need aid to survive. The WFP warns that needs could rise again with the onset of winter.

In August 2025, the UN-backed Integrated Food Security Phase Classification declared famine conditions in Gaza, a finding Israel called a lie. The classification was lifted in December, two months after the US-brokered ceasefire, though Israeli strikes have continued.

The WFP’s resources have fallen sharply, partly because US President Donald Trump drastically cut contributions to UN agencies. It needs about $450 million over the next six months for Gaza and the West Bank, but has received only around half its usual funding for the Palestinian territories this year. In September, it halved the number of people it helps in the West Bank to 200,000.

Israel has let more goods into Gaza under the ceasefire but still blocks items it considers to have potential military use, including prosthetic limbs, according to the UN. It also accuses the UN of bias.

“Now is not the time in the history of Palestine and Gaza to turn away from what is happening in Gaza,” Hughes said.

Washington has taken the rare measure to issue a red alert to US citizens in 12 countries across the Middle East, citing risks of potential attacks on US interests.
US embassies across the entire Middle East issued identical security alerts on Monday, warning their citizens in Israel, Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Lebanon, Jordan, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, the UAE, Yemen and Iran to exercise heightened vigilance.

The alerts warn all US citizens of potential flight cancellations, airspace closures and travel disruptions due to the risk of military escalation linked to Yemen’s Houthis and Iran.

The warning covering 12 countries states that “the hostilities have the potential to escalate rapidly beyond that immediate area” and to “develop contingency plans in advance.”

“Iran and groups supportive of Iran may target other US interests overseas or at locations associated with the United States and Americans throughout the world, including US businesses and other institutions,” the alert said.

In Saudi Arabia, the embassy warned of a “complex security environment,” as “Iranian-supported Houthis have engaged in hostilities against Saudi Arabia, including attacks on civilian airports and other critical infrastructure resulting in civilian casualties and severe travel disruptions.”

The alert also states that “Iran and groups supportive of Iran may target other U.S. interests overseas or at locations associated with the United States and Americans throughout the world, including US businesses and other institutions.”

In Qatar, the US embassy urged its citizens on Sunday to monitor official alerts and local media, avoid demonstrations and large gatherings, and follow instructions from local authorities.⁠

EU tourist arrivals fell from 5 million in 2019 to 160,000 in 2025 as sanctions, cancelled flights and other restrictions due to Russia's ongoing war in Ukraine severed Western leisure travel, prompting Moscow to look east.
Russia has introduced visa-free entry for Vietnamese citizens as part of a broader effort to deal with the collapse of Western visits since its 2022 full-scale invasion of Ukraine, with visitors from Asia and the Middle East.

The number of tourists from EU countries travelling to Russia fell to around 160,000 in 2025, from just over 5 million in 2019. Cancelled direct flights, payment card restrictions and sanctions have effectively severed leisure travel from Europe and the US, and inbound tourism as a whole declined further in the first half of 2026.

The Vietnam visa-free decree signed by Vladimir Putin and taking effect on Thursday covers tourists, businesspeople, members of delegations and transit passengers.

Vietnamese nationals seeking long-term residency, employment or study in Russia will still require a visa. For several years, Russian citizens have been able to enter Vietnam visa-free for stays of up to 45 days.

Vietnamese visitors to Russia rose 69% in the first half of 2026 compared with the same period in 2025, according to the Association of Tour Operators of Russia, which projects a further 25-30% increase after the visa requirement was removed.

Vietnam has become the third most popular foreign destination for Russian travellers. Figures were also rising on the other end: the Vietnam National Administration of Tourism recorded 1,134,000 Russian visitors in the first nine months of 2026, a 160% increase, making Russia its third-largest source market.

The visa arrangement followed an early September meeting in Moscow between Putin and Vietnamese President To Lam, who described the Russian leader as "a great friend and close brother of the Vietnamese people."

Moscow and Beijing introduced reciprocal visa-free travel in late 2025, with Chinese arrivals growing but falling short of official expectations.

The Russian government has projected 4.2 million Chinese visitors by year-end and 5.5 million in 2027. Up to 516,000 Chinese tourists arrived in the first half of 2026, a pace analysts described as well below those targets.

Safety concerns have been cited as a factor, with reports of drone incidents over Moscow's airports attracting attention in Chinese media.

Vietnam has not joined Western sanctions against Russia over the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 and does not supply weapons to Kyiv. Hanoi has called for a ceasefire and negotiations but has avoided publicly criticising the Kremlin. China maintains a similar position of official neutrality.

The 43-year-old male driver was detained by civilians, Mason said, and then arrested by police, who found a knife in his car.
Australian independent lawmaker Helen Haines was deliberately struck by a car and suffered "critical injuries" on Monday in what Prime Minister Anthony Albanese described as an attack.

Haines was hurt outside her office in Wangaratta, just over 200 kilometres northwest of Melbourne, police said.

The 65-year-old "suffered what has been described as critical injuries", Victoria Police Acting Superintendent Ashley Mason said, without identifying the victim in line with police custom in Australia.

The 43-year-old male driver was detained by civilians, Mason said, and then arrested by police, who found a knife in his car.

"This wasn't an accident. There was a deliberate act," Albanese told national broadcaster ABC.

He also told journalists: "All of our thoughts are with Helen, her family, her friends, her team, and her community at this extremely difficult time. We are praying for her full recovery."

The alleged attacker's motive remained unknown, Albanese said, with Australia's counter-terrorism squad activated.

The Australian Federal Police has documented a steady rise in threats and abuse directed towards the nation's politicians in recent years.

They established a dedicated team last year to investigate groups and individuals targeting lawmakers.

"If you look at anyone's social media feeds and look at the comments, people will say things that they would never say face to face."

Haines entered parliament in 2019 after working as a nurse and midwife, becoming known for stances such as supporting stronger climate action.

She was widely praised for her efforts to increase transparency in politics and crack down on corruption.

"This afternoon, our beloved Helen was involved in an incident outside her Wangaratta Electorate Office, and is receiving medical care," her family said in a statement.

"We thank the swift assistance of bystanders and the dedicated health workers who assisted her, and police for their response."

"Our family, and Helen's staff, will be making no further comment at this time, and ask that their privacy be respected by the public and media," the statement said.

"She is fiercely intelligent but, most importantly, she's just a thoroughly decent human being," he said.

Corruption is a top concern for many Brazilian voters ahead of the weekend runoff vote but experts say its greatest impact may not be on which candidates voters choose, but on whether they decide to sit out of the election altogether.
Firing off personal attacks, Brazil's President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and right-wing senator Flávio Bolsonaro held an acrimonious first debate on Sunday, a week before a tight presidential election runoff.

A chaotic exchange between the two men, who called each other liars on multiple occasions, accusing each other of corruption and dragging up various scandals, encapsulated the tone of a bitter election campaign in the deeply polarised nation.

Bolsonaro, 45, son of the far-right former president Jair Bolsonaro, secured a shock first-round lead over Lula earlier this month and polls predict a close battle for the runoff on 25 October.

The two men sparred over the economy, public security, healthcare, corruption and Brazil's ties to the United States.

"Brazil is going to retire you," fired off Bolsonaro, seeking to unnerve his adversary by pacing up and down behind him throughout the debate in view of the camera, shaking his head, smirking and scoffing while his adversary spoke.

Lula called Bolsonaro "a habitual liar, a thug," and said he did not want to stand close to a "militiaman," alluding to his alleged ties to Rio de Janeiro's paramilitary groups.

Bolsonaro, who made several jabs about Lula's time jailed for corruption on charges that were later annulled, retorted that it was better he stay at a distance "because my wallet could be stolen."

Lula, who at almost 81 is seeking a fourth term in power, battled to control his irritation at times and repeatedly asked viewers to "look it up on Google" to prove his adversary was lying.

While Bolsonaro has led in polls since the first round, his edge slightly narrowed in two polls this week which showed him with 51% of voting intentions compared to 49% for Lula.

Bolsonaro hammered away at the primary concerns of Brazilians, such as the cost of living and insecurity, and accused Lula of "reckless spending to get re-elected" due to various social measures he has announced while campaigning.

Lula countered by asking Bolsonaro about his plans to emulate the drastic "chainsaw" cuts to public spending implemented by Argentina's libertarian President Javier Milei, which led to higher unemployment and cuts to benefits.

Lula questioned the right winger's his ties to US President Donald Trump, an ally of the Bolsonaro family, and plans to "hand over" Brazil's rare earth minerals.

"It is striking how the Bolsonaro family is subservient to American interests," said Lula.

Former president Jair Bolsonaro came up several times, including when his son asked Lula why so many prisoners had supported him in the election.

Lula said he wanted all of their votes, including that of Jair Bolsonaro, who is currently serving a 27-year sentence under house arrest for a botched coup bid after his failed 2022 re-election bid.

Corruption is a top concern for many Brazilian voters ahead of the weekend runoff vote but experts say its greatest impact may not be on which candidates voters choose, but on whether they decide to sit out of the election altogether.

In the minds of many Brazilians, both candidates are associated with graft and for those who strongly support either candidate, such alleged ties will do little to curb their enthusiasm.

But experts say that a generalised perception of corruption can increase abstention and anti-incumbency sentiment, potentially benefiting Bolsonaro, who received 2.2 million more votes than Lula in the first round.

During the first round, many lined up to cast their votes wearing either red hats and T-shirts — signalling support for Lula’s party — or the yellow and green of the Brazilian flag adopted by Bolsonaro’s movement, reflecting the polarisation gripping the country.

Supporters of Bolsonaro and Lula are highly distrustful of the other side and view their opponents as a threat. Such voters are less likely to give weight to allegations of corruption, experts say.

“We have a great ability to filter information that goes against our beliefs,” said Nara Pavão, a political science professor at the Federal University of Pernambuco. “People don’t want to discover the truth about their candidates, they want to beat the opponent.”

Camila Tobji de Aquino, a 19-year-old student of pedagogy and psychology, thinks that corruption is a cross-partisan issue and a significant problem in Brazil.

“If I were to vote only for a candidate not tied to any corruption, I would vote blank,” she said. “But that’s not the choice Brazil is facing. For me it’s a question of being able to vote in the next election.”

As many as 87% of UAE residents say the government is well prepared for disasters while only 14% of Lebanese citizens believe the same, and more than half of Iraqi and Lebanese respondents say their governments do not care about them at all, latest study shows.
Disaster resilience in the Middle East and North Africa shows a sharp divide between Gulf states and the Levant, with Lebanon, Iraq, and Palestine falling well below the global average, according to the latest worldwide risk poll published this month.

The poll, conducted by Gallup for Lloyd's Register Foundation through more than 143,000 interviews in 140 countries and territories in 2025, assesses how well individuals, households, and communities can withstand major shocks and recover.

Kuwait ranked first among countries covered in the regional data with a resilience score of 74 points, followed by Bahrain at 72 and Oman and the United Arab Emirates at 70 each, against a global average score of 57.

Confidence in government preparedness was particularly high in the Gulf. In the UAE, 87% of respondents said the government was well prepared for disasters, the second-highest proportion among the 133 countries included in the resilience index. In Bahrain the figure was 82%.

In contrast, Lebanon scored 48 points on the index, Iraq 49 and Palestine 52. In Lebanon, only 14% of respondents said the government was well prepared for disasters, and just 39% of those affected by a disaster said they received an advance warning, against a global figure of 80%.

Some 59% of Iraqis and 56% of Lebanese said their governments do not care about them at all, compared with a global average of 29%.

More than half of households in Iraq, Jordan and Palestine said they would be unable to cover basic needs for a month if they lost their income.

Lebanon recorded some of the starkest individual risk perceptions in the survey: 71% of respondents expressed concern that drinking water could cause serious harm to their health, placing the country fifth globally on that measure, while 90% said they were worried about violent crime, ranking it sixth worldwide.

North Africa recorded the largest regional improvement, posting a five-point rise on the resilience index since 2023 — the biggest increase of any region in the study.

The report links gains in Algeria and Egypt to wider internet access, improved household preparedness and greater financial capacity to absorb crises.

Despite the region's high temperatures, Oman, Saudi Arabia, Bahrain and the UAE were among the countries least concerned about heatwaves and drought, the report notes. In Israel, the share of the population affected by a disaster during the period covered was under 1%.

Globally, around 1.1 billion adults experienced a disaster in the five years preceding the poll, and nearly 220 million received no advance warning.

The gap between income groups was pronounced: roughly a third of adults in low-income countries reported being affected by a disaster in the previous five years, compared with 16% in high-income states.

Only 64% of those affected in low-income countries received prior warnings, against 84% in high-income nations, and fewer than half of those warned in low-income and lower-middle-income countries took preventive action, compared with 71% in wealthier states.

The number of warning channels also affected behaviour: 37% of those alerted through a single channel took preventive action, rising to 51% for those reached through two and 64% for three, such as text messages, television and social media.

Of the roughly 220 million people worldwide who experienced a disaster without advance warning, 77% owned a mobile phone, representing more than 170 million individuals.

Replying to Euronews' questions in Uzbekistan's historic city of Samarkand, the US secretary of state outlined plans for new commercial agreements and cooperation on critical minerals.
US companies were preparing to announce new agreements in Uzbekistan as Washington seeks to expand its economic presence in Central Asia and diversify critical mineral supplies, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio told Euronews on Monday.

Speaking to reporters during a visit to Samarkand, Rubio said the deals could mark the start of broader US engagement with a region he believes American policymakers have paid too little attention to.

Asked by Euronews what people in Uzbekistan could expect from the visit and the accompanying business delegation, Rubio said, "I think we'll have some concrete deals to announce."

He said US companies were keen to do business in Uzbekistan and that announcements would follow the forum's meetings. "This is not the end. This is the beginning," he added.

Rubio said nearly 300 American businesses were represented in Samarkand, where they were discussing agreements potentially worth billions of dollars.

The US State Department had previously said more than 200 US business leaders were accompanying his trip. No final list of agreements or values had been announced at the time of his remarks.

In response to a second question from Euronews, Rubio identified critical minerals as an area for potential cooperation with Uzbekistan.

"It's not smart just for the whole world to depend on one place for 100% of anything," he said, stressing that he was not singling out any particular country.

He argued that industries and technologies likely to shape the 21st century would increasingly rely on critical minerals, making supply-chain diversification an international priority rather than solely a US concern.

He said that for resource-rich countries, developing mineral deposits and processing capacity responsibly could also serve their own economic interests.

"We're obviously going to explore that and I think we have a chance to do that here," Rubio told Euronews.

The subject was also discussed at the C5+1 summit US President Donald Trump hosted in Washington in November 2025. Uzbekistan and its neighbours have been seeking additional investment and technology to develop their mineral resources, while the US and Europe are looking for alternatives to highly concentrated supply chains.

Asked separately about China's growing economic influence in Central Asia, Rubio described US involvement as competition, not an attempt to make regional governments choose sides.

"This is not about coming here to say, stop talking to somebody else or stop working with some other country," he said, describing Uzbekistan as open to economic relationships with different partners and praising its economic growth.

Rubio began Monday's programme with a morning tour of Registan Square, Samarkand's historic architectural landmark, alongside Saida Mirziyoyeva, Head of Uzbekistan's Presidential Administration. He called the city a centuries-old crossroads of trade and commerce.

His official schedule also includes talks with Uzbek Foreign Minister Bakhtiyor Saidov and a document-signing ceremony, followed by a meeting with Kazakhstan's Foreign Minister Mukhtar Tileuberdi and the C5+1 ministerial meeting involving Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Turkmenistan.

On Monday, Rubio is also scheduled to meet President Shavkat Mirziyoyev and attend events involving the Uzbekistan–US Business and Investment Council. The council, co-chaired by Saida Mirziyoyeva and US Special Envoy Sergio Gor, was established to promote commercial partnerships and investment.

The visit follows last year's White House C5+1 summit, where more than $25bn (approximately €22bn) in commercial agreements and commitments were announced. Those commitments covered several Central Asian countries and were at different stages of implementation.

The visit also gave Rubio an opportunity to address growing tensions between Washington and Kyiv over Russia's war in Ukraine.

Asked about Ukrainian claims that the US had briefly stopped sharing intelligence used to warn of Russian missile attacks, Rubio denied any interruption.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy had said on Sunday that intelligence sharing had resumed after a three-day suspension. The two accounts remain at odds.

Rubio urged both Russia and Ukraine to focus on ending the war that began with Russia's all-out invasion, now in its fifth year. "What he should be focused on and what we want to be focused on is bringing an end to this war," he said.

Trump had criticised Zelenskyy a day earlier, accusing him of lacking the will to end the conflict and blaming Ukrainian attacks on Russian oil refineries for disruption to global diesel markets.

Alberta has the world's fourth-largest proven oil reserves and separatist momentum has been driven by anger over Ottawa's management of the sector.
The province of Alberta will decide on Monday if it wants to pursue a binding referendum on secession from Canada, with Prime Minister Mark Carney warning a separatist win would bring dangerous uncertainty during a trade war with the United States.

Polls indicate less than a quarter of people in the western Canadian province support independence.

But attention has focused on turnout in the days before the vote and whether a motivated separatist camp could defy the polls, helped by complacent federalists.

"Don't let anyone tell you this is already decided. Turnout will determine the outcome," Carney said at a rally on Thursday.

Carney, who grew up in Alberta, has called the referendum "a dangerous bluff" as US President Donald Trump imposes punishing tariffs and levels annexation threats aimed at breaking Canadian resolve, Carney said.

Alberta has the world's fourth-largest proven oil reserves and separatist momentum has been driven by anger over Ottawa's management of the sector.

Many Albertans loathed Carney's predecessor, Justin Trudeau, who they accused of trying to suppress oil production to advance his pro-climate agenda.

"If you want me to pinpoint who is responsible for the separatist sentiment, I think it's Justin Trudeau," Alberta Premier Danielle Smith told the Canadaland podcast this month.

At a separatist rally in Edmonton on Saturday, 61-year-old Sandy Martel said people connected to the oil industry feel "robbed, cheated (and) mistreated."

The vote comes days after a fire at a military base in the province left at least four soldiers dead and wounded dozens, prompting a national outpouring of condolences.

Alberta’s conservative premier says she wants Alberta to remain in Canada but called the referendum to settle the divisive issue.

If a majority votes to separate, she has pledged to move forward with a binding referendum.

Analysts have compared Smith's tactics to those of former UK prime minister David Cameron, who had hoped to resolve the debate over Britain's European Union membership with a vote.

Carney led the Bank of England through the turmoil that surrounded Brexit and issued a warning to Albertans about that experience on Thursday.

"For the next few years, while that was being determined, there was huge uncertainty, investments stopped, the economy started to underperform," he added, cautioning over unintended economic consequences.

Monday's referendum features 10 questions, including several related to immigration, but focus is on question 10, which asks: "Should Alberta remain a province of Canada?" or should the province take the legal steps required for a definitive independence vote.

Alberta's election authority says ballots for question 10 will be counted first, with results expected in the early hours of Tuesday.

Carney has urged "a decisive result," but experts are concerned about a contested outcome no matter the numbers.

"A very likely result of this referendum is going to be a community that sees this election as stolen or at least unfair," said Aengus Bridgman, director of the Media Ecosystem Observatory at McGill University.

Close Trump allies have also inserted themselves into the discussion, including Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who in January described Alberta as a "natural partner" for the United States.

After a separatist party won Quebec's provincial election on 5 October, tech billionaire Elon Musk posted on X that "they would be better off" if they seceded.

Asked about alleged foreign interference, Carney said on Thursday that foreign governments, including the US, should not "get themselves involved in our elections."

The official growth figure from the July-September quarter beat expectations and picked up from 4.3% in April-June, the slowest pace in more than three years.
China said Monday its economy expanded 4.5% in the July-September quarter from a year earlier, driven by strong exports, a record that moves the country closer to its annual growth target just as trading partners press harder to narrow their deficits with Beijing.

The official figure beat expectations and picked up from 4.3% in April-June, the slowest pace in more than three years.

Chinese leaders have set a 2026 target of 4.5%-5%, down from last year’s 5% expansion. Earlier this month, the International Monetary Fund (IMF) cut its forecast for China’s growth this year to 4.5%, the bottom of Beijing’s range.

Exports remain one of the few bright spots. After a 36% jump in exports last month and a $938 billion surplus for January-September, China is on course to top last year’s record $1.2 trillion trade surplus.

Higher US tariffs have dented bilateral trade, pushing Chinese exporters toward Southeast Asia, Latin America and Europe. High-tech goods, including semiconductors and hybrid and electric vehicles, are leading the growth.

In pursuing self-sufficiency to guard against Western export controls, China has built vast, heavily subsidised manufacturing capacity in sectors such as solar and EVs. But there have also been issues.

Output expanded quickly while consumption lagged, so much of it is sold abroad at competitive prices. Ecaterina Bigos, senior market strategist at BNP Paribas Asset Management, said this tech-driven, export-reliant model has produced limited job creation and income gains, while the risk of AI-driven job losses is growing.

As such, the headline official growth figure is becoming “an increasingly poor indicator of how the broader economy is performing,” according to Max Zenglein, Asia Pacific senior economist at The Conference Board.

Zenglein believes, despite the impressive number, it hides “widening disparities across industries, regions, and households", and that managing domestic imbalances may prove harder for Beijing than handling outside pressures.

Domestic demand has softened, weighed down by a years-long property slump that has eroded household wealth and pushed up savings.

China shipped about 919,000 passenger vehicles overseas in September, driven by strong foreign demand for plug-in hybrids and all-electric models, according to data from the China Association of Automobile Manufacturers.

For the first nine months of 2026, exports topped 7.1 million passenger cars, a jump of more than 70% from the same period a year earlier.

However, fear that cheaper Chinese imports threaten their own domestic industries and trade balance continues to remain among Beijing's partners.

The 27-nation European Union bloc runs a deficit with China of more than $1.1 billion (€981.2 million) a day, and Chinese exports to the EU rose 16% in the first nine months of the year.

Some member states want further curbs, even after an agreement this month to roughly halve Chinese hybrid vehicle imports over the next few years.

Analysts say China, however, can keep working with other countries on disputes while protecting its market access.

The hybrid deal, for instance, may let it keep selling more in the longer run while avoiding a broad tariff hike. Still, Lynn Song, chief economist for Greater China at ING, said trade imbalances “will be difficult to resolve quickly.”

Policymakers also argue that an undervalued yuan against the dollar and euro has fed the surplus, a claim China’s central bank has rejected.

Beijing has said it does not seek a large trade surplus. Last Friday, central bank governor Pan Gongsheng said deficit and surplus countries should cooperate and that China is committed to expanding domestic demand.

Trump's late backing comes as the two leaders’ strategies on Iran and Gaza have diverged, and he warns the US may not “do this work” if Israel changes leaders.
US President Donald Trump on Sunday endorsed embattled Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu in the final stretch of his reelection campaign.

The endorsement is a coveted boost given Trump’s popularity in Israel, but one he withheld for months as the vote approached and the two leaders’ strategies in the Middle East diverged.

In a post on social media, Trump said that Netanyahu “has been a great warrior prime minister and an even greater partner for peace in combatting the Islamic Republic of Iran.”

Israelis are set to vote on 27 October in the first national elections since Hamas attacked Israel on 7 October 2023.

“Thank you, President Trump, for the tremendous support and work together,” Netanyahu said in a Hebrew-language statement on X. “Together we will complete the mission!”

The lack of an endorsement had been a blow to the Likud party politician, who has billed his close ties to Trump as a selling point for staying in power.

Likud attached a 35-second video to the statement, showing clips of the two leaders together and Trump praising Netanyahu.

The absence of an endorsement was a setback for the Likud politician, who had billed his tight ties to the Republican president as a selling point for staying in power.

Trump has backed Netanyahu in past elections, and in November he wrote to Israeli President Isaac Herzog asking him to pardon Netanyahu on corruption charges.

In recent months, however, Trump’s remarks about the prime minister had been far more subdued, and he repeatedly demurred when asked whether he would support him.

Asked about it in a Time magazine interview published this month, Trump said, “I’m not involved in politics anymore,” even though he continues to campaign for and endorse US politicians.

Trump remains popular in Israel; however, many Israelis, including leading commentators, have voiced disappointment over the inconclusive outcome of the Iran war and over Trump’s ceasefire proposal, which fell short of many of the key goals the two countries had set when they jointly launched the war in February.

In his post on Sunday, Trump again threatened to “soon finish the job” against Iran “unless they change their ways rapidly." He added that he might not want to if Netanyahu loses.

“If Israel has the wrong leader, which would be anyone other than Bibi, America will have to do this work ourselves, and I am not inclined to do so,” Trump said.

He described Netanyahu as “strong, brave, and brilliant in the face of tremendous opposition, including that coming from even Israel itself.”

Trump and Netanyahu have long been close allies and appeared more aligned than ever after the joint US-Israeli strikes on Iran’s nuclear facilities in 2025. Over the course of the nearly eight-month war with Iran this year, however, they have clashed.

Trump criticised Israel’s decision to attack a key Iranian gas field in the first weeks of the war. In June he called Netanyahu "crazy", and he said he was “a little bit perturbed” that Israel’s fighting with Hezbollah militants in Lebanon was holding back peace talks with Iran.

On Gaza, Netanyahu in August rejected Trump’s latest plan to advance a stalled ceasefire. The plan called for Israel to withdraw from territory it controls in Gaza before Hamas has been disarmed, and Netanyahu pushed back on it.

Especially in his second term, Trump has sought to put his stamp of approval on candidates in foreign elections claiming credit when they win.

He endorsed Hungarian Prime Minister Viktor Orbán in his unsuccessful bid for a fifth term, as well as Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, Polish President Karol Nawrocki and several Latin American leaders.
