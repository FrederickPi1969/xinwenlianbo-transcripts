Nailympia London showcased fantasy nail art inspired by folklore, forests and steampunk, as global artists raced to create miniature worlds on nails.
… More
Fantasy nail artists transformed fingertips into tiny worlds at Nailympia in London, creating designs inspired by goddesses, Chinese folklore, magical forests and steampunk.

In the fantasy category, competitors had one hour to complete their creations, with judges assessing both the concept and the overall look. Some entries featured elaborate themes spread across all ten nails, turning each hand into a miniature scene.

Other categories focused on technical skills. Competitors worked on long stiletto nails, carefully filing and decorating them against the clock.

The event brought together artists from around the world, with judges encouraging them to develop their own style rather than simply follow previous winners.

Rospotrebnadzor, Russia's public health watchdog, said it saw "no epidemic risk" following the death of a 28-year-old employee of the Irkutsk Anti-Plague Research Institute, identified as Darya Shipilova, who died last week in the town of Shelekhov near Irkutsk.

The agency said Shipilova had been "vaccinated and had immunity to the dangerous infections she was working with," and that nearly 5,000 laboratory tests had detected no dangerous pathogens.

More than 90% of those who had contact with her had completed medical monitoring, it said.

The Kremlin dismissed reports of a second suspected plague case in Siberia, telling journalists that most media reporting on the situation was "false".

"The media are reporting a great deal right now, and the overwhelming majority of what the media are reporting around this situation is false information," Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said, urging citizens to rely solely on Rospotrebnadzor's official statements.

Russian authorities have maintained since the start of the week that Shipilova died of pneumonia and that her cause of death remains "undetermined".

US President Donald Trump had a call with his Russian counterpart Vladimir Putin scheduled for Wednesday to address the plague incident, and his administration sent Moscow a demarche requesting additional information.

"We have officially requested additional information from the Russian government. We are also in official communication with other countries around the world to share information," a US State Department spokesperson said on Wednesday.

Peskov said he had no information that the Kremlin had received any US official complaint.

WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus on Wednesday called on Moscow to clarify the situation over media reports of a second staff member with pneumonia of unclear origin.

"Timely, complete and transparent information-sharing in line with the International Health Regulations is critical," Tedros said.

The WHO said the day before that the possible case posed a "very low" risk beyond Russia's borders, citing the country's reduced contact with the rest of the world due to its ongoing all-out war in Ukraine and resulting sanctions.

It noted that the institute studied "dangerous pathogens" and that plague occurs naturally among wild animals in the Irkutsk region.

Pneumonic plague, the most dangerous form of the disease, affects the lungs and can spread person to person through airborne droplets, with an incubation period as short as 24 hours.

It can be fatal without appropriate care, but the chances of recovery are high if identified and treated promptly. Symptoms include shortness of breath, coughing and blood-streaked sputum.

The more common bubonic plague is caused by the bite of an infected flea and can produce purulent ulcers.

Homes cost more per square metre in Madrid than in several major Latin American cities, but lower wages in those cities can make renting even less affordable.
For the price of one square metre of housing in Madrid, you can buy almost two in central Mexico City and nearly three in Buenos Aires, Bogotá or São Paulo. But higher property prices do not necessarily mean a heavier burden on local wages.

In the Madrid region, renting an 80-square-metre flat at December 2025 asking prices would have absorbed 71% of the average gross salary advertised on InfoJobs that year, according to a study by Fotocasa and InfoJobs.

And in parts of the Santiago metropolitan area, anyone on the minimum wage can end up spending between 78% and 96% of their take-home pay on the rent for a small flat.

These measures illustrate the pressure on incomes, although differences in flat sizes, geographical coverage and wage definitions mean they are not directly comparable.

A commonly used affordability benchmark is to spend no more than 30% of income on rent. Taking Spain's modal salary (the most frequently recorded salary, rather than the average), which was €16,520 in 2024, and dividing it by 12 gives approximately €1,377 a month before tax. Applying that benchmark leaves about €413 a month for rent.

At that price, searches on housing portals found nothing available in Madrid. The closest option was €500 for a flat of barely 30 square metres.

The exercise produces virtually the same result in Barcelona. For €500, you can find somewhere noticeably larger than in the national capital, but the search returned just three homes.

Existing homes in Madrid were advertised at an average of €6,471 per square metre in August, according to property website Idealista. This is 2.2% more than a year earlier and below the April peak of €6,619. The slowdown has not reached the cheapest districts: in Villaverde, the borough with the most affordable homes per square metre, prices have jumped 25% in a year.

Rents, meanwhile, show no sign of hitting a ceiling. In September, they set a record of €23.4 per square metre a month, 4.8% more than a year ago. A study by InfoJobs and Fotocasa, comparing advertised salaries with asking rents for an 80-square-metre home, put the cost at 71% of gross pay in the Madrid region in 2025, compared with a national average of 50%.

The Madrid regional government has not designated any “stressed” housing areas under Spain's Housing Act, which allows rent limits in those areas. Other regions, including Catalonia, the Basque Country and Navarre, have used the mechanism.

The national government has also sought to intervene. After parliament rejected two housing decrees on 2 October, the cabinet approved replacement measures on 6 October, including rental contract extensions, limits on annual rent increases and protection against evictions for vulnerable households. The new decrees still require parliamentary ratification.

Madrid is not the most expensive provincial capital for buying a home, although it is for renting. According to Idealista, in August San Sebastián was more expensive: €6,680 per square metre versus Madrid's €6,471. Behind them came Palma (€5,835) and Barcelona (€5,440).

For rentals, Madrid led the way with €23.4 per square metre in September, ahead of Barcelona (€20.1) and Palma (€19.6), while the national average was €15.1.

At the same time, Madrid’s asking house prices rose 2.2% in the year to August, the smallest increase among the cities compared here. Seville (11.5%) and Bilbao (10.6%) recorded the largest increases.

According to the Catalan Land Institute (Incasòl), the average rent on new contracts was €1,137 a month in the first quarter of 2026, 4.7% less than two years earlier.

The latest asking-rent figures also show a contrast: rents in Barcelona fell 0.8% in the year to September, while in Madrid they rose 4.8%.

The bill passed 174 to six despite cabinet opposition, carrying penalties of up to two years in prison, fines and flogging for Iranians who speak to outlets designated as hostile by the Intelligence Ministry. The bill has not yet become law and faces Guardian Council review.
Iran's parliament voted 174 to six on Tuesday to pass an article banning Iranian nationals from giving interviews to or participating in discussions with media outlets designated as "hostile" by the Intelligence Ministry, or with outlets owned, managed or funded by operatives connected to countries classified as adversaries of the Islamic Republic.

Violations of Article 22 of the bill on "countering the penetration of intelligence services and foreign governments or entities" carry sixth-degree penalties under the Islamic Penal Code, including imprisonment of more than six months up to two years, fines, 31 to 74 lashes, deprivation of social rights and other restrictions.

The bill classifies foreign governments into two tiers: a "level 1 government" is a state formally designated as hostile under Iranian law or by decision of the Supreme National Security Council.

"Level 2" is defined as a state that has planned or carried out actions aimed at damaging Tehran's political, defence, security, economic, social or cultural interests.

The list of level two governments is to be drawn up by the Intelligence Ministry and the IRGC intelligence organisation, then approved by the Supreme National Security Council.

A proposal by Tehran MP Alireza Salimi to exempt government officials from the scope of Article 22 was also approved at the same sitting.

President Masoud Pezeshkian's cabinet had formally opposed the bill. At its 22 August meeting, the cabinet said the legislation had "serious flaws," including restrictions on scientific activity, the acceleration of elite emigration and negative consequences at national and international level.

First Vice-President Mohammad Reza Aref had warned of consequences for science and technology. Presidential legal deputy Majid Ansari said "criminalising broad and general categories in this bill could harm citizens' rights."

Parliament also passed Article 20 of the same bill, under which anyone who accesses unpublished official information by virtue of their job may not share it with a foreign agent without prior authorisation. The penalty is four to 10 years in prison and a permanent bar from the post through which the information was accessed.

The legislation follows months of hardline rhetoric from Iranian officials against Persian-language broadcasters outside Iran.

On 26 August, armed forces spokesman Abolfazl Shekarchi accused BBC Persian, Iran International and Radio Farda of links to US and Israeli intelligence organisations and said their staff could be placed in the Islamic Republic's "military targets bank".

Following the parliamentary vote, the bill still needs further approval to become law. The Majlis must complete its examination of remaining articles before sending the text to the Guardian Council, which reviews legislation for compatibility with Sharia and the constitution.

If the Guardian Council raises objections and the Majlis insists on its position, the Expediency Council makes a final ruling.

The parliament that passed the article emerged from elections in which turnout was reportedly at around 41%, the lowest since the 1979 Islamic Revolution.

Turnout in Tehran was around 24%. The leading candidate in the capital won some 598,000 votes, compared with more than 1.6 million for the leading Tehran candidate in the 2016 elections.

A skull found nearly a century ago has helped Egyptian and European researchers revise part of the crocodile family tree, shedding light on the Mediterranean's prehistoric wildlife and revealing an unexpected connection to the Great Sphinx.
Scientists have identified a new crocodile genus from a 45-million-year-old Egyptian fossil after using CT scans to re-examine a skull recovered nearly a century ago.

The fossil comes from the Mokattam Formation, the same limestone formation from which the Great Sphinx was carved. Like the monument, the skull is missing the tip of its snout.

Researchers from Egypt, the UK, Sweden and Germany have named the animal Herugavialis cairensis. The findings were published in Royal Society Open Science.

The animal was first described in 1927 as Tomistoma cairense, placing it in the same genus as the living false gharial, which has a long, narrow snout.

But the new examination revealed differences in the skull's structure and the animal's evolutionary relationships. Among its distinctive features were raised, projecting eye sockets.

Those differences prompted the researchers to place the species in a separate genus, changing a classification that had stood for almost a century.

Paul Burke of the Swedish Museum of Natural History led the study, co-led by Philip Mannion of University College London.

Mannion said in a statement that the resemblance between the fossil's missing snout tip and the Sphinx's absent nose helped inspire the name, which honours the Egyptian god Heru.

About 45 million years ago, much of the region lay beneath shallow seas. Herugavialis lived alongside at least two other species of long-snouted crocodiles.

The animal belonged to a group whose only surviving members are the Indian gharial and false gharial. Fossils show that their relatives once had a far wider distribution, with more than 40 species recognised.

The researchers suggest that cooling and falling sea levels reduced suitable habitats around the Mediterranean, while later warming may have allowed their diversity to recover.

Studying more fossils could help explain how these ancient crocodiles spread around the Mediterranean and why they later disappeared from the region.

Cemil Tugay, who won İzmir for the CHP with a 314,000-vote margin in 2024, received his AK Party badge from Erdoğan in Ankara on Wednesday, ending 27 years of centre-left rule in Turkey's third-largest city.
İzmir Mayor Cemil Tugay joined President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan's AK Party on Wednesday, shocking the Turkish left and handing the ruling party a symbolic prize it has failed to win in elections three times in a row.

Erdoğan presented Tugay — who ran for mayor as centre-left CHP party candidate two years ago — with an AK Party badge at the party's Ankara headquarters on Wednesday morning and welcomed him in a speech to MPs at the opening of the legislative year.

"İzmir is different today. Very enthusiastic," Erdoğan said. "Good things await İzmir," Tugay told reporters.

Tugay reversed a series of public pledges that he would never join the ruling party, which he previously described as "not even a one-in-a-million chance."

The switch makes Tugay the first right-wing mayor of Turkey's third-largest city in 27 years. İzmir has been governed by centre-left or left-wing mayors since 1999.

In the March 2024 local elections, Tugay won 48.97% of the vote against AK Party candidate Hamza Dağ's 37.06%, a 314,000-vote margin.

In August 2025, Tugay told a television programme "there is not even a one-in-a-million chance" of him joining the AK Party, adding that a mayor elected from the CHP had no right to switch parties.

"I find that very shameful. I am not that kind of person," he said. In January, he told the outlet T24 that his mother said she'd disown him and his wife would divorce him if he were to join the AK Party.

Tugay left the CHP in June following a court ruling that reinstated Kemal Kılıçdaroğlu as party leader, and served as an independent for three months.

He was expected by many observers to join the YENİ or New Party founded by former CHP leader Özgür Özel, but did not.

In August, Tugay said joining any party was "out of the question". "I will remain independent, I have said many times that I will stay independent, but in truth I am upset that this keeps being talked about every day," he explained.

Yet on 30 September he met Erdoğan at the presidential complex and two days later he resigned from both the Atatürkist Thought Association and the Social Democracy Association.

The day before the announcement, the AK Party posted a video showing a rubbish-strewn street being covered with flowers as a patriotic Turkish song about İzmir from the 1920s played, with the date "7 October 2026" appearing at the end without specifically naming Tugay.

New Party leader Özgür Özel said on Tuesday that Tugay had faced "threats, blackmail and pressure" and that the AK Party had "never won İzmir at the polls and never will."

"This is not something you can achieve with grab-and-run tactics," Özel said, addressing Erdoğan.

New Party spokesman Zeynel Emre said party switches could not be assessed independently of judicial proceedings against opposition mayors.

The motion, filed by MPs aligned with the Paydari Front, accuses Araghchi of meeting US envoy Steve Witkoff at the UN General Assembly without authorisation, a claim directly opposed by Supreme National Security Council chief Mohsen Rezaei, who said the talks had prior approval.
A group of hardline Iranian MPs has filed a motion to impeach Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi over his talks with US special envoy Steve Witkoff on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly last month, exposing divisions within the Islamic Republic over how and whether to pursue negotiations with Washington to end the war.

Member of parliament's presiding board Mohammad Rashidi confirmed on Tuesday that the request had been registered in the parliamentary system but had not yet been referred to the board or placed on the agenda.

The MPs behind the motion, led by Amirhossein Sabeti of the hardline Paydari Front, argue that Araghchi's meeting with Witkoff on 22 September was conducted without authorisation from Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei or coordination with the Supreme National Security Council.

The talks were held with Qatar acting as an intermediary and focused on conditions for reopening the Strait of Hormuz, a key stumbling block in negotiations between Tehran and Washington since the war began in late February.

Supreme National Security Council's secretary Mohsen Rezaei directly contradicted that claim.

"It was agreed that Mr Araghchi, during his trip to New York, would convey Iran's conditions to the intermediaries," Rezaei said, indicating the talks had the prior approval of Iran's higher authorities.

Araghchi set out Tehran's conditions in the New York talks, including the immediate lifting of the US naval blockade, the immediate transfer of frozen Iranian assets and an end to the war on all fronts. US President Donald Trump rejected the proposal.

A second signatory, MP Hosseinali Hajideligani, accused Araghchi of "repeatedly, through targeted interviews, helping to lower oil prices for the United States."

Sabeti listed "crossing red lines" during negotiations, "serious miscalculations" in political analysis and mishandling of the US file as the main grounds for impeachment.

Araghchi's name has appeared on impeachment lists drawn up by hardline MPs multiple times over the past two years, and Hajideligani said in August that they were gathering documentation for a formal motion.

Alireza Salimi, himself a signatory of the letter and a member of the presiding board, told Mehr news agency that filing an impeachment motion does not mean the speaker supports it or that it will reach the floor.

The motion was filed while parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, one of Iran's main negotiators with the US side, holds the chair.

Some MPs oppose the move. Ruhollah Lak Aliabadi said "impeachment proceedings, especially impeaching the foreign minister in this wartime situation, are definitely not in the country's interest" and could send "a negative signal and a sign of weakness to the enemy."

Aliabadi said a recent parliamentary meeting with Araghchi lasted four and a half hours, during which the IRGC commander called Araghchi by phone at least three times, which he cited as evidence of coordination between diplomacy and the military.

Conservative political figure Mansour Haghighatpour also pushed back. "You do not change saddles in wartime," Haghighatpour said.

Araghchi's position appears secure for now. After he returned to Tehran following the New York trip — during which social media speculation circulated that he had sought asylum abroad — the foreign ministry formally described him as a member of Iran's six-person decision-making team, a signal that he retained the regime's confidence.

Moscow proposed wet-leasing Vietnamese aircraft, crews and maintenance, but any transfer would involve Airbus or Boeing jets, exposing Vietnamese carriers to secondary sanctions. Nearly a fifth of Russia's largest airlines' planes are already grounded, double the normal downtime rate.
Russia has asked Vietnam to lease its passenger jets complete with crews and maintenance, as the fleet it seized from Western lessors in 2022 deteriorates and nearly a fifth of the country's largest airlines' planes sit grounded.

Transport Minister Andrei Nikitin said Moscow had proposed that Vietnamese carriers transfer aircraft to Russian airlines under wet lease — an arrangement that covers jets, crew, maintenance and insurance — and that Hanoi was studying the proposal.

Vietnam's main carrier exclusively operates Airbus and Boeing aircraft, meaning any transfer to Russia would involve Western-built planes. That creates the same secondary sanctions risk that led Ethiopia to decline a similar proposal, as companies could face US penalties for facilitating the operation of sanctioned aircraft types on behalf of a Russian carrier.

Approaches to other countries in Asia, Africa and the Middle East have so far produced nothing.

Russia's request to Vietnam shows how far Russia's seized and cannibalised fleet of passenger jets — built on legally contested re-registrations and parts that do not meet international airworthiness standards — has deteriorated.

When sanctions were imposed after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, Moscow passed a law allowing airlines to re-register more than 400 foreign-leased Western aircraft domestically rather than return them to their owners.

Under international aviation law, dual registration of a single aircraft is prohibited, meaning Russia's re-registrations are illegal, but the International Civil Aviation Organisation (ICAO) has no enforcement mechanism to compel their return.

Lessors have filed insurance claims totalling around $8 billion (€7.1bn) to recover their losses, with many cases still ongoing.

Before the full-scale invasion of Ukraine, roughly 850 of Russia's 900 commercial aircraft were US and European-made Boeing or Airbus planes, of which around 85% were leased from foreign companies.

With Western spare parts and technical services now sanctioned, airlines have been cannibalising the seized fleet for components, making legal matters worse.

Under international airworthiness standards, every component must have a certified and traceable maintenance history.

Parts stripped from aircraft cut off from Western-certified maintenance chains cannot legally be used on internationally operated aircraft.

Russia's aviation regulator Rosaviatsia has been issuing its own certificates for such parts, which are not recognised by the EU or US aviation authorities.

Of 673 aircraft operated by Russia's 11 largest airlines, 130 or nearly one-fifth are not in service, according to Russian industry data. The normal downtime rate is around 10%.

Airlines outside the Aeroflot group are in worse shape: 93 of their 322 planes, close to a third, are idle.

Fuel supply is a separate problem. Restrictions on refuelling have been introduced at at least 26 major airports, according to the outlet Verstka.

A group representing the bereaved families organised tributes, including a silence at 6:29am - the minute the attack began - and a ceremony that will follow tonight in Tel Aviv.
Israelis gathered in a moment of silence on Wednesday to mark three years since the Hamas-led attack of 7 October 2023, which a UN inquiry confirmed killed 1,200 people and saw 252 taken hostage.

In southern Israel, families mourned at road junctions, bomb shelters and the site of the Nova music festival, all places overrun by militants in the deadliest attack against Israel.

It is the first anniversary on which all the hostages that survived have returned home, bringing a new phase of mourning.

Among those marking the day was Elkana Bohbot, 37, who was abducted from the Nova festival and is spending the anniversary in Israel for the first time. He was freed last year after 738 days in captivity, most of it in a tunnel 50 metres underground. He said his daughter's birth in August this year was a message to his captors that "we [Israelis] were here, and we will always be here forever".

At the site of the Nova music festival, where Hamas militants in trucks and paragliders opened fire around dawn on October 7, family members bowed their heads in silence, although AFP reported at least one put on techno music in tribute to the fallen.

European Commission president Ursula von der Leyen paid her respects to the victims and their families in a post on X.

"The EU remains ready to help turn today's fragile ceasefire into a durable peace, based on a two-state solution."

The ceasefire von der Leyen referred to took effect last October, two years into the war Israel launched against Hamas in response to the attack.

Up to 75,000 Palestinians have been killed in the retaliatory campaign, according to the UN.

The UN said this summer that about 1.7 million Palestinians, roughly 80% of the population, were living in tents and temporary accommodation while Israeli maps show more than half of the Gaza strip is now controlled by its troops.

On Wednesday, AFP reporters in Israel heard loud bangs from across the border in Gaza during the commemorations as Israel carried out new strikes, despite the ceasefire agreement.

Gaza officials said at least two people were killed in overnight strikes in the region, most of which has been reduced to rubble.

Wednesday's gatherings were separate from the government's official memorial services, held on 5 October to fall closer to the anniversary in the Hebrew calendar.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu vowed there would be "no forgiveness for the murderers" and promised never to allow Hamas to rule in Gaza.

More than 200 former hostages and relatives wrote to Netanyahu last year demanding an inquiry into the 7 October attacks or his resignation but Israel's leader opted to publish redacted government documents instead of launching an investigation and described the attacks as "a serious intelligence failure".

Christa Pike remains shackled to her hospital bed with burned and blistered arms, while Tennessee has suspended executions and its prison chief has resigned.
A US death row inmate who survived an attempt to execute her by lethal injection is conscious and speaking, her lawyers have said, as they go to court to find out what went wrong.

Christa Pike, 50, was given two doses of the drug pentobarbital at a Tennessee prison on 30 September. She is believed to be the only person to survive after lethal injection drugs were administered.

"Against all odds, Christa Pike is conscious, speaking, and continues to receive critical medical care," her attorneys said on Tuesday.

"Despite the severe damage she endured to her arms, she is still handcuffed and shackled to the bed," they said. "Her prognosis remains unclear but, at a minimum, we expect a long recovery."

Pike was sentenced to death for the 1995 killing of 19-year-old Colleen Slemmer, committed when she was 18. She would have been the first woman executed by Tennessee in more than 200 years.

Her lawyers are due in court in Nashville on Wednesday, seeking an order requiring the prison system to preserve evidence including drug lot numbers, needles and execution logs.

The Tennessee attorney general's office said the order was unnecessary because the prison system is "obliged to maintain this evidence in other cases and must assure it is preserved".

Experts have focused on whether a problem with the IV line caused the drug to pool in her tissue instead of entering her bloodstream. A court filing said both Pike's arms were "swollen, burned, and blistered" when she arrived at hospital.

Governor Bill Lee called what happened "deeply disturbing" and ordered a "comprehensive, third-party review". He has suspended executions in the state pending the investigation, and the prison chief has resigned.

Pike's lawyers have again called on Lee to commute her sentence to life imprisonment without the possibility of parole.

The Government's delay in bringing the Verifactu e-invoicing system into force gives firms and the self-employed more time, but fuels uncertainty over its rollout.
The Spanish government has postponed until 2027 (source in Spanish), and later to 2028, the entry into force of Verifactu, the electronic invoicing system designed to strengthen tax control over companies and self-employed workers. The move pushes back by one year the original timetable, which had set its implementation for 2026 (source in Spanish).

Under the new calendar, companies subject to Corporation Tax will have to adapt from 2028, while the self-employed and SMEs will have until 1 July of that same year.

The system requires invoices to be issued using certified software that guarantees the authenticity, integrity and traceability of records, as part of the fight against tax fraud.

The postponement gives more time to adapt IT programmes and internal processes, reducing the immediate pressure on millions of small businesses.

However, different voices in the sector point out that the delay has also created confusion because of the lack of clarity about technical requirements and the final implementation deadlines.

These doubts are compounded by warnings from organisations representing the self-employed and from the business community itself. The National Federation of Associations of Self-Employed Workers (ATA) had for weeks been calling for an extension (source in Spanish), citing adaptation difficulties and a lack of clear information.

Even so, the union representing technicians at the Ministry of Finance, Gestha (source in Spanish), believes the extension is a "key tool" that could be "useful" for millions of taxpayers.

Despite the extension, Verifactu remains a structural change in Spain's tax system that will affect most of the business sector. The rules, promoted by the Tax Agency, form part of measures to digitise the economy and strengthen fiscal transparency, with possible sanctions for non-compliance once they come into force.

According to Eurostat data, only 58% of small and medium-sized enterprises in the EU have reached a basic level of digital services, well below the 90% target set for 2030. The gap is even wider compared with large companies, where the level of digitalisation reaches 91%, highlighting the specific difficulties SMEs face in adapting to technological and regulatory change.

In the case of Spain, the figures show progress but also limits to business digitalisation. According to a survey (source in Spanish) by the National Statistics Institute (INE), 44.3% of companies use cloud computing services and 21.1% use artificial intelligence, while 84.5% have a website.

However, the level of adoption of more advanced technologies remains uneven, especially among small companies.

Moreover, various official indicators place Spanish SMEs in an intermediate position within the EU: around 60% reach a basic level of digitalisation, in line with the European average, but still far from the goals set by Brussels for this decade.

The Caspian Sea has been shrinking for decades. Now, a new island has emerged

Scientists warn that parts of the northern Caspian could face an Aral Sea-like scenario, while climate change and human water use are adding pressure on the region.
The Caspian Sea is the largest inland body of water on Earth, but for years it has been shrinking.

The northern and north-eastern parts along Kazakhstan's coast form the shallowest sector of the Caspian Sea, typically ranging between just three and six metres. But as water levels continue to fall, following decades of climate change and human activity, environmentalists are discovering clusters of under-water islands.

Volunteer and environmental activist Daniyar Akimzhanov was part of the group that reported the discovery. He says that the newly emerged island is relatively small, but it is far from an isolated case.

In fact, over the past four to five years, Akimzhanov says he has documented several new islands in this part of the Caspian Sea.

“The one in the photo I sent you is the smallest. There are much bigger new islands,” he explains.

Some stretch around one kilometre in length and 200 to 300 metres in width and wildlife has already begun reaching the newly exposed land.

Akimzhanov says he personally spotted a wild boar on one of the islands, while wolves also make their way there across the ice. The newly emerged islands usually do not get names, he says.

The Kazakh Research Institute of the Caspian Sea, which began work in September 2025 after being established on the instructions of President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev, says more areas of land are likely to emerge. However, not all of them will become permanent islands.

“The future of each newly exposed area depends on the seabed topography, waves, wind-driven changes in water levels and the subsequent dynamics of the Caspian Sea level,” the institute says.

The emergence of new islands is one of the most visible signs of a much broader transformation: the Caspian Sea is retreating.

The Caspian Sea is the world's largest landlocked body of water. It is bordered by five countries: Kazakhstan, Russia, Azerbaijan, Iran and Turkmenistan. More than 80 per cent of its river inflow comes from Russia’s Volga River.

Dr. Sifan Koriche, a hydroclimate scientist at the University of Alabama who has published several studies on the Caspian Sea, explains its water balance in simple terms.

“It's like a simple water balance. The incoming water is from river inflow and precipitation, while the outgoing water is only through evaporation,” he says.

According to instrumental observations, the Caspian Sea level in the Kazakh sector fell from -26.82 metres in 1996 to -29.27 metres in 2025. This represents a decline of 2.45 metres over the past three decades, or an average of around 8–9 centimetres per year.

The decline has continued. According to the Kazakh Research Institute of the Caspian Sea, the average sea level at the Kulaly Island monitoring station stood at -29.42 metres as of July 2026, the lowest level recorded during the entire study period.

The north-eastern Caspian, including the coast of Kazakhstan's Atyrau region, is particularly vulnerable to falling water levels. With an average depth of around six metres and a very gentle seabed slope, even a relatively small drop in water level can cause the shoreline to retreat over long distances.

Doha will host the Asian Games in 2030 and is among seven potential hosts for the 2036 Olympics, while Formula 1 says its Qatar and Abu Dhabi races will go ahead as scheduled, bringing much-needed relief after months of uncertainty.
Qatar has formally taken over as the next host of the Asian Games as the Gulf country prepares for another major international sporting event, and Formula 1 has confirmed its return to Doha later this year.

The flag of the Olympic Council of Asia was handed over to Qatar at the closing ceremony of the Aichi-Nagoya Asian Games in Japan on Sunday, beginning the four-year countdown to Doha 2030.

Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman bin Jassim Al Thani received the flag on behalf of Qatar from Olympic Council of Asia President Sheikh Joaan bin Hamad Al Thani.

Organisers also unveiled the new Doha 2030 identity and its slogan, “The Games We Live For".

“Receiving the Asian Games Flag in Nagoya is a proud moment for Qatar and an important moment for the wider Asian sporting community,” Qatar Olympic Committee Secretary General Jassim bin Rashid Al Buenain said.

“We thank Aichi-Nagoya for its welcome and for the spirit of the successful Games it has shared with athletes and nations across the continent," Al Buenain added.

The handover followed the end of an Asian Games featuring around 11,000 athletes from 45 countries and regions competing across 43 sports.

China topped the medal table with 169 gold medals and 341 medals overall, followed by hosts Japan with 83 golds.

Qatar’s ambitions go beyond 2030. The IOC confirmed on Tuesday that the country is among seven to have expressed interest in hosting the 2036 Olympics, alongside India, South Africa, South Korea, Germany, Hungary and Turkey.

Meanwhile, Formula 1 has confirmed that its final two races of the 2026 season in Qatar and Abu Dhabi will go ahead as planned despite continuing tensions in the Middle East.

Formula 1 CEO Stefano Domenicali said on Saturday that the current schedule remained in place, with the Qatar Grand Prix set for 29 November and the season finale in Abu Dhabi on 6 December.

“Based on the information we have, everything is confirmed for Qatar and Abu Dhabi,” Domenicali told French broadcaster Canal Plus.

The Bahrain and Saudi Arabian races were cancelled earlier this season following the outbreak of the Iran war, while the Bahrain Grand Prix was relocated to Malaysia before being staged at Sepang this past weekend due to the ongoing tensions in the Gulf.

A possible alternative had also been discussed that could have seen the Abu Dhabi race moved to Imola in Italy.

Seven-time F1 champion Lewis Hamilton said last month that he hoped the season would finish in the Gulf as planned.

“Personally, I want to go. I love going to the Middle East,” the Ferrari driver said, while adding that he trusted Formula 1 to assess the security situation.
