US President Donald Trump and New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani spoke to reporters after their meeting at Gracie Mansion in New York.

Mamdani said all members of the press would be welcome at the event, despite the ongoing dispute over White House media access.

Trump responded to the TV networks’ boycott by saying, “They never boycott me,” while pointing to the journalists gathered outside.

Trump also said he had an obligation to keep what he called “fake news” out of the White House.

French President Emmanuel Macron walked through busy Manhattan streets on Monday evening as he headed to Trump Tower for a meeting with US President Donald Trump.

Accompanied by security and aides, Macron crossed the city on foot, passing police officers and greeting one with a handshake.

French Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot was among his delegation. Macron and Trump met to discuss issues including Ukraine ahead of the UN General Assembly in New York.

Police and medical teams were on the scene, and authorities searched for the attacker while security around the school was tightened.
A teenager opened fire outside a high school in western Turkey on Tuesday, wounding 11 people before being detained by police, a minister said.

The incident happened around 8:00 am in Turgutlu in Manisa province, near the western resort city of Izmir, the regional governor's office said.

"Eleven students were injured, two of them seriously...The minor who carried out the attack was quickly apprehended and taken into custody," Justice Minister Akin Gürlek wrote on X.

He said the suspect's parents and grandfather had also been detained by investigators probing the attack, which saw police and rescuers rushing to the scene alongside panicked parents.

DHA news agency broadcast CCTV footage of the alleged attacker, a young man wearing black jeans, a black jacket and a green top walking along a street holding what appeared to be a long-barrelled gun.

IHA had a similar image of what appeared to be the same person with a gun, although this time running.

Footage from outside the school showed police wrestling a man in a white shirt to the ground before leading him away, as a woman screamed, with onlookers pressed against the school fence, filming with their mobile phones.

"How the weapon used in the attack ended up in the minor's hands, the conditions under which it was kept and supervised...and allegations of bullying prior to the incident are all being investigated," he wrote on X.

School shootings are rare in Turkey, although there were two attacks on consecutive days earlier this year in the south of the country that sparked widespread anger.

In April, a 14-year-old opened fire at a school in the southern province of Kahramanmaraş, killing a teacher and nine students aged 10 and 11. The attacker died at the scene.

A day earlier, another teenager opened fire at his former high school in the southeastern province of Şanlıurfa, wounding 16 people before taking his own life when confronted by police.

Following the two attacks, President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan dismissed a deputy education minister and pledged his government would introduce tighter controls on gun ownership.

"When we look at similar attacks around the world, especially in the United States, we see one of the perpetrators' aims is to terrorise society," he said.

The attack came a day after Education Minister Yusuf Tekin and Interior Minister Mustafa Çiftçi signed a protocol setting out security measures for the 2026-2027 academic year, which began last Monday.

"To ensure such pain is never experienced again, I would like to remind everyone that we must be extremely vigilant this year," he said.

The interior ministry also carried out a fresh security assessment of schools and assigned police officers to first- and second-degree risk schools, where they remain on permanent duty, he said.

Two independent exit polls covering 27 countries put New People first with around 45% and United Russia as low as 2.8%, the reverse of official results inside Russia, where Putin's party took nearly 58%. Exit poll organisers said the gap "destroys the image of supposed support for the authorities."
Russians voting at embassies and consulates abroad backed the liberal New People party over Vladimir Putin's United Russia by a margin of eight to one, according to two independent exit polls conducted during this weekend's State Duma elections — the reverse of official results from inside Russia, where Putin's party took nearly 58% of the vote.

The Vote Abroad project, which surveyed almost 15,700 voters at 27 polling stations across 22 countries, put New People at 43%, the Communist Party at 18.3% and United Russia at 5.5%.

In 10 countries, including Armenia, Germany and Finland, United Russia failed to clear the 5% threshold required to win Duma seats.

A separate exit poll by opposition movement Vesna and the Progress project, covering 18 stations in 17 countries, put New People first at every location with 47.3%, the Communist Party second at 19.2% and United Russia at 2.8%.

Inside Russia, the Central Election Commission reported United Russia winning nearly 58%, the Communist Party at 14%, the LDPR at almost 9% and New People below 8%.

"The contrast is indeed very stark," Ivan Zhdanov, director of the Progress project and a former head of Alexei Navalny's Anti-Corruption Foundation, told Euronews.

"83.5% of those who answered our question on trust do not fully or mostly trust the official results," Zhdanov added.

"In purely arithmetic terms, votes cast abroad are hardly going to determine the composition of the Duma," he said.

"But they are important: they show a completely different reality and destroy the image of supposed support for the authorities."

Many Russians who left the country after the 2022 invasion oppose the war, and the only anti-war party Yabloko was barred from the election on a procedural pretext and excluded from the federal ballot entirely.

Those abroad who opposed the war had no Yabloko option and predominantly voted New People instead, exit poll workers said.

"The aim of our civic initiative is to provide a truthful picture of the electoral process abroad," said Irina Kh, part of the active core of Vote Abroad alongside around 15 other activists.

"We use no party symbols and have no affiliation with political organisations — we carry out our surveys impartially and independently."

As Iran’s academic year begins, families face severe strain from doubling tuition fees, soaring stationery prices, war fears and a plunging currency. Euronews Persian spoke to Iranians about declining enrollments and deep economic distress across the country.
Iran's new academic year opens on Wednesday against a backdrop of tuition fees that in some cases nearly tripled over the summer, with enrollment falling at private schools across Tehran as war fears, sanctions and the continued collapse of the rial squeeze family budgets.

Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian marked the start of the academic year by ringing the school bell at a girls' primary school in Tehran on Monday.

The Ministry of Education has instructed schools to prioritise in-person teaching after periods of online learning during previous phases of the war.

The ministry also issued a directive capping tuition increases at private schools at 35% for the new academic year, setting fees across the country at between 14 million and 145 million tomans (€53–€549). In practice, the cap is not being observed.

Bita, a final-year high school student in eastern Tehran, told Euronews she paid 80 million tomans (€303) last year and 220 million tomans (€833) this year — a 175% increase.

"I'm in my final year of high school. Students in lower grades pay somewhat less. We also have to pay a separate fee for pamphlets and school-printed textbooks, which comes to around 20 million tomans (€76)," Bita said.

"Right now, for a senior at a private high school, their family has to pay roughly 250 million tomans (€947) for their education. Of course, in the affluent neighborhoods in the northern part of the city, this cost goes even higher," she explained.

Although the rial is Iran’s official currency, everyday pricing and transactions are conducted in tomans — where 1 toman equals 10 rials — with the open market rate on Monday standing at slightly over 264,000 tomans (or 2,640,000 rials) per euro.

She said tuition at one school she works with rose from 36 million tomans (€136) two years ago to 85 million tomans (€322) this year. She has also seen declining enrolments driven not only by cost but by security concerns.

"At one of these schools many pupils have not enrolled because it is opposite a police station and their parents worry that if the war starts again their children will not be safe there," Maryam told Euronews.

"Their other concern is that classes may move online and they are not willing to pay heavy fees for virtual lessons at non-profit schools."

One school in Tehran's Shahrak-e Valiasr district closed entirely after failing to reach the minimum enrolment required to operate, Maryam added.

Official statistics put total enrolment in Iran's fee-charging schools and universities at approximately 3.3 million students, of whom nearly 70% pay tuition.

Total enrolment has fallen from 4.4 million students over the past decade, a decline the ministry partly attributes to reduced purchasing power.

Mehra, a third-term law student at the Islamic Azad University in Tehran, said her combined fixed and variable tuition doubled from 11 million tomans (€41) to 22 million tomans (€83).

The costs extend beyond tuition. A basic school supplies bundle now ranges from 3 million tomans (€11.30) to 17 million tomans (€64.30).

In a rare move, the top five US broadcasters said on Monday that they were halting "pool" coverage of the White House, where a broadcaster films on behalf of all television outlets for the day, in solidarity with CNN.
US news networks staged an extraordinary protest against the White House ban on CNN, MS NOW and Politico on Monday, effectively boycotting all coverage of President Donald Trump.

Journalists from the three outlets were denied any access to the White House grounds on Saturday and had their passes confiscated because of coverage Trump said he considered "fake news".

CNN, meanwhile, was removed from its pool duties, which are shared by the five major networks. It had been scheduled to cover the president's trip to New York for the United Nations General Assembly.

In an email to members, the TV pool said there would be no replacement coverage, and other networks would suspend their own pool coverage indefinitely.

“Effective today, the TV pool will not be covering events designated as pool coverage of the President,” said the email from the current chair of the pool, Fox's Washington bureau chief, Bryan Boughton. “This follows the White House’s position preventing CNN from fulfilling its assigned pool duties."

"The public has a vital interest in receiving accurate, independent information about its government. No administration should restrict a news organisation because it objects to its reporting," ABC, CBS, CNN, Fox and NBC said in a statement.

Other news outlets also took steps in solidarity. A number of news organisations, including The AP, AFP, Washington Post and The New York Times, decided not to publish photographs of presidential events on Monday.

As a result of the row, Trump's opening of his cherished new White House helipad and his trip to the United Nations General Assembly proceeded without live television coverage.

The ribbon-cutting ceremony for the giant granite helipad on the South Lawn was only streamed on the White House's website, and Trump's comments were completely inaudible due to the lack of pool microphones.

On Monday evening, the White House launched a streaming channel, "Trump TV," which it said would show "top videos, major remarks, and must-see highlights streaming 24/7 and updated in real time."

When it went online, it was showing archive footage of Trump delivering a speech on July 3 at Mount Rushmore.

Vice President JD Vance accused the news outlets of disseminating "effectively far-left propaganda."

"They can still report. They still have the access to free speech. They're just not going to get an office in the White House anymore," he told reporters.

The outlets announced on Monday that they have decided to sue the Trump administration. "This ban could not be a more direct assault on the First Amendment nor a more blatant violation of our most fundamental constitutional principles," the organisations said in a court filing.
