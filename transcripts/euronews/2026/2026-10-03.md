IEA says 325 million barrels of emergency oil released, with 75 million still to come
Oil is in short supply because of wars in Middle East and Ukraine

G7 leaders agreed on Friday to release 100 million barrels of diesel and crude after US pressure on Europe, with supplies squeezed by Iran's restrictions on shipping through the Strait of Hormuz.
The International Energy Agency said Saturday that member countries had so far released 325 million barrels of oil and oil-derivative products from strategic reserves, from the 400 million barrels promised in March.

The update came a day after G7 leaders, in a statement issued by French President Emmanuel Macron's office, agreed in coordination with the IEA to immediately release 100 million barrels of diesel and crude oil, following US pressure on Europe to tap its reserves and ease supply concerns caused by the US-Iran war.

The G7 did not say whether the 100 million barrels included the outstanding 75 million barrels from the March IEA commitment, or came on top of that.

The IEA statement said "around 325 million barrels of the IEA collective action announced on 11 March have so far been released, representing over 80 percent of the 400 million barrels originally pledged in the action".

Donald Trump's administration has been pressuring allies to release diesel reserves to ease a spike in prices for the fuel, being felt in America and elsewhere.

The war in the Middle East has squeezed diesel supplies, with Iran restricting shipping through the Strait of Hormuz in response to US and Israeli attacks.

The market has also been hit by Ukraine's strikes on Russian refineries, as Kyiv hits back against Moscow's own attacks.

China opens anti-dumping probe into EU days before Beijing trade talks
Sevcovic is due in Beijing next week.

Running a €1 billion-a-day deficit with China, Brussels has nearly tripled the rate of its own trade investigations and is warning of "harsher measures" unless Beijing offers something concrete.
China has opened an anti-dumping investigation into a chemical imported from the European Union, less than a week before the bloc's trade commissioner arrives in Beijing for talks meant to stop a dispute from escalating.

The commerce ministry in China said on Saturday it would examine whether low-priced p-nitrotoluene from the EU was flooding the Chinese market. A spokesperson said domestic producers had asked for the probe, complaining of "damage to production and operations".

The chemical is used to make dyes, pharmaceuticals and pesticides and the investigation is due to conclude within 12 months, with a possible six-month extension.

It follows an investigation by the EU, opened in September, into polyvinyl chloride imports from countries including China. Beijing denies that its manufacturers owe their competitiveness to state support and has threatened countermeasures against EU moves to block its firms.

"China has repeatedly reiterated that the EU needs to face its own economic and trade problems directly and resolve mutual concerns through dialogue and consultation," the spokesperson said.

Dumping is when a company exports a product for less than it charges at home, or for less than it costs to make. World Trade Organization rules allow the importing country to respond with extra tariffs, known as anti-dumping duties, if an investigation finds that the cheap imports have injured its own producers.

Brussels is reaching for the tool far more often. Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said in August that the EU had opened 30 trade defence investigations in a year, nearly triple the usual rate, as Chinese imports rose 45% in five years.

EU Trade Commissioner Maroš Sefčovič will be in Beijing on 8–9 October to co-chair a session of the EU-China Trade and Investment Council and meet Commerce Minister Wang Wentao. The visit marks the deadline Brussels set in June for the first tangible results from its dialogue with Beijing.

Brussels wants assurances that China will curb exports to the EU and extend a truce on rare earth exports, with the bloc's trade deficit running at €1 billion a day. Beijing has already warned it would respond firmly to any new EU restrictions on Chinese companies or products.

In an exclusive interview with Euronews last month, Sefčovič described the talks as highly political and said negotiations had to produce concrete results. Without them, he warned, there would be "a strong political movement to push for, I would say, harsher measures".

He also told Euronews that if he had nothing to show EU leaders, "the political interest would be to look for the solution through other instruments". Those leaders meet in Brussels on 15–16 October, a week after his trip.

Some 1.5 million people are eligible to cast ballots in the parliamentary election, Latvia's 11th since gaining independence from the Soviet Union in 1991.
Polls opened on Saturday in Latvia's parliamentary election shaped by concerns over neighbouring Russia and a cost of living crisis.

Some 1.5 million people are eligible to cast ballots in the parliamentary election, Latvia's 11th since gaining independence from the Soviet Union in 1991.

Partial results are expected on Saturday evening, with preliminary official results due on Sunday.

The Baltic state is a key pillar of NATO's eastern defensive flank, and experts say there is widespread support for Ukraine in the war with Russia.

"The stance towards Russia is the main reason for Latvians' Western, NATO and European anchoring," a Western diplomat in Riga said, adding that it would be hard to imagine major political changes.

Polls predict the centre-right United List, led by Prime Minister Andris Kulbergs, will emerge the largest party in parliament in Saturday's vote.

But his centre-right coalition, which also brings together the centrist New Unity, nationalist National Alliance and the Union of Greens and Farmers, is expected to emerge weakened from the election.

The current governing coalition is made up of United List, New Unity, the Union of Greens and Farmers, and the National Alliance. Polls suggest the bloc is unlikely to retain power in its current form after the vote.

Security is at the top of voters' minds in a country bordering Russia which was annexed and ruled by Moscow for much of the 20th century.

According to Roberts Kits, an analyst at the Latvian think tank LaSER, "defence and security will be the priority". Apart from a few dissenting voices, he said, there is broad consensus on maintaining military spending at nearly five percent of GDP and on supporting Ukraine.

Latvia is the least affluent of the three Baltic States in terms of living standards, despite catching up since joining the EU in 2004.

Growth is sluggish, with voters pointing to the cost of living, the state of the healthcare system and the difficulties of public finances. Complicating this are major regional disparities.

The gap between Latvia's capital and the rest of the country is stark. Around Riga, GDP per capita reached 101 percent of the European average in 2024, according to a European Commission report. In other regions, the figure does not exceed 50 percent.

The contrast is sharpest in Latgale, Latvia's easternmost region, where depopulation is accelerating and economic opportunity is scarce. The region is home to a large majority of Russian speakers, and its decline is reflected most visibly in Daugavpils, the country's second-largest city, where 90 percent of the population is Russian-speaking.

"The most important thing is the lack of young people and the lack of good jobs," said Aigars Soikants, a 30-year-old Daugavpils resident. "There isn't enough work. People are leaving."

Despite living in a city with a predominantly Russian-speaking population, Soikants says identity is not what will drive his vote. He plans to back "a centrist or right-wing party which acts in the interest of the country."

Nearly 59% of Russian households own at least one cat, the highest rate globally, but the 2022 mobilisation order left hundreds of thousands of animals abandoned when owners fled the country or were sent to the front in Ukraine. Up to 98% of Russia's stray cats and dogs were once domestic pets.
Russia has the highest rate of cat ownership in the world, with nearly 59% of households keeping at least one cat — and an estimated 2.8 million stray cats living on its streets, a number that rose sharply after the 2022 mobilisation order sent thousands of pet owners fleeing the country or to the front amid Russia's ongoing all-out war in Ukraine.

According to a 2024-2026 study by analytics platform Seasia Stats, Russia ranks first globally for cats as pets. Romania ranks second in Europe at 48%, followed by Poland at 41%, Latvia at 37% and Hungary at 35%.

Outside Europe, Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand, New Zealand and the US also make the top 10. The Seasia Stats findings are consistent with a 2023 study by insurer Ingosstrakh and the Financial University, in which 58.8% of Russian respondents described themselves as cat owners.

The stray figures come from a separate study by the Centre for Animal Nutrition and Well-being Studies, the Nika animal charity and research firm Ipsos.

They put the total stray animal population in Russia in 2025 at nearly 3.6 million, of which 2.8 million are cats and 800,000 are dogs.

Of those, around 170,000 were in shelters at the time of the study, while the rest were living on the streets. Researchers say up to 98% of stray cats and dogs were once domestic pets abandoned by their owners.

Animal welfare organisations recorded a sharp increase in abandonments after Russia announced partial mobilisation in September 2022.

Men who received call-up papers were either leaving the country or preparing for deployment and in many cases could not arrange care for their animals or find shelter places.

On classified advertising websites, owners threatened to have pets put down if no one took them. Shelter volunteers reported similar ultimatums from people surrendering animals in person.

Russia has 819 shelters, the overwhelming majority privately run, alongside hundreds of charities and tens of thousands of volunteers. Together they care for between 170,000 and 250,000 animals — no more than 5–7% of the estimated stray population.

The popularity of cats in Eastern Europe has historical roots: before industrialised agriculture, cats served a practical function on farms, where their presence and scent deterred rodents.

In Southeast Asia, researchers link rising ownership to urbanisation and the growth of a middle class.

Cats have lived alongside humans for around 10,000 years. Today, they are human companions of choice, alongside dogs.
