Hundreds of secondary school students blocked school entrances in Rennes and Paris during a national day of action, calling for more staff and funding for education.
… More
Hundreds of students took part in protests in Rennes and Paris, blocking school entrances and demanding more resources for education.

In Rennes, students barricaded the entrance to Lycée Bréquigny, where bins were set alight and police faced protesters.

In Paris, students at Lycée Simone Weil used bins and crates to block an entrance and called for more staff and funding, citing overcrowded classrooms and poor conditions in some schools.

The bomber went down near Krasnoyarovo shortly after taking off from the Ukrainka air base, which Russia had used to shelter surviving strategic bombers after Ukraine's Operation Spiderweb destroyed or damaged more than 40 aircraft at Russian airfields in June 2025.
A Russian military plane crashed in the far eastern region of Amur on Tuesday, killing six crew members and leaving another injured, the Russian defence ministry said.

The Russian defence ministry said the military aircraft went down in an uninhabited area during a training flight and was not carrying ammunition.

"According to preliminary data, six crew members died, while one was injured and evacuated to a medical facility," the ministry added.

According to the Russian state-affiliated media, the crash occurred near the village of Krasnoyarovo shortly after the bomber took off from the Ukrainka air base.

Authorities have opened a criminal case and an investigative commission from Russia's Aerospace Forces has been flown to the crash site. Initial assessments suggest the aircraft went down because of a technical malfunction.

Unverified videos posted on social media showed what appeared to be flames and smoke rising from the crash site, with booming and crackling sounds in the background.

The Tu-95 is a large, nuclear-capable bomber designed in the Soviet Union, first flown in 1952. It is the only turboprop-powered strategic bomber still in operational use.

The Ukrainka air base — one of Russia's largest strategic long-range bases — is located in the Seryshevo district of the Amur region, in the country's Far East.

In June 2025, it was among the targets of a major operation by Ukraine's security service SBU against Russian military airfields codenamed Spiderweb.

In Operation Spiderweb, FPV drones concealed in lorries with remotely opening roofs were used to destroy or damage more than 40 Russian aircraft, including between seven and eight Tu-95s.

Russia redeployed the surviving strategic bombers from its European air bases to Ukrainka.

Months into the war with Iran, Gulf exporters are finding ways to keep oil and gas moving, but crossing Hormuz remains risky and some customers are still missing contracted cargoes.
Middle East crude oil exports rose in September to their highest monthly level since the Iran war began seven months ago.

Preliminary data from Kpler put regional exports at 16.3 million barrels a day. That was still around 3.2 million barrels a day below the 19.5 million recorded in February.

Saudi Arabia accounted for much of the increase, more than doubling its exports from 2.45 million barrels a day in August to around 5.4 million in September.

Flows through the Strait of Hormuz were projected to reach about 9.7 million barrels a day as Saudi Arabia sent additional crude from its Gulf terminals after attacks damaged its East-West pipeline to the Red Sea. The regional total also includes oil shipped from ports outside the strait.

Several Qatar-linked LNG tankers also crossed Hormuz this month after no visible crossings were recorded in August.

Normal exports have not resumed, however, and QatarEnergy has extended delivery suspensions for some customers in Europe and Asia.

Edison said it had received a new notice from QatarEnergy extending force majeure until early December. Another six LNG cargoes due at Italy’s Adriatic LNG terminal will not be delivered.

That brings the number of missed cargoes since April to 35. Edison has replaced 23 of them, mainly with gas from the United States.

Edison’s 25-year contract with QatarEnergy supplies 6.4 billion cubic metres of gas a year, equal to around 10% of Italy’s total consumption.

Some customers in Pakistan and Bangladesh have also been told that delivery suspensions will continue through November.

Force majeure allows a company to suspend its contractual obligations when events outside its control prevent it from fulfilling them.

The disruption began after joint US-Israeli strikes on Iran on 28 February in the opening salvo of the ongoing war, which has sharply reduced traffic through Hormuz, while both the US and Iran targeted commercial ships in and around the strait.

US President Donald Trump on Saturday rejected an Iranian proposal to reopen Hormuz and end the fighting within seven days. The proposal had been passed to Washington through Qatari mediators, who are continuing to push for talks.

Iranian Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf said no infrastructure in the region would be safe unless Iran’s security was guaranteed, according to state broadcaster IRIB.

Ship-tracking data from Kpler and LSEG showed GasLog Skagen off Sri Lanka on Sunday carrying LNG from Qatar’s Ras Laffan terminal. It had last been tracked inside the Gulf on 20 September.

Al Ghashamiya delivered a Qatari cargo to India last week, while Shandong Redwood carried LNG to Pakistan. Al Daayen emerged from Hormuz with a shipment bound for China.

This year's Pacific hurricane season is shaping up as more severe than usual, as a Super El Niño, an extreme version of a recurring weather pattern, wreaks devastation, in the form of historic droughts and flooding.
Hurricane Polo made landfall on Mexico's Baja California peninsula on Tuesday, cutting power and causing flooding as the sea breached coastal defences.

The Category 3 storm is the latest in a busy storm season turbo-charged by the El Niño weather phenomenon which has caused unprecedented warming in the equatorial Pacific.

Polo hit just south of Las Barrancas, a small fishing village in Baja California Sur state on Mexico's west side, the US National Hurricane Centre (NHC) said, warning of life-threatening winds and flash flooding.

As Polo approached, strong winds churned the sea and waves off Puerto San Carlos, a fishing port of nearly 6,000 people in Baja California Sur.

AFP reporters noted that the sea had already breached several sections of a sand barrier, causing water to pour into the street.

The town's power supply was cut, palm trees swayed violently in the wind and Mexican authorities ordered residents to take shelter.

Juan Manuel Sarabia had boarded up the facade of his home and reinforced it with wooden beams.

This year's Pacific hurricane season is shaping up as more severe than usual, as a Super El Niño, an extreme version of a recurring weather pattern, wreaks devastation, in the form of historic droughts and flooding.

Meteorologists have issued Polo-related warnings of heavy rain and flooding in parts of 10 US states including Arizona, New Mexico, Texas and Colorado. Several of the states are experiencing drought.

The head of Mexico's meteorological service, Fabian Vazquez, said storm surges and heavy rains could cause "very severe" damage.

At one high school near the sea, shopkeeper Maria Palomino was preparing to ride out the storm on a mattress on the floor.

"If something happens, it's better to come to the shelter," the 57-year-old said. "We'll be among people we know."

Only a handful of homes and businesses in the town were boarded up or sandbagged against flooding.

A passing police officer told AFP he believed the entire town should have been forced to evacuate.

The military has deployed nearly 7,000 troops in Baja California Sur state, where classes were suspended and public servants have been ordered to work from home, where possible.

Laura Velazquez, National Coordinator of Civil Protection, said all maritime activities were suspended in the area.

Remnants of the so-called IS terrorist group have continued to target energy infrastructure after the fall of al-Assad in the eastern province of Deir ez-Zor. Syria produces a fraction of its pre-war gas output against increasingly high demand.
An apparent explosion on an eastern gas pipeline in Syria halted gas flow to power stations in the latest act of sabotage against the Mediterranean country's energy facilities, authorities said Tuesday.

"A fire broke out Monday evening in a gas pipeline between the Al-Shoula area and Deir ez-Zor due to the explosion of a sectional valve caused by sabotage," the official SANA news agency reported.

Quoting an official at the Syrian Petroleum Company, SANA added that "the fire halted gas flow from the Al-Jabsah plant to power stations".

Eastern Syria's Deir ez-Zor province, home to the country's main oil and gas fields, has seen persistent attacks since the so-called Islamic State terrorist group's defeat in 2019.

IS group remnants regularly target energy infrastructure in the region in asymmetrical attacks meant to subvert Damascus' push to fully secure its energy logistics.

A gas pipeline at the same facility was struck in what Syrian authorities also described as deliberate sabotage on 18 August.

Syrians across the country have been complaining about rising fuel prices, as authorities cite the Iran war as the reason for the hike.

Earlier this month, Syrians across the country took to the streets to protest the price increase.

Before the 2011-2024 war, which ravaged the country's infrastructure, Syria produced 30 million cubic metres of gas per day for electricity generation, according to Energy Minister Mohammad al-Bashir.

Today, production has dropped to 8.5 million cubic metres, while demand ranges between 22 and 25 million cubic metres, according to al-Bashir.

In June, Damascus signed a contract with US oil giant ConocoPhillips to develop the country's gas sector as the new authorities, who toppled longtime dictator Bashar al-Assad in 2024, seek to attract investment to support post-war reconstruction.

The Al-Jabsah facility is one of Syria's main gas processing plants, located in an area that was returned to the central government's full control earlier this year.

The blasts that wounded three South Korean soldiers last week were apparently caused by North Korean mines, Seoul said on Tuesday, adding it was an armistice violation that would prompt "corresponding measures."

One troop lost a foot due to the explosions in the Demilitarised Zone (DMZ), the heavily mined buffer zone that has divided the two Koreas since their 1953 armistice.

The northern and southern boundaries of the zone are among the world's most heavily fortified frontiers and landmine incidents have previously heightened tensions between the two countries.

Defence Minister Kang Shin-chul's comments came a day after his ministry said North Korean mines were "highly suspected" of causing the two explosions last week.

"This is a clear violation of the armistice agreement and since North Korea bears responsibility, we will take corresponding measures," he told a parliamentary hearing, when asked whether the military had tentatively concluded that the mine was North Korean.

Kang said South Korean forces were in the DMZ to clear the area in advance of an operation with the UN Command to assess terrain changes caused by North Korean forces south of the Military Demarcation Line, which marks the nations' effective border.

The mine detectors used by the officials were the "latest models," the minister said, but detection efforts were hampered by dense vegetation in the area, noting that "clearing all the vegetation as they advanced was difficult."

"Our current assessment is that the mines may not have been detected because the detectors were being operated too far above the ground," he said.

At the hearing, Yang Jin-hyeok of the Joint Chiefs of Staff told lawmakers that a site investigation earlier on Tuesday discovered an unexploded North Korean plastic anti-personnel mine.

Kang told lawmakers the military "has a range of necessary measures at our disposal, and we will implement them in stages", without giving details.

Seoul and Pyongyang remain technically at war since the Korean conflict ended in a truce, not a peace treaty.

Kwon Dae-won, vice chairman of South Korea's Joint Chiefs of Staff, said on Monday that two landmines exploded during the 21 September incident and that a third, unexploded mine was found while soldiers were withdrawing after the blast.

"Taking into account the crater and the pattern of the flames from the explosion, the extent of the injuries sustained by the service members...we assess that North Korean landmines are highly suspected to have caused the blast," Kwon said at the time.

Since 2024, after North Korean leader Kim Jong-un designated South Korea as his country's "principal enemy," Pyongyang's military has laid mines and built anti-tank barriers along the frontier as part of efforts to permanently seal it off.

In a 2015 landmine incident, two South Korean soldiers were seriously wounded in a blast on the southern side of the DMZ, with one losing both legs.

Seoul concluded that North Korean troops had crossed the Military Demarcation Line to plant the mines, an accusation Pyongyang denied.

A Caspian Airlines Boeing 737 was seized at Istanbul Airport on Monday by court-ordered enforcement officers acting on a debt claim of approximately €3 million, stranding passengers who had already boarded a flight bound for Iran.

Lawyers and bailiffs representing the creditor, aviation representation and supervision company ACM Temsil Gözetim, arrived at the aircraft — registered EP-KPB — as it was ready for departure and served the seizure order before it could take off, according to Anadolu Agency.

Caspian Airlines is a Tehran-based carrier founded in 1993 and operating as part of Iran's state aviation group. It flies Boeing 737s on domestic and international routes, including to Istanbul.

Iranian carriers have faced chronic difficulties maintaining their fleets and meeting financial obligations to foreign counterparts as a result of decades of US sanctions, which restrict access to aircraft, spare parts, maintenance services and international financial systems.

The war between the US and Iran that began in February has compounded those pressures, with Iranian airspace repeatedly disrupted and international routes curtailed.

The seizure came days after sweeping US secondary sanctions on Iran's aviation sector took effect last Wednesday.

The US Treasury had sanctioned 27 Iranian airlines and nine associated service providers on 8 September under its Operation Economic Outcast, warning that foreign airports, fuel suppliers, ground handlers and ticketing companies that continued servicing Iranian carriers would themselves face sanctions and risk being cut off from the US dollar system.

The secondary sanctions triggered a rapid contraction of Iranian aviation internationally, with flights by Iranian carriers suspended in Iraq, the UAE, Azerbaijan and Oman.

Lufthansa and Austrian Airlines suspended their own routes to Iran through 24 October. Qatar Airways suspended services citing airspace restrictions. China, Russia and Pakistan have continued to maintain flights to and from Iran.

Daily international flights at Tehran's main airport fell from 80 to 90 before the sanctions to around 41 by Monday, according to tracking data.

Tehran has filed formal complaints with the UN, the International Civil Aviation Organisation and the International Court of Justice.

Turkey has not formally barred Iranian carriers, which is why Caspian Airlines was still operating the Istanbul route at the time of the seizure.

The president of the United Arab Emirate, Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan, met with Israel’s Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu in the UAE on Sunday, official news agency WAM said, confirming earlier Israeli media reports about the meeting.

"The two sides discussed bilateral relations between the UAE and Israel and ways to strengthen and develop them," the brief statement said.

On Monday, Israel said that Netanyahu had visited the UAE the day before to discuss the Iran war and regional issues.

"The visit focused on strengthening relations between the two countries and on regional challenges," the prime minister's office said in a statement on X.

The visit came weeks after a bombshell Israeli media report that the Emirati president had warned Netanyahu ahead of the Hamas-led attack on Israel on 7 October 2023, which the Israeli premier did not pass on to the security services.

According to Israeli media, Netanyahu flew to the UAE on Sunday to ask Sheikh Mohamed to deny having given him that warning in 2023.

Meanwhile, Open Source Intel reported that Netanyahu had also met with “officials from several Gulf states,” during the UAE visit, noting that Israel has no formal diplomatic relations with many of them.

“Military and government aircraft that landed in Abu Dhabi during the visit included a Libyan government plane, a Moroccan Hercules, a Qatari military plane, and an Emirati government plane that flew in from Riyadh,” OSINT said in a post on X.

Israeli media also reported that delegations from Morocco, Libya, Jordan and Qatar had apparently attended the talks.

Earlier on Monday, before Netanyahu's office formally confirmed the destination, Israel's domestic security service filed a complaint against a TV channel that reported the visit.

"This evening the Shin Bet filed a complaint with the Chief Military Censor, demanding that Channel 12 be prosecuted to the fullest extent of the law following a severe censorship violation," read a statement from Netanyahu's office, which oversees the security agency.

The publication of his visit "endangered the lives of the prime minister and his wife, as well as the delegation, which included the head of the Mossad" spy agency and other top security officials, the PM's office said in its statement.

The visit was made public when the premier's plane was still outside Israeli airspace, it noted.

Mossad, which also operates under the Prime Minister's office, issued a similar condemnation, according to the statement.

In May, the UAE had denied reports that Netanyahu secretly visited the country, after Netanyahu's own office said he had met with the UAE president during the war with Iran.

The UAE is a top US ally in the region and one of a handful of Arab nations with official ties to Israel after signing the Abraham Accords during US President Donald Trump's first term in 2020.

Russian President Vladimir Putin has signed a decree to boost the army by 15,500 troops in the fourth such order this year.

"The authorised ... strength of the Russian armed forces is to be set at 2,441,630 units, including 1,550,500 military personnel," the decree said.

Putin previously signed decrees boosting the army's size in March, June and July. The combined increase since March amounts to 47,860 soldiers.

Russia's president did not state the reason for the decision, but it has been taken against the backdrop of rising losses in Moscow's full-scale war in Ukraine.

This year, Russian advances on the battlefield have slowed, as widespread drone use has made rapid movement of large troop units almost impossible. In recent weeks, reports have also emerged of Ukrainian breakthroughs on certain sections of the front.

The last time Putin announced a "partial mobilisation" of reservists was in September 2022 after a series of setbacks at the front. That decree remains in force.

In August, Ukraine's intelligence services reported that Moscow plans to call up up to 300,000 people this year for deployment to the front, and the same number again next year.

A number of analysts, citing data on preparatory measures by Russian authorities on the ground, predicted a new wave of mobilisation as soon as this autumn, immediately after elections to the State Duma, triggering renewed fears among ordinary Russians of being dragged to the front and a surge of interest in emigration.

Putin dismissed these rumours as "utter nonsense". The Kremlin insisted that contract soldiers and volunteers were meeting the needs of Russia's armed forces, while independent analysts say recruitment among them continues to shrink despite promises of large financial rewards.

"In essence, Russia has already begun additional mobilisation, it is expanding the scale of recruitment to its ranks, using various methods for this," Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said on Tuesday, commenting on Putin's decree in a video address published on X.

"It also plans to increase the number of foreigners, and it is important that the governments of the countries from which Russia recruits people into its ranks counteract this," he added.

Zelenskyy said that he has new data indicating that North Korea is preparing to send 10,000 of its soldiers to Russia, in addition to the 8,000 who have already been deployed there.

The Euronews Travel & Tourism Summit 2026 in Brussels brought together political leaders, policymakers and industry experts to debate the biggest issues shaping the future of travel.
Europe’s travel industry was in the spotlight today at the Euronews Travel & Tourism Summit 2026 at the Albert Hall in Brussels.

Throughout the day, Euronews was live from the Belgian capital with interviews, panels and debates exploring how tourism is responding to a year marked by geopolitical disruption, airspace closures and extreme weather.

The Michelin Guide’s expanded role beyond restaurants is helping travellers sort through digital noise with a human-centred curation of stays that doesn’t rely solely on new technologies.

At the closing panel of the Euronews Travel & Tourism Summit 2026, Marie-France Perrel, Head of Business Development, Hotels at Michelin Guide, said the hotel selection for the Michelin Keys “is based on real humans travelling the world deciding whether or not a property should be a part of the collection based on the five criteria”.

Amid algorithms and endless digital reviews at play, Perrel said that the role of hotel guide is “essential” amid all the online chatter.

Hotels can receive one, two or three keys based on their quality and consistency, architecture and interior design, personality and character, value for money, as well as what the property adds to its locale.

She also highlighted a growing trend of food and travel being seen as one experience. Data from the Michelin Guide found that in 2026, users looking to book a hotel also searched for dining spots nearby.

Perrel also highlighted the changes in luxury travel trends, with a shift towards travellers seeking a sense of place and properties that offer a connection to their surroundings. Luxury travel is now an “ecosystem,” she said, adding that it’s also a “broad spectrum,” with the Guide also offering insights into the wide world of hospitality.

Kyrgyzstan’s Ambassador Extraordinary and Plenipotentiary to Belgium has revealed that the Central Asian country is planning direct air links to Europe, with routes potentially launching as early as 2027.

Aidit Erkin, Kyrgyzstan’s envoy, said that transport connectivity is an “extremely important” priority, alongside strengthening cooperation and partnerships with Europe. Currently, travellers in Kyrgyzstan connect to Europe with Turkish Airlines-operated flights via Istanbul.

However, now that all Kyrgyzstan-certified carriers have been removed from the EU’s Air Safety List in June, Erkin announced during the Euronews Travel & Tourism Summit 2026 that work is underway for “direct flights from Kyrgyzstan to Europe”, with routes on the horizon to launch next year.

Joining Erkin on the panel was Florian Sengstschmid, Chief Executive Officer of the Azerbaijan Tourism Board. He highlighted a growing demand for travel between Azerbaijan and Europe, saying that policymakers should consider ways to facilitate that demand.

Sengstschmid pointed to visas as a “bottleneck” in Central Asian-European connectivity. Meanwhile, Erkin added that the Kyrgyz President is promoting a common “Silk Road visa” that would unlock access to all Central Asian countries for travellers with a single visa.

Amid preparations for Expo 2027, Dušan Borovčanin, advisor at EXPO 2027 Belgrade, sees the event as a catalyst for tourism and development for Serbia’s capital and beyond, across the wider Western Balkans. Belgrade won the right to host over competing proposals like Spain, the US, Argentina and Thailand.

Speaking at the Euronews Travel & Tourism Summit 2026, Borovčanin highlighted that Belgrade’s hosting of the event marks the first time it's being hosted in the region. As well as instilling a sense of “national pride” for Serbians, Borovčanin added that Expo 2027 will be an opportunity to showcase the country’s development over the past 20 years.

“We want this to be a catalyst for the further development of our region in economic terms,” he said. “But also in terms of the national branding and national pride, and I think that is the reason why countries are also signing up and participating.”

The Arakan Army accused the army of using fighter jets to drop bombs near the civilian market around noon on Monday, killing many people. The incident is the latest in a continued territorial fight between Myanmar’s ruling junta and the Arakan Army, which controls the state of Rakhine.
An airstrike carried out by Myanmar’s military near a market in territory controlled by a powerful rebel group killed at least 49 people and wounded 58 others on Monday, according to representatives of the group and a rescue official.

The attack in western Rakhine state was the latest in a series of deadly airstrikes against pro-democracy resistance forces and ethnic armed groups across the country.

Myanmar has been in turmoil since the military seized power from the elected government of Aung San Suu Kyi on 1 February, 2021, triggering widespread opposition.

After security forces suppressed peaceful protests with lethal force, many opponents of military rule took up arms, plunging large parts of the country into conflict.

Monday’s attack occurred in Kyauktaw township, about 340 kilometres west of Mandalay, the country’s second-largest city, according to the Arakan Army, a powerful ethnic armed group based in Rakhine which controls Kyauktaw.

The Arakan Army said in a statement released early on Tuesday that a fighter jet dropped two bombs near a local market around noon, killing vendors, shoppers and pedestrians. The group said 58 other people were injured, 22 of them critically. Details of the attack could not be independently verified.

The statement said the group "will take effective retaliatory measures soon against the individuals and organisations directly responsible for the attacks involving deliberate airstrikes that have caused mass casualties.”

Wai Hun Aung, a senior rescue official in Rakhine, confirmed the casualties to The Associated Press, citing rescue workers at the scene. He said the market was the region’s main wholesale centre, drawing traders and shoppers from across the state.

“This is one of the most severe airstrikes in Rakhine,” he said. Photos and videos posted on social media showed buildings on fire, damaged vehicles and parts of a nearby bridge destroyed.

The military did not immediately comment on the attack, though it has routinely stated in previous instances that it strikes only legitimate military targets and has accused resistance groups of terrorism.

The Arakan Army is the well-trained and well-armed military wing of a movement seeking autonomy for Rakhine’s ethnic minority from Myanmar’s central government.

It launched an offensive in Rakhine in November 2023 and has since captured a strategically important regional military headquarters and 14 of the state’s 17 townships.

The military has imposed severe restrictions on trade routes since fighting intensified in Rakhine, worsening food shortages.

In December, a military airstrike on a hospital in Rakhine’s Mrauk-U township killed at least 34 people and wounded 80 others.

A second high profile prosecutor in Trump’s grand conspiracy Justice Department probe into whether former government officials conspired against him has abruptly resigned from his post. Kurt Olsen was one of the biggest backers of the 2020 rigged elections claims that Trump has championed for years.
A lawyer who backed President Donald Trump’s false 2020 election claims has resigned from a Justice Department investigation into whether former government officials conspired against the Republican president, a person familiar with the matter said on Monday.

Kurt Olsen's resignation from the so-called “grand conspiracy" probe represents further turmoil in a meandering investigation that has proceeded in fits and starts since its inception as prosecutors pursue an ill-defined right-wing theory of a conspiracy against Trump.

It comes just weeks after the departure of another Trump ally, Joseph diGenova, who had been leading the closely watched investigation in Florida.

The person who confirmed Olsen's resignation spoke on the condition of anonymity to discuss personnel matters. Olsen did not immediately comment on or confirm the news.

Olsen’s prominence as an election denier elevated his profile in the Trump orbit, but his absence of experience as a Justice Department prosecutor made him an unconventional pick for such a high-profile investigation.

Investigators have focused attention on multiple major investigations of Trump, including the probe into Russian interference in the 2016 election and his retention of classified documents at his Mar-a-Lago estate in Florida, and have demanded testimony from officials from both of those probes as they search for any signs of a potential conspiracy to deprive Trump of his rights.

Olsen’s role in the investigation came into focus earlier this month when an attorney for former CIA Director John Brennan revealed that he had discussed with Olsen a subpoena compelling Brennan to testify before the grand jury in Fort Pierce on 25 October.

It's the latest sign of upheaval in the yearlong conspiracy investigation. Last April, a lead national security prosecutor in the Brennan investigation was replaced on the team after raising questions about the strength of the case. Days later, diGenova was sworn in.

The 81-year-old diGenova served as the US attorney for the District of Columbia during the Reagan administration and for years has been a prominent Trump ally, including backing his efforts to overturn his 2020 election loss and publicly and repeatedly claiming that the president was the victim of an intelligence community conspiracy.

DiGenova resigned earlier this month without bringing a single criminal case. It remains unclear whether any will be, or what information has been gathered to support any basis for the idea of a plot against Trump by the government officials who investigated him.

Olsen, who unsuccessfully pushed the Justice Department in 2020 to back the president’s false claims, joined the Justice Department months ago after serving in a White House post as director of election security and integrity.

Olsen helped Texas’ Republican attorney general, Ken Paxton, launch a longshot bid to get the US Supreme Court to overturn Joe Biden’s 2020 win and later represented Kari Lake during her attempt to overturn her loss in the 2022 Arizona governor’s race. Arizona courts sanctioned Olsen for making false statements in that case.

The pontiff appeared to target US President Donald Trump who has routinely referred to concerns from AI leaders about the rapid pace of development of the technology as a ‘hoax’ stemming from his political opponents.
Pope Leo XIV said on Monday that concerns about artificial intelligence going rogue are not “fake news” and should be taken seriously, insisting that safety issues raised by AI experts should be discussed and acted on.

The pontiff, who dedicated his first encyclical to AI and protecting humanity in its wake, was asked during a press conference about what the United States and China especially should be doing to prevent AI from posing catastrophic risks to humanity.

US President Donald Trump has struck down concerns about AI, its rapid pace of development and potential of going rogue, repeatedly, despite calls from top AI firm executives – including Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei and OpenAI’s Sam Altman, downplaying them as a “hoax.”

“This is a problem that, I think, we need to sit down and talk about,” the American pope told reporters onboard a plane back to the Vatican from France, where he had just completed the first apostolic journey to the country in 18 years.

Trump has been a champion of the technology, often warning that the United States must remain ahead of China in the AI arms race. He has said efforts to limit the technology were part of a “conspiracy” by his political opponents.

Several AI giants, including Anthropic, have called for a slowdown in development to give safety measures time to catch up to the fast-moving technology.

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, for example, has warned that within six to 12 months, AI could be capable of leading a swarm of agents that could take over the entire internet.

Trump had a late-night dinner with Amodei on Sunday, during which they discussed Americans' concerns about the technology and how to keep the US dominant, the White House said.

“If someone were to ask me ‘Am I in panic mode?’ No I’m not. I sleep at night,” said the pontiff. “I do think that the concerns raised by many of the experts, specialists in AI should be taken seriously,” he stressed.

“I don’t think that that is ‘fake news’ as some have said to try and cause whether financial or some other kind of benefit,” Leo said.

The pope, who called for robust regulation of AI and a slowdown of the technology in his encyclical “Magnificent Humanity,” is up to speed on developments in the field.

Despite being at the end of a gruelling four-day trip to France, Leo noted a news report on Monday afternoon about Nvidia introducing guardrails into AI. He also noted that the company has previously resisted outside regulation of AI development.

He identified AI as one of the biggest challenges to humanity within days of his election and took the unusual step of personally launching his own encyclical alongside Anthropic's co-founder Christopher Olah at a Vatican event in May.

The initiative was part of the Vatican's effort to engage Silicon Valley about the human costs of AI.

“We need to continue to invite political leaders, leaders of AI, social organisations, associations to come together and look at what is happening already and what could be down the road,” he said on Monday.

“To simply say ‘Oh it’s not going to happen’ and close our eyes to it I think is probably not the most responsible way to go about that,” he added.
