Bilal Faizi, a spokesperson for the provincial emergency service, said the attack had taken place in the district of Dera Ismail Khan in the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province.

The Tehreek-e-Taliban Pakistan (TTP) group, or Pakistani Taliban, has claimed responsibility for the bombing.

Pakistan has seen increasing militant attacks in recent years, with the TTP claiming many of them.

The Counter Extremism Project (CEP) describes the TTP as a "conglomerate" of roughly 40 tribal and non-tribal militant groups primarily fighting against the Pakistani state. The group, founded in late 2007, is allied with the Afghan Taliban, which returned to power in 2021 after overthrowing the US-backed government in Kabul.

According to the CEP, TTP militants regularly carry out attacks against security forces, civilians, and political figures across both Pakistan and Afghanistan.

In Zaporizhzhia Oblast in southeastern Ukraine, two women died and seven people were injured following attacks on the region, regional governor Ivan Fedorov reported on Saturday morning.

"A total of 86 reports were received of damage to infrastructure, residential buildings, and vehicles," he wrote in a post on Telegram.

Two people were also killed and eight were injured in Donetsk in eastern Ukraine, governor Vadym Filashkin said, with 1,121 people evacuated from the front lines.

In Kherson Oblast, Oleksandr Prokudin, the head of the Kherson Regional Military Administration, reported that another person was killed and three people were injured in attacks across the region.

"Russian forces struck critical and social infrastructure, as well as residential neighborhoods in the region’s settlements, damaging five private homes in particular. The occupiers also destroyed private vehicles," Prokudin said.

A 69-year-old man was also killed in a drone strike on the Sumy region in the north of the country, according to local authorities. A further 10 people were wounded in a guided aerial bomb attack on the region.

Russian forces also carried out another wave of strikes on the Ukrainian capital overnight, injuring five people, per Kyiv Mayor Vitali Klitschko.

The Ukrainian air force said early Saturday that Russia had launched 173 attack drones at Ukraine overnight.

"According to preliminary data, as of 7:30 am, air defense forces have shot down or neutralised 134 enemy UAVs of the Shahed and Gerber types, as well as other types of drones," the force said, adding that strikes were recorded at 19 locations.

On Saturday, Ukrainian Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha said Russia had also carried out a "double strike" attack on a logistics facility serving fast-food chain McDonald’s.

"Relentless attacks disrupt Ukrainians’ daily lives, damage businesses, threaten jobs, and undermine international investment," he wrote in a post on social media, while also sharing video footage appearing to show the aftermath of the strikes.

It comes as Russian President Vladimir Putin told reporters in Saint Petersburg that Moscow was not preparing for conflict with Europe, despite warnings from European officials amid an increasing number of suspected Russian hybrid attacks.

"We are not preparing for any kind of hostilities with Europe," the Russian leader said. "There is no real motive to escalate the situation with Europe, no matter what anyone says, no matter who says it, including in the Baltic states".

Sitting on NATO's eastern flank, Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania are particularly vulnerable to security threats stemming from Moscow's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. In recent months, the three Baltic states have seen a rapidly growing number of drone incursions and related incidents, raising fresh concerns over airspace security and prompting Brussels to step up efforts to coordinate Europe's response.

Russia's recruitment drive targeting Belarusians has become a key tool to boost manpower on the battlefield and exploits economic hardship and isolation.
Russia has long regarded Belarus as a convenient reserve for topping up its manpower in the war against Ukraine, especially after suffering heavy losses at the front.

Minsk’s economic dependence, the political vulnerability of Aliaksandr Lukashenka's regime and the legal uncertainty around the Union State create conditions in which the Kremlin can act with considerable freedom.

In the Belarusian corner of the internet, in recent months a large number of adverts offering Belarusians the chance to sign a contract with the Russian army, have appeared. They are circulated everywhere – in Telegram channels, in Viber chats and in local community pages where people discuss everyday issues.

At times it looks almost surreal: nestled between posts about selling vegetables or finding a tutor, a sudden offer appears promising a "high salary, benefits and Russian citizenship" in return for going to war.

Investigations by DW, TVNET, CityDog and Current Time have documented a sharp increase in such posts. The human rights initiative Belpol regularly highlights examples of adverts in which recruiters use Belarusian phone numbers to inspire trust.

On Belpol’s Telegram channel you can see messages promising huge payouts, help with loans, "annulment of criminal convictions" and fast-track Russian citizenship.

On X, the OSB Belpol account posts screenshots of ads in which Belarusians are offered a contract with a one-off payment of 4.35 million roubles and rapid issuance of a Russian passport.

These examples are not isolated incidents but part of a systematic campaign targeting socially vulnerable people.

Belarusians are promised the equivalent of tens of thousands of euros, high salaries, housing, food, a military pension and life insurance.

Some adverts even talk about "sorting out problems with the law," which is particularly dangerous given that participation in an armed conflict abroad falls under the article on mercenary activities in Belarus' criminal code which carries a prison term of up to seven years.

Recruiters insist that the service is "legal," claiming it is not mercenary activity but "service in the army of the Union State."

Research shows that among those recruited, the majority are men from small towns and villages, people on low incomes, with debts or with existing legal problems.

Many do not realise the legal consequences and some are simply looking for quick money, without understanding that they will be sent to the most dangerous sectors of the front.

Human rights activists point out that foreign contract soldiers are often placed in assault units, where casualty rates are particularly high.

The recruitment scheme is simple and effective. A person sees an advert on social media and writes to the recruiter, who is using a Belarusian phone number. They are then told about payments, benefits and the supposed "legality" of the service.

After that they are offered rapid paperwork and travel to Russia – most often via Smolensk or Pskov. There they sign a contract and become a member of the Russian armed forces. At that point there is almost no way back.
