In a TV address, the right-wing leader touted US President Donald Trump's recent suggestion that Washington could reconsider its neutral position on the islands, which have been at the centre of a long running and bloody dispute between Argentina and the United Kingdom.

"President Trump recently stated that the United States is reevaluating its historical position regarding Las Malvinas," Milei said, using the Argentine name for the islands.

"This threat is not to be taken lightly," he continued, adding: "Winds of change are blowing across the world in favor of our cause."

Trump had earlier this week cast doubt on US support for the UK in a potential conflict over the territory, pointing to Britain's lack of backing in the Iran war - which began in late February following US and Israeli strikes on Iran.

"Your country is not doing well," he told GB News. "Don't forget, you get a big percentage of your oil from the Strait of Hormuz, and you weren't there to help me. Your country was not there to help me".

In his speech, Milei also announced that he would be signing off on a series of new sanctions targeting oil companies working off of the islands, which he described as "under British occupation."

"For 50 years, the United Nations has called on Argentina and the United Kingdom to refrain from taking unilateral actions regarding the territory, including the exploitation of its natural resources, until the dispute is resolved. However, the United Kingdom has systematically failed to comply with this call," he said.

He then referenced the British-Israeli Sea Lion oil project, an oilfield roughly 220 km to the north of the Falkland Islands, saying it was due to begin production in the next few months.

"It means that if we do not act firmly and swiftly today, in a few months they will have the material capacity to appropriate the oil reserves lying beneath our sea, something that has never happened before," Milei said. "If we allow this to happen, we will be creating an incentive for the British government to deepen its occupation of the islands and the exploitation of our resources".

In a post on X on Friday morning, British Defence Secretary Wes Streeting reaffirmed the UK's "absolute and unshakeable" commitment to the Falklands.

"The Falklands are British because Falkland Islanders choose to be British," he wrote, adding that Milei's statement showed "more about domestic politics in Argentina than it does about the Falkland Islands."

Sovereignty over the islands has long been a divisive and highly politicised issue, particularly following the 10-week war of 1982, which began after Argentine forces invaded the territory.

Then-British Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher launched a naval task force to reclaim the islands, and the conflict ended with Argentina's surrender in June of that year.

In 2013, Falkland Islanders voted overwhelmingly to remain British following a referendum. Of the more than 1,500 votes that came in, over 99% were in favour, with three votes against.

Nepal Army images show two hydropower workers rescued alive after nine days trapped inside a flood-buried tunnel in Rasuwa.

Nepal Army handout images show the rescue of two hydropower workers who survived for nine days inside a tunnel buried by mud and debris after devastating floods swept through the country’s Trishuli Valley.

The workers, identified as Sanjay Sah and Kabir Maharjan, were found about 170 metres inside the Trishuli 3A hydropower tunnel in Rasuwa district. Rescuers heard voices before reaching them and brought both men out alive on Friday. They were taken to hospital for treatment.

The rescue offers fresh hope for others still missing from hydropower sites. Hundreds of workers are believed to remain trapped in tunnels blocked by flood debris, while teams continue clearing entrances and searching underground.

The USS Abraham Lincoln's arrival has drawn global media interest after reports of deteriorating living conditions on board after more than 280 days at sea.
Two sailors from a US aircraft carrier making a port call to Thailand were sent back to the ship after a drunken altercation, Thai police said on Friday.

Police were alerted to a "small scuffle" involving the sailors in the early hours of Thursday near Pattaya's nightlife hotspot Walking Street, city police chief Colonel Anek Srathongyoo told the AFP news agency.

He described the incident as "trivial" and said local residents had only called police to prevent it from escalating.

The unidentified sailors were not charged as "they did not violate any Thai law," Anek said.

The USS Abraham Lincoln's arrival has drawn global media interest after reports of deteriorating living conditions on board after more than 280 days at sea.

It has also put the spotlight on Pattaya, a resort popular with foreign tourists with a reputation for its nightlife, including sex tourism.

Authorities launched a crackdown on sex workers ahead of the ship's visit, although they denied it was linked to the sailors' arrival.

The police chief told AFP there had been no other notable incidents since the Lincoln docked, and one Pattaya masseuse said it had been calmer than previous military port calls.

Nattakamol Chartmontri, who has worked in Pattaya for several years, told AFP many of the sailors appeared "young, well-behaved and rule-abiding."

Pattaya businesses had been hoping for a windfall from the visit, which comes during low season, but Nattakamol, 48, said it had so far stayed quiet.

"People on social media are so hyped about (the sailors), but actually, they have not made a significant difference to Pattaya businesses," she said.

US Democratic lawmakers called for an investigation into conditions on the Lincoln last month following reports of food shortages, broken plumbing and mental health crises, including attempted suicides.

President Donald Trump dismissed the concerns, which have fuelled criticism of his dragging war on Iran, although the Lincoln was replaced in the Middle East by the USS George Washington.

About 900 workers are missing from 12 hydropower projects in Nepal, with roughly 500 believed to be trapped in tunnels, according to local authorities.
Rescuers in Nepal pulled two workers alive from a hydropower tunnel on Friday as emergency teams continued an operation to reach others believed to be trapped inside, officials said.

The two men were rescued from the Trishuli 3A tunnel nine days after floods, caused by a glacier collapse, devastated towns and swept away villages in Nepal on 26 August.

Nepalese lawmaker Shri Ram Neupane posted about the second rescue on his Facebook page shortly after army spokesperson Brigadier General Raja Ram Basnet confirmed the first trapped worker’s rescue.

The men were named as Sanjay Shah, 30, a mechanical foreman, and Kabir Maharjan, 45, a mechanical supervisor, by Home Minister Sudan Gurung.

Local television news channels showed images of the first rescued worker covered in mud and being carried from the site on a rescuer’s shoulder.

Officials said both men were in stable condition and were flown to a hospital in the capital Kathmandu.

At least 557 workers are missing from the Trishuli 3A hydropower project along the Trishuli River, with about 115 believed to be trapped in the main tunnel, according to the Independent Power Producers Association of Nepal.

Officials present at the rescue site said rescuers believe some people may still be alive inside the tunnel.

Nepal’s Communication Minister Bikram Timilsina said officials remain hopeful that more workers will be rescued.

“We are waiting for the rescue of more and more people,” Timilsina wrote on his Facebook page.

About 900 workers are missing from 12 hydropower projects in Nepal, with roughly 500 believed to be trapped in tunnels, according to local authorities.

Australian rescue expert Arnold Dix, who has advised on multiple tunnel-collapse rescues, has been supporting the operation remotely and is travelling to Nepal to support teams on the ground.

Earlier on Friday, Dix, president of the International Tunnelling and Underground Space Association, said teams at the Trishuli 3A hydropower project believed they had heard a faint response from inside the tunnel.

"They believe they received a faint response from inside saying, 'hunchha', yes or OK," Dix told the AFP news agency, adding that he was en route to Nepal to join the rescue effort.

"Right now, I'm 'glass three-quarters full'...After a week of 24/7 heartache, we might, just, be back in miracle country," he said.

Dix said teams had dug through debris blocking the tunnel for 50 to 60 metres (164-196 feet) and had been hoping for a breakthrough at any moment.

The Trump administration says its proposed legislation aims to make university equal and accessible to all and end “woke” campaigns which deliberately favour certain students over others due to socioeconomic or racial backgrounds.
The Trump administration is proposing a new rule that would strip private schools and colleges of their tax-exempt status if they provide targeted help to students based on their race, a significant escalation in the White House's campaign to eradicate diversity programs directed at Black, Hispanic and other minority students.

The US Treasury Department proposed the change on Thursday in a new regulation that, if made final, would kick in after May 2027 with the conclusion of the academic year.

The rule is broadly aimed at ending any policies or programmes that help students because of their race, and it specifically says such benefits in admissions, scholarships and facilities “would be incompatible” with the rule.

It’s the latest attempt by the Trump administration to pressure schools and colleges to weed out diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) policies that had become common before President Donald Trump returned to the White House with a promise to end them.

Trump officials have used Civil Rights-era laws to unwind the policies, saying they discriminate against white and Asian American students. Higher education leaders criticized the proposal.

“The administration’s latest rules changes are its most blatant attack to keep working class Americans and people of colour from accessing higher education and a better life,” said Mike Gavin, President and CEO of the Alliance for Higher Education.

“By claiming that efforts to increase fair opportunity for all students are discriminatory, the administration is trying to gaslight the American people into believing that up is down and black is white.”

Scores of universities have shut down or rebranded their DEI offices and ended scholarships and clubs designed for minority students under pressure from the White House.

In a statement announcing the proposal, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent suggested that even policies that are no longer under the banner of DEI could be targeted.

“Schools rebranding race-based preferences as equitable, inclusive, or diversity-enhancing does not change their discriminatory nature,” Bessent wrote in a post on X announcing the proposed legislation.

The Treasury Department and Internal Revenue Service (IRS) estimate that up to 18,000 private schools, colleges and other education institutions could be affected by the proposal.

US private universities have been exempt from many taxes for more than a century because they provide a public good. The benefit saves many universities millions of dollars every year.

Trump has seen the tax-exempt status as a lever to pull in his pressure campaign against colleges that he describes as hotbeds of “wokeness” and “radical left ideology”.

He previously threatened to cut the benefit for Harvard University last year during his battle with the nation’s oldest college. In a response, Harvard officials said there was no legal basis for doing so and argued it would force cuts to financial aid and crucial medical research.

It’s incredibly rare for the federal government to go after a college’s tax-exempt status, but there’s one notable precedent. Bob Jones University, a small Christian school in South Carolina, lost the benefit in the 1970s over a ban on interracial dating and marriage on campus.

The Supreme Court upheld the IRS’s decision to deny the school its exemption. The school has since ended the ban and regained tax-exempt status in 2017. Laws forbid the IRS from targeting individuals and organisations for ideological reasons, and federal officials are not allowed to direct IRS investigations.

Oil prices again spiked this week after a few weeks of relative stability following the renewed fighting as Iran continues to maintain a stranglehold over the strategic Strait of Hormuz, responsible for roughly 20% of global oil shipping.
Iran said it hit US military bases in Kuwait and the UAE on Thursday, vowing to avenge a wedding attack it accused Washington of carrying out as US Vice President JD Vance said the strike was being investigated.

The new clashes, which began with US raids on Sunday, have deepened the impasse in the six-month war, as Iran maintains a stranglehold on the strategic Strait of Hormuz and Washington presses its counter-blockade of Iranian ports.

Kuwait's army said on Thursday it intercepted a missile and drone attack from Tehran, with the Iranian army reporting that it hit US assets at the Ahmad al-Jaber Air Base "with missiles and drones". Kuwait called the strikes a "grave breach of the rules of international law".

Iran's army also said it struck Al Minhad Air Base in the United Arab Emirates, but the Gulf country reported no such attack.

The strikes came after the Iranian Red Crescent said a missile attack on a wedding in the coastal town of Kuhestak killed at least four people on Tuesday night, including a child, and wounded more than 50 other people attending the celebrations.

Vance said Washington was probing the alleged strike but cast doubt on reports out of Iran.

"I know that we're investigating," he told a White House briefing. "I will say that the Iranian state media has not been a very good scribe about what's happened in the conflict thus far. So I'm extremely sceptical of this."

Iran's Revolutionary Guards (IRGC) chief Ahmad Vahidi vowed on Thursday to avenge the victims of the attack, saying "the pure blood of these oppressed martyrs will not go unanswered.”

US Central Command (CENTCOM) spokesman Tim Hawkins said "the US military never targets civilians, unlike the IRGC.” The incident is similar to a previous attack in the early days of the war where more than 120 school children were killed in an attack on a girls’ school, initially denied by the US, but later confirmed after investigations.

Extensive US bombardment of sites across Iran killed at least 19 people over the past week after President Donald Trump said his military was prepared to attack Iran at "any time”.

An Iranian state-run news agency added on Thursday that six members of the Iranian army's naval forces were killed by US attacks this week, but it was not immediately clear if the six were among the 19 dead.

Tehran retaliated with strikes across the Middle East this week, saying it targeted US bases and forces in Washington-allied Gulf Arab countries, including the UAE, Kuwait, Bahrain and also Jordan and Iraq's Kurdistan region.

Vance said however that he "wouldn't call it a war," claiming that "right now, there is no active shooting,” instead describing occasional bouts of exchanges as “flare-ups” but maintaining that the active firing phase only lasted roughly six weeks.

The war erupted on February 28 with US-Israeli attacks on Iran that killed its former Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei in the opening salvo of the war.

A fragile ceasefire collapsed in July after attacks on commercial ships in the Strait of Hormuz resumed, through which one-fifth of world oil supplies previously flowed.
