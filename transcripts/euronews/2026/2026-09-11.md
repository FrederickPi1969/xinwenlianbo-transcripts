Russian President Vladimir Putin has arrived in New Delhi ahead of a meeting of BRICS group leaders, with global conflicts and economic challenges likely to set the tone for the weekend.

Putin landed in the Indian capital on Friday, where he was greeted by Indian Minister of External Affairs Kirti Vardhan Singh and a welcome party flanked by photographers.

The two-day summit is expected to see the 11-member organisation focus on the wars in Ukraine and the Middle East, as well as oil and global trade.

Chinese President Xi Jinping is also set to attend from Saturday to Sunday, in what would be his first visit to India since 2019.

"It is fully expected that the conference will feature positive and meaningful discussions on various issues, conducted with a view to global welfare," Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi wrote in a post on social media on Friday.

"With the virtuous, one may debate and share friendship; with the unvirtuous, one should have no dealings whatsoever," he added.

Modi is scheduled to hold talks with Putin later on Friday as the Indian leader looks to build on one of New Delhi's most important strategic relationships.

Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian is also due to arrive on Friday amid the ongoing Iran war.

Harsh V. Pant of India's Observer Research Foundation said the Gulf conflict would likely be "the central fault line this time during the BRICS summit."

"What was seen until last year as an achievement -- the expansion of BRICS -- today looks like a big constraint on BRICS," Pant added.

The BRICS group markets itself as a forum for 11 "emerging markets and developing countries" - Brazil, China, Egypt, Ethiopia, India, Indonesia, Iran, Russia, Saudi Arabia, South Africa and the United Arab Emirates.

The first BRICS summit was held in 2009 in Yekaterinburg, Russia. The group has since expanded and also now includes 10 "partner" nations, namely Belarus, Bolivia, Cuba, Kazakhstan, Malaysia, Nigeria, Thailand, Uganda, Uzbekistan, and Vietnam.

Houthi fighters reached Perim Island which divides the Bab el-Mandeb into two shipping lanes after government forces withdrew, completing the group's takeover of the strait. Together with Iran's Hormuz blockade, this puts roughly a third of the world's seaborne trade under hostile control.
Yemen's Iran-backed Houthis took control of the Bab el-Mandeb strait on Friday, after capturing an island located in the middle of the crucial Red Sea waterway, a local government official and eyewitnesses said.

"Boats carrying armed Houthi fighters reached Mayyun Island after government forces withdrew from it yesterday," the official said, referring to a rocky volcanic island also known as Perim that divides the narrowest part of the strait into two shipping lanes.

"The Houthis have completed their takeover of the Bab el-Mandeb area and Mayyun Island, which is located in the middle of the strait," he added, speaking on condition of anonymity.

An eyewitness said gunmen were "deploying along the Bab el-Mandeb shore and are driving around in military vehicles."

On Thursday, Houthis captured the strategic port city of Mokha from government forces in a blitz offensive that also saw them taking control of Zuqar Island in the southern Red Sea "after rocket attacks and a ground assault carried out using boats transporting fighters," according to military sources.

Footage appeared online of Houthi fighters' apparent celebrations in Mokha and their seizure of considerable weapons and equipment left behind by government forces. Euronews could not verify the authenticity of the footage.

The Houthis, who controlled about one-third of Yemen's territory prior to the offensive, have previously targeted shipping in the narrow waterway which links Asia to Europe via the Red Sea and Suez Canal.

Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb account for more than one quarter of seaborne crude oil and petroleum trade. Nearly one-third of the world's container traffic passes through the Red Sea alone.

The Houthis have also reportedly consulted with Tehran on imposing a safe passage toll through the strait over the summer, in an apparent plot to further strain global economies.

Yemen's internationally recognised government has repeatedly warned of Houthi plans for taking control of the waterway, whose name in Arabic means "Gate of Tears".

Formally known as Ansar Allah, the Houthis came to power in late 2014 after storming Sana'a and seizing the presidential palace and key military sites, forcing Yemen's government into exile.

The group is part of Tehran's so-called Axis of Resistance — a loose network of militant groups in several countries in the region including Hezbollah in Lebanon, Hamas in Gaza and militias in Iraq, all backed, trained and equipped by the IRGC to varying degrees.

The group triggered a return to hostilities in Yemen's civil war in July, when they let an Iranian plane land in defiance of Saudi control of Yemeni airspace.

Riyadh retaliated by striking the airport, triggering the Houthis to declare a blockade on the kingdom targeting its oil trade in July.

The Houthis engaged in a large-scale offensive last week against government forces backed by Saudi Arabia, while also launching attacks on Saudi territory that left dozens wounded, leading Riyadh to respond with daily strikes on Houthi-held parts of Yemen.

Saudi Arabia, which earlier this week said it had always offered “opportunities for negotiation,” also said it has the right to defend itself.

A major fire broke out early Thursday at the Marché du Soleil in Marseille, a large market near Saint-Charles station known for selling counterfeit goods.

Flames tore through around 2,000 square metres of the 3,600-square-metre site. Marseille marine firefighters deployed 110 personnel and 35 vehicles to tackle the blaze.

Nearby residents were evacuated during the operation, but no injuries were reported. The market had been closed since February and recently ordered by a court to shut down permanently following concerns over fire safety and compliance with regulations.

Two Azerbaijani sailors were killed when a tugboat was struck by drones in the Black Sea on Tuesday, in the latest attack on civilian shipping near the Ukrainian port of Odesa.

The Somali-flagged tugboat TEDY, owned by Mert Marine Corporation, was first hit near Odesa before coming under two further drone attacks overnight as it sailed toward the Romanian port of Constanta.

Azerbaijan's Foreign Ministry confirmed the deaths of crew members Aslan Aliyev and Asim Gasimov. Two other Azerbaijani sailors, Imran Novruzov and Rauf Aliyev, were hospitalised in the Ukrainian city of Chornomorsk with burn injuries. Six further crew members received treatment at the scene.

The vessel carried 10 crew members in total, including Azerbaijani, Turkish, German and Ukrainian nationals.

Azerbaijan's Foreign Ministry said it was coordinating with diplomatic missions and relevant authorities and was taking measures to repatriate the injured and carry out procedures relating to the deceased. It did not attribute responsibility for the attack.

Ukraine's Ministry for Restoration, Infrastructure and Transport said Russia had carried out the attack, describing it as part of a sustained campaign against civilian vessels and port infrastructure.

Russia's Defence Ministry said on Wednesday it had struck two cargo vessels heading to Odesa and a tugboat, claiming the ships were carrying military supplies. Kyiv did not comment on the Russian claim.

Chornomorsk is a major Ukrainian commercial port on the Black Sea and one of the principal terminals for Ukrainian grain exports.

Following Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in early 2022, Kyiv and Moscow negotiated the Black Sea Grain Initiative, which allowed Ukrainian agricultural exports to resume under UN and Turkish supervision before Russia withdrew from the agreement in July 2023.

Attacks on civilian shipping in the Black Sea have intensified since July, when multiple merchant vessels flying the flags of third countries have been struck, raising concern about the risk to seafarers.

The attack on the TEDY is the latest in a series of incidents involving Azerbaijani sailors. Five Azerbaijani citizens were killed in attacks on two vessels in the Sea of Azov in June.

Prices for basic medicines such as paracetamol and insulin have skyrocketed amid major shortages of less common pharmaceuticals. More than 70% of healthcare costs in Iran are paid out of pocket, leaving the most vulnerable patients unable to afford treatment.
Iran's pharmaceutical sector is facing shortages of hundreds of medicines, a near-six-fold price hike for basic drugs and collapsing supply chains, as the war with the US enters its seventh month and the effects of sanctions and a naval blockade accumulate.

The shortages include roughly 90 medicines classified as essential or life-saving, according to Iranian media.

Hadi Ahmadi, spokesperson for the Iranian Pharmacists Association, told domestic media last week that around 400 of the scarce medicines are produced domestically — evidence that local manufacturing has not insulated the sector from disruption, because many domestic producers rely on imported raw materials.

Ruhollah Lak Aliabadi, a member of parliament's health commission, told Tabnak news site that access problems for imported inputs were feeding directly into production and distribution failures.

Vahideh Mahmoudkhani, head of the health department at Iran's trade centre in Shanghai, told the newspaper Hamshahri that the war had completely shut down some supply routes and made others significantly longer.

Medicines and humanitarian goods are typically exempt from direct sanctions under international law.

But banking restrictions, difficulties transferring foreign currency and blockade-related transport disruption can prevent Iranian companies from purchasing drugs and raw materials abroad regardless of their formal legal status.

The US naval blockade of Iranian ports and cargo vessels imposed in April was briefly lifted following the framework agreement before being reinstated after talks collapsed.

Washington launched what it called Operation Economic Outcast in late August, designed to further restrict Tehran's access to international financial networks.

Mehdi Pirsalehi, head of Iran's Food and Drug Organisation, said on Tuesday that 44 companies in the medicines and medical equipment sector had been targeted in attacks since the war began, with staff killed and wounded.

He made the remarks at the opening of a pharmaceutical exhibition in Tehran, while stressing that two companies struck directly by several missiles were present at the event and had resumed production.

One of those companies was Tofigh Daru. In late March, Israeli officials announced they had struck it because of what they said was its role in supplying fentanyl to a research body within Iran's defence ministry.

Tofigh Daru rejected the allegation, saying it produced only fentanyl citrate in limited quantities and exclusively for authorised medical uses including surgical anaesthesia.

The physical shortage of medicines is compounded by price rises that are pricing patients out of treatment.

Mehr state news agency has reported that gabapentin — used to treat epilepsy and nerve pain — rose 220% in price over a year.

Paracetamol rose 375%, amoxicillin 285% and fluoxetine 100%. Insulin, which diabetic patients require daily, has in some cases reached six times last year's price.

Nearly 3,000 American flags cover the lawn at Pepperdine University in Malibu, California, for the annual “Wave of Flags” commemoration of the September 11 attacks.

During a tour of Doha’s Hamad International Airport, Qatar Airways’ CEO and management discussed the airline’s recovery from regional disruption while preparing for the winter season, new aircraft and next generation of Qsuites.
Qatar Airways is preparing for the busy winter travel season and planning the next phase of its growth. After months of regional disruptions, including the temporary suspension of flights in February, the Doha-based airline has had to adapt its operations to offer passenger support, expand its routes and remain resilient.

And now, Hamad Ali Al-Khater, Chief Executive Officer at Qatar Airways Group, said that “passenger numbers have recovered to around 90%-95% of last year’s levels”, with Hamad International Airport currently handling more than 140,000 passengers a day as international carriers gradually return to Doha. This includes British Airways, which resumed daily direct flights from London Heathrow to the Qatari capital earlier this month.

Al-Khater was speaking during a behind-the-scenes tour at Hamad International Airport. Here, Euronews Travel was given in-depth access into the teams and facilities that have kept the airline’s operations running through a period of regional and international disruption, as well as preparing for the winter season.

Qatar Airways is also planning for the long-term. Al-Khater said the airline is set to receive new long-range aircraft by the end of the year with a new cabin configuration. This will allow the carrier to “reach destinations further away than our existing narrow-body aircraft can serve”.

He added: “That will open up opportunities to fly to other cities and what we call secondary airports, which may previously have been difficult to serve economically.”

Over the next five years, 138 new aircraft are also slated to be added to the Qatar Airways fleet. Travellers can expect a new generation of the airline’s Qsuite business class product, which Al-Khater said is due to be unveiled in summer 2027.

Behind the scenes, returning to normal levels of operation, and preparing for the winter travel rush, involves a vast operation, spanning aircraft maintenance, customer service, ground operations and inflight offerings.

During the 2026-2027 winter season, Qatar Airways will expand its global network to more than 170 destinations. On the horizon are 186 weekly flights across Africa, Asia Pacific, Europe, the Americas and the Middle East.

In Europe, the airline is set to increase its London Heathrow service from 49 to 56 weekly flights starting on 25 October. While flights to Frankfurt will increase from 18 to 21 weekly flights from 7 December.

“In terms of winter preparation, [this] is a normal exercise for Qatar Airways,” Ali AlSaadi, chief maintenance, repair and overhaul officer (MRO), told Euronews Travel.

“We understand that the traffic pressure will increase [during this time], so, we ensure that our aircraft need to be utilised to the highest level.”

With aircraft and cabin maintenance beginning during the pre-winter season, the carrier’s large MRO centre oversees the testing and enhancement of its fleet, as well as upgrades to its technical capabilities. Here, Euronews Travel was given a closer look at one of the aircraft undergoing maintenance as part of Qatar Airways’ continued rollout of Starlink connectivity.

In August, Qatar Airways became the first airline to operate a Starlink-equipped Boeing 787-9 aircraft. In total, it operates 150 Starlink-equipped widebody aircraft, which is 83% of its connectivity ambitions.

In addition to the new winter schedule, Qatar Airways also announced earlier this year that it would operate four weekly flights to Helsinki in Finland starting from July, and ramped that number up to seven by August.

The carrier also expanded its South American network in July, launching flights to Bogotá, Colombia, and Caracas, Venezuela. The new service made Qatar Airways the first Gulf carrier to serve Venezuela and the first airline to connect the Middle East with Caracas and Bogotá.

While at Hamad International Airport, Euronews Travel also visited the airport’s integrated operation centre, where flight operations and navigation services professionals outlined how the airline adapted to ongoing disruptions.

Chalk artists create portraits of the 40 Flight 93 passengers and crew at the Shanksville memorial ahead of the 25th anniversary of 9/11.
… More
Chalk artists have gathered at the Flight 93 National Memorial in Shanksville, Pennsylvania, to draw portraits of the 40 passengers and crew members killed on September 11, 2001.

The group of 20 artists is creating the portraits ahead of the 25th anniversary of the attacks, with each drawing offering a personal tribute to those who died aboard United Flight 93.

Among the works is a portrait of one of the passengers remembered for taking part in the attempt to regain control of the hijacked plane. Another artist is drawing the youngest person aboard the flight, saying the work has a strong personal meaning.

The chalk portraits are temporary and will gradually fade while on display at the memorial.

A new Ipsos poll finds that roughly half of US adults, unprompted, name 9/11 among the most significant events of their lifetime — ahead of the Covid-19 pandemic, the elections of Barack Obama and Donald Trump, and Hurricane Katrina of 2005.

Nine in ten say the attacks changed the country "a great deal" or "quite a bit," and most Americans can still recall exactly where they were when they heard the news.

Yet despite that lasting wound, only around one in ten plan to actively mark Friday's anniversary - for those that do a moment of silence and the flying of the flag are the most common gestures.
