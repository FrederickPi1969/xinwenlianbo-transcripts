Dutch police clashed with around 500 far-right protesters during a rally against immigration and asylum policies in The Hague.
… More
A far-right demonstration in The Hague descended into violence on Saturday as protesters clashed with Dutch police.

Around 500 people took part in the “We Are The People” rally, which was called to protest government policies, particularly on immigration.

Police officers were deployed in large numbers as protesters threw fireworks and other projectiles. Mounted police detained several people, while water cannons were briefly used to disperse the crowd.

After the explosion, the flight tracking site Flightradar24 said the airport was experiencing "major problems." A journalist for the AFP news agency also reported seeing firefighters working to extinguish a burning fuel truck close to the site.

Saudi authorities said there were no casualties or damage and did not say what prompted the alerts, but the Saudi Civil Defence had been sending messages to residents in several parts of the country overnight, warning of potential danger and advising people to remain indoors and away from windows.

The Houthis later claimed, without providing evidence, that they had targeted “sensitive sites” in Riyadh and facilities belonging to Saudi oil giant Aramco in Yanbu. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree said cruise and ballistic missiles, as well as drones, were used. He also claimed Saudi Arabia had attempted to target the Houthi-held capital Sanaa, without providing details.

It comes amid an uptick in attacks from Yemen's Houthi rebels, who declared a naval blockade of Saudi Arabia in July.

The Houthis, whom the US has designated as a Foreign Terrorist Organisation, have since been increasingly targeting Saudi cities and shipping, further heightening tensions in the Middle East - which is already reeling under the effects of the Iran war and tensions surrounding the Strait of Hormuz.

Saudi Arabia has hit back with its own strikes on the group - which has seized vast stretches of territory in Yemen this month in a lightning offensive - but has failed to stop its advance.

“Developments around the Bab al-Mandab Strait underline the urgent need to prevent Yemen’s conflict from further threatening the security of the Red Sea and this vital international maritime route,” Khaled Khiari, the UN assistant secretary-general for the Middle East, Europe, the Americas, Asia and the Pacific, said earlier this week.

“What is needed now is sustained engagement that reduces tensions, prevents further escalation and safeguards freedom of navigation," he added.

Tehran split into two worlds: unveiled women ran openly in a city marathon while state forces paraded machine guns nearby. The striking contrast exposes a deeply divided capital, caught between defiance and regime mobilisation amid rising warfare.
Tehran saw two very different scenes unfold at the same time on Friday, as in one part of the city, many women raced a marathon without a headscarf, and in another part, a regime military display took place with thousands of women in hijab showing their loyalty with machine guns in their hands.

The state-organised rally brought together 300,000 supporters of the Islamic Republic. The "Jan-Fada" (Self-Sacrificers) marched from Imam Hossein Square toward central Tehran. Among these, the image of a woman in a full chador with a baby wrapped in a keffiyeh perched behind a machine gun stood out as particularly striking.

On the other hand, female runners joined a sporting event and competed in a track-and-field race. During the event, women whose clothing, according to published images, did not comply with Iran’s mandatory hijab rules were featured.

The Tehran 10-kilometre marathon was held at Velayat Park, with men and women competing in separate divisions.

According to the official schedule, the women’s race started at 5:30 AM, followed by the men’s at 7:00 AM. Published photos showed some female participants running without headscarves and wearing standard athletic gear, including long-sleeve tops and track pants.

This event proceeded despite the cancellation of the "Mahan Run" in the city of Kerman just days earlier, which had been scheduled for 5km, 10km, and 21km distances.

Kerman's seminary had demanded the cancellation, citing co-ed participation and concerns over "religious values", ultimately leading authorities to withhold a permit.

The procession moved from Imam Hossein Square toward the city centre, showcasing military hardware, drones, and organised Basij units.

This trend has continued despite the two recent wars involving Iran against Israel and the US.

Russia was added in May to the UN Secretary-General's list of countries credibly suspected of committing sexual violence in conflict — codified into international law decades ago as war crimes and crimes against humanity.
More than 1,000 cases of sexual violence recorded since Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 involve victims as young as 4 and as old as 82 in a "shocking pattern of torture, intimidation and coercion," according to the report published on Friday by the UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights.

"I am deeply alarmed by the prevalence of these abhorrent acts documented in our report, and fear that they are almost certainly continuing amid the unceasing violence we are witnessing every day in Ukraine," said UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk.

Both crimes were committed by Russian troops, who account for 89% of perpetrators in the 1,004 cases included in the report.

While the majority of documented victims are men, particularly those held in places of detention, women and children have been most severely affected in occupied Ukrainian territory.

Crimes against Ukrainian prisoners of war and detained medical workers include gang rape, genital mutilation and other acts of sexual humiliation.

The most common single method recorded was the use of a Soviet-era military field telephone known as a "tapik" to administer electric shocks to the genitals: 155 cases, or 27% of the total, involved this form of abuse at least once, the report says.

Individual survivor testimonies documented in the report include Ukrainian prisoners of war sexually assaulted with objects such as batons, cell door keys or shovel handles.

A woman in Zaporizhzhia was raped several times a week for a month by a Russian soldier who promised better treatment for her detained husband. A woman in Kherson was assaulted nightly and repeatedly threatened with gang rape.

In the Kyiv region, two Russian soldiers broke into a family home in 2022, shot and killed a woman's husband, then gang-raped her, threatening to shoot her in front of her four-year-old son if she resisted.

"Sexual violence is a grave feature of the full-scale armed attack against Ukraine. It must stop immediately, irrespective of the perpetrators," Türk said.

"Given the high prevalence of cases attributed to its forces, however, the Russian Federation in particular must take concrete and discernible steps to protect civilians and prisoners of war from all forms of torture, including sexual violence."

The 20-page report covers the period from 24 February 2022 to the end of July 2026 and draws on confidential interviews with hundreds of victims, along with court documents and official records.

Of the 11% of cases attributed to Ukrainian forces, nearly half involved threats of sexual violence against Russian prisoners of war and prisoners of war from third countries. The remainder included forced nudity, electric shocks and beatings to the genitals, sexually degrading treatment and two cases each of sexual assault and attempted rape.

Russia was added to the UN Secretary-General's list of parties credibly suspected of committing sexual violence in conflict in May of this year — a list that includes Sudan, Somalia and Haiti as repeat offenders. The Kremlin rejected the findings as politically motivated.

The UN Human Rights Council's Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine has separately documented widespread use of torture by Russian authorities against Ukrainian civilians and prisoners of war in occupied territory and inside Russia.

Russia formally withdrew from the European Convention for the Prevention of Torture in September 2025 following its expulsion from the Council of Europe, ending visits by European monitors to Russian detention facilities.

French President Emmanuel Macron is travelling to Saint Pierre and Miquelon this weekend, an archipelago the Élysée describes as "central to today's France."
French President Emmanuel Macron is travelling to Saint Pierre and Miquelon this weekend to discuss the future of the archipelago, as well as France's geostrategic presence in the North Atlantic, maritime sovereignty and cooperation with Ottawa.

The last French president to make a trip to the overseas collectivity, which is located some 20 kilometres off the Canadian province of Newfoundland and Labrador, was François Hollande back in December 2014.

Eleven years on, Macron will be there on Sunday alongside Mark Carney, the Canadian prime minister.

Their meeting comes amid Carney's apparently blossoming relationship with Brussels and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen's proposal to make Canada an associate member of the EU.

The key goal of the trip is to "deliver a message (...) about France's presence across five oceans," the French presidency said during a telephone briefing with the media, including Euronews. France has a presence in the Atlantic, Indian and Pacific oceans, as well as "in the Arctic, through Saint Pierre and Miquelon" and in the Southern Ocean.

Located at the gateway to the Arctic, Saint Pierre and Miquelon gives France a significant exclusive economic zone and allows it to maintain a military presence there.

That presence is partly provided by the Fulmar, a navy patrol vessel and the only French military ship permanently based in the archipelago, which is due for replacement.

Saint Pierre and Miquelon is not a peripheral outpost, the Élysée stresses, but "a territory, an archipelago that is central to today's France."

During the presidential visit, questions of sovereignty will also be discussed in relation to digital cables and connections linking the archipelago and the European continent, as well as space-related activities, where further investment expected.

Another item on the agenda will be regional cooperation. Paris wants to turn Saint Pierre and Miquelon into a "rear base for polar cooperation," working not only with Canada but also with Denmark and Greenland.

The president is also expected to focus on the day-to-day difficulties faced by residents.

Saint Pierre and Miquelon is grappling with economic and social problems similar to those seen in many isolated territories around the world while also experiencing the effects of climate change.

The archipelago's population is also declining. The biggest blow came with the collapse of the fishing industry following years of a Canadian moratorium on cod fishing. France also saw its exclusive economic zone there reduced following a court ruling in 1992.

Another threat facing the territory is rising sea levels. The village of Miquelon-Langlade, located on a narrow strip of sand just two metres above sea level, is being moved farther inland. It is the first relocation project carried out in France in response to the effects of global warming.

Paris supports closer ties between Brussels and Ottawa and has reiterated its willingness to work with all countries that "share our values and want to strengthen their ability to decide, produce and act for themselves."

France is looking to Canada to help secure European supplies of hydrocarbons, at a time when fuel prices are soaring due to global conflicts and tensions surrounding the Strait of Hormuz.

La Paz hopes the deal, awaiting final authorisation by the IMF’s executive board, will open other lines of finances from different institutions, like the World Bank, as the country works to restore its struggling credit status.
Bolivian lawmakers approved a $1.9 billion (€1.65bn) loan agreement with the International Monetary Fund on Friday, delivering the conservative government a key victory in its efforts to ease the country's deep economic crisis as unions threatened to renew protests.

Just hours after Congress approved the loan, President Rodrigo Paz announced an immediate end to subsidies for the diesel powering Bolivia’s trucks, buses and tractors — a step towards meeting IMF demands.

Gasoline, used mainly in private cars, would remain subsidised for now, though Paz had already scaled back that support in recent months.

The Bolivian Senate ratified the IMF agreement a day after the lower house approved it, clearing the final legislative hurdle for the three-year financing programme aimed at replenishing dwindling foreign reserves and stabilising an ailing economy marked by high inflation and weak growth.

The IMF first announced the staff-level agreement in July after months of negotiations with Paz’s market-friendly government, which took power last year after nearly two decades of socialist rule as part of a wave of new Latin American leaders allied with the Trump administration.

The programme still requires approval from the IMF’s executive board before funds can be disbursed. Economy Minister Christian Morales told senators that the deal would give other lenders, including the World Bank and the Inter-American Development Bank, greater confidence in the government and help it secure about $5 billion in additional financing.

But the assistance is conditioned on tough economic measures, including the elimination of fuel subsidies, that threaten to reignite unrest in Bolivia, where weeks of road blockades in June and July paralysed much of the South American nation as protesters demanded Paz’s resignation.

Congress on Thursday extended for another 90-days a state of emergency that Paz had declared to clear roads during the protests. It allows for military intervention and the suspension of some civil liberties to quell unrest.

The Bolivian Workers’ Central, the country’s main labour federation, and other unions have voiced fierce opposition to the IMF loan, warning that the government spending cuts required under the deal would drive up living costs and deepen hardship for low-income and struggling families.

Although Paz’s Christian Democratic Party lacks a majority in Congress, the centrist and right-wing lawmakers that dominate both chambers rallied behind the deal.

The Movement Toward Socialism, the party that dominated Bolivian politics after the former coca growers’ union leader Evo Morales won the presidency in 2005, now holds just two of the 130 seats in the lower house and none in the 36-member Senate.

Declining natural gas exports have deprived Bolivia of billions of dollars it had relied on previously to import gasoline and diesel, contributing to chronic fuel shortages that began in 2023 and have persisted under Paz.

The Iran war dramatically surging global oil prices have made fuel subsidies even more burdensome and dug the country into a deeper financial hole.

“No one can buy something expensive and sell it cheap,” Paz said in his late-night declaration that diesel in Bolivia would now be sold at international prices.

To cushion the blow, he announced about $79 million (€68.8m) in cash assistance for approximately 2.9 million Bolivians, along with loans on preferential terms for truckers, small businesses and producers facing the new higher diesel costs.

Paz also promised the change would end the country’s persistent diesel shortages, which have disrupted harvests and delayed deliveries of imported goods.

The bill received widespread opposition from Democrat lawmakers, who favoured tougher sanctions on Russia, but opposed provisions in the draft which would enable Trump to impose tariffs freely on nations that continue to buy large amounts of Russian energy.
US President Donald Trump signed into law a sweeping sanctions package targeting Russian officials and key pillars of its economy on Friday, that is meant to punish and pressure Moscow for its ongoing war against Ukraine.

The measure was a final legacy item for the late Senator Lindsey Graham, a close ally and friend of the president who had just returned from Ukraine when he unexpectedly died in July.

In the works for more than a year, the bill passed with large bipartisan margins; 86-11 in the Senate and 262-159 in the House.

It’s meant to starve Russian President Vladimir Putin of the resources Moscow needs to continue its war in Ukraine after invading its neighbour in February 2022.

The bill, co-drafted by Senator Richard Blumenthal, sanctions Russian officials, banks and a shadow fleet of tankers the Kremlin uses to evade existing Western sanctions and keep Russian energy moving.

“I wish Lindsey were here to celebrate this historic day. I know he would be so proud. Lindsey believed this bill, which he worked on for well over a year, would help end the war through squeezing Putin economically and forcing his customers to re-evaluate their relationship with Russia,” said Senator Darline Graham, Lindsey Graham's sister, in a statement Friday.

Darline Graham was appointed to complete her late brother's Senate term, set to end on the 3rd of January 2027.

“If he were here today, he would be jubilant about our bill’s passage — and already thinking about the next one,” Blumenthal said of Graham after the House passed the legislation on Wednesday evening.

“Putin is a thug who understands only force and strength, which is what we must show clearly and unequivocally,” Blumenthal added.

The bill also enables Trump to impose up to 100% tariffs on the top five importers of Russian oil or natural gas, with an exception for countries that import less than 15% of Russia’s natural gas exports and have taken significant steps to reduce those imports.

Despite overwhelming support on Capitol Hill, the legislation faced criticism from a vocal set of Democratic lawmakers, who favoured a tougher stance on Russia, but warned that Trump could use his new expanded tariff powers to impose fresh levies in the European Union and elsewhere.

“Why in the world would this Congress or the People’s House give this president unfettered authority to visit more tariffs on the world that will have an adverse economic impact on the American people?” said Democrat lawmaker and House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries.

Graham’s journey to getting Trump’s sign-off on the bill had been full of turbulence, as the White House had to be convinced that the president would be able to retain sufficient flexibility on sanctions.

Eventually, Trump gave a final nod of approval to the bill after it included his push for a provision which would enable his administration to extend sanctions on Iran for another five-year period.

Greenland's prime minister, Jens-Frederik Nielsen, welcomed the agreement, saying it was "to the benefit of us all" and that it would "ensure and strengthen" the security of the island.
The United States and Denmark have announced that they have come to an agreement over Greenland, with US President Donald Trump saying the deal would give Washington "permanent control" of the island's security.

"I am pleased to announce that the United States of America has entered into an Agreement with The Kingdom of Denmark, and Greenland, that gives the United States permanent control over security," Trump wrote in a post on his Truth Social account on Friday.

"From now on, no U.S. adversary can EVER have a base in Greenland, have a military presence in Greenland, or make sensitive investments in Greenland, without our express written approval," he added.

Trump has repeatedly threatened to seize the Danish autonomous territory since his return to the White House in January 2025, arguing that it was an essential "national security" concern and that if Washington did not act, then China or Russia would do so.

His rhetoric has sparked pushback from across Europe and prompted fresh funding pledges from Brussels and a renewed focus on Arctic security.

In a statement on Friday, Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen touted a "great agreement for Greenland, the Kingdom of Denmark, and the United States," while promising that it would recognise "the sovereignty and territorial integrity of the Kingdom and the Greenlandic people’s right to self-determination.”

"An agreement which strengthens our common security in the Arctic and the North Atlantic Area and therefore is great for NATO and Europe as well," she added.

Greenland's prime minister, Jens-Frederik Nielsen, also welcomed the agreement with the US, saying it was "to the benefit of us all" and that it would "ensure and strengthen the security of the island.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen had visited Greenland earlier this month as part of EU efforts to counter Trump's Arctic ambitions, announcing a €200 million partnership with the territory during her visit - which is set to be invested this year and the next.

Speaking at the World Economic Forum's annual meeting in Davos this year, von der Leyen had said the EU was working on a "massive European investment surge" in Greenland in a bid to ramp up Arctic security.

The Commission has proposed allocating €530 million to Greenland under its budget for the 2028–2034 period, which it said reflected its "growing geostrategic importance in the Arctic."

The US is reported to have had 17 military facilities and over 10,000 troops on Greenland at the height of the Cold War. It now operates just one base on the island, the Pituffik Space Base - the US Department of Defense's northernmost installation.

Pituffik is used for missile warning, missile defense and space surveillance missions, per the US Space Force.

Requests for emergency emigration assistance from Russia have surged eightfold since July, with searches for "how to leave" and "mobilisation" at their highest since early 2023. An electronic summons register now automatically triggers an exit ban, documented cases show.
Russians fearing a new military mobilisation are leaving the country in advance of possible restrictions on departure, according to the anti-war project Ark, after requests for emergency assistance and emigration advice from Russia have nearly octupled since the start of the summer.

The surge coincides with Russia's State Duma elections, which began on Friday and run through Sunday.

Kremlin critics and several Western governments have accused Moscow of planning to announce a new call-up to beef up its troops in its ongoing all-out war in Ukraine after the vote concludes, allowing an unpopular measure to avoid affecting the election.

Russia's Vladimir Putin has dismissed mobilisation rumours as "utter nonsense," but independent Russian media have counted 17 official denials of an imminent draft since the summer.

Jan Slovitsky, a human rights activist and coordinator of the Ark project, said the current wave was driven by lessons learned from 2022.

"Since mid-July a wave of panic has been rising in Russia, linked to the risk of mobilisation, fuel shortages and lack of internet access," he told Ark's network from Yerevan, where he is assisting new arrivals.

"People are leaving in advance because they have seen what happened during previous waves. And Armenia is facing a migration crisis, if we are not already in the thick of it."

The number of daily requests for relocation assistance has risen from around 70 to 120 since July, according to organisations helping Russians move abroad.

Yandex and Google searches for "how to leave" and "mobilisation" reached their highest level since early 2023 during the summer and the first ten days of September, according to independent Russian media analysis.

Preparations for a possible call-up are already visible. Tens of thousands of people have received mobilisation notices setting out the steps required if called up, and an electronic register of summonses has been introduced under which an exit ban takes effect automatically when a notice is issued.

In early September, passport control officers in Krasnodar detained a 24-year-old resident of Rostov region who had completed his military service in 2024 and served him with a notice banning him from leaving the country, the first such case reported at that crossing.

Daniil Neonov, a former candidate for municipal deputy in the Moscow City Duma, described leaving at the last moment.

"If I had waited another day or two, they would most likely not have let me leave, because they would have blocked my exit," he said. "I received an electronic summons. But I still managed to get out."

Fear of mobilisation has also spread to members of the elite and draft office staff, according to international media reports claiming officials have begun opening foreign bank accounts and moving assets abroad.

In August, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy warned of the risk of mobilisation in Russia, citing intelligence data.

Kyiv described a Kremlin plan to rapidly call up several hundred thousand Russians by the end of the year, with at least as many again the following year. Moscow has not commented on those claims.
