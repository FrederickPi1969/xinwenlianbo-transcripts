Updated: 4 November 2026 - 18:30 GMT+1
Dozens of Santas gathered at Rio de Janeiro's iconic Christ the Redeemer statue to celebrate their graduation. The 55 new recruits are preparing to spread Christmas cheer across Brazil.
… More
Christmas came early to Rio de Janeiro on Tuesday, when 55 graduates of the Escola de Papai Noel do Brasil gathered beneath Christ the Redeemer to mark the end of their training. Dressed in red suits and white beards, the new Santas sang carols as they rode the Corcovado mountain railway to the statue, then climbed the final steps for a ceremony that included a blessing and the presentation of diplomas.

The free course, open to men aged 50 and over with naturally white or grey hair and beards, runs for a month of Tuesday afternoon classes covering acting, improvisation, diction, posture, make-up, costume and beard care, plus Brazilian Sign Language. It was founded in 1993 by the actors Limachem Cherem and Conrado Freitas, with the costume designer Fátima Tavares Cherem, in the basement of Cherem's house in Vila Isabel.

More than 1,000 Santas have passed through the school in 33 years, along with about 300 Noeletes, its female equivalent. Many graduates are retirees earning extra income at shopping centres, hospitals and parties; some, such as the lorry driver Marinezio Teixeira de Oliveira, come back for a second certificate.

Updated: 4 November 2026 - 17:48 GMT+1
Police arrested supporters of Palestine Action outside the Supreme Court in London, United Kingdom, on Wednesday 4 November, as a legal battle over the British government's ban on the group resumed.
… More
Footage shows demonstrators lying on the pavement, with groups of officers lifting them and carrying them to a waiting police van, while others continued holding placards. Police said 25 people had been detained on suspicion of supporting a proscribed organisation under the Terrorism Act, with further arrests expected.

The legal challenge was brought by Palestine Action co-founder Huda Ammori, who is seeking to overturn the group's designation as a terrorist organisation. The court heard that it was the first of 98 proscribed groups banned solely for causing serious property damage, rather than advocating violence against people.

More than 4,000 arrests have been made under the ban, including 325 outside the US embassy on 31 October. Charges linked to the prohibition could be affected if the appeal succeeds.

Updated: 4 November 2026 - 17:31 GMT+1
Bodycam footage from a police officer killed in the attack captures the moments before a Russian bomb strikes a shelter in Komyshuvakha, in Ukraine's Zaporizhzhia region, killing at least 37 people.
… More
The recording shows people gathered outside a former school building, anxiously discussing the threat of further attacks. "Misha, is it coming again?" a woman asks, before a warning crackles over a police radio: "Guided aerial bombs heading towards Komyshuvakha! Take shelter!" Officers urgently direct people down the stairs into an underground refuge. Moments later, the camera records a violent explosion.

The strike killed at least 37 people, according to Ukraine's national police, including the officer whose bodycam captured the attack. The shelter, located inside a former school building, was hit by a Russian FAB-3000 aerial bomb, a powerful weapon weighing approximately three tonnes.

Rescue workers continued searching through the wreckage for victims as the scale of the destruction became clear. The footage provides a harrowing record of the final warnings and frantic efforts to reach safety before the building was struck, offering a rare first-hand perspective on the deadly bombardment.

Armenia sees 'totally new global role' for South Caucasus 'as a whole'
Armenian Prime Minister Nikol Pashinyan speaks at Euronews EU Enlargement Summit 2026, in Brussels, 3 November 2026

Armenia’s Prime Minister Nikol Pashinyan told Euronews that peace and convergence in the South Caucasus, based on a carefully balanced pro-EU political vector, create a major opportunity for the region, with "democracy as a strategy" for Armenia.
Armenian Prime Minister Nikol Pashinyan told Euronews that his country's strategy is to diversify and balance its foreign relations to pursue its national interests, as the current political and economic momentum in the South Caucasus brings the region a “totally new global role”.

During an interview at Euronews’ Enlargement Summit on Tuesday, Pashinyan said that the “South Caucasus is a region with differences, but with common interests and there can be common views and strategies as well”, which led the region’s countries to establish relations which “will open up the regional infrastructure connectivity.”

“The need to overcome the global supply chain crisis that the world is facing and the South Caucasus project, with peace between Armenia and Azerbaijan, cooperation between Georgia, Armenia and Azerbaijan, brings a new opportunity for the global supply chain supply chain crisis,” the Armenian premier told Euronews.

“The Central Asia region is getting more and more important globally and with the TRIPP (Trump Route for International Peace and Prosperity) corridor, now we are in the process of establishing a new global trade route for all countries, especially for the European Union, for China, for the Middle East, for Africa,” Pashinyan added.

Pashinyan then announced Armenia’s strategic interest in applying for EU membership, which has so far placed his country on a collision course with its membership in the Russia-led Eurasian Economic Union.

In a cautious approach that has characterised his position on this issue since the recent Armenian elections, the prime minister said his strategy was to balance Armenia's economic and political relations.

“If there is no balance in any relations, there is always a risk, and the process of balancing our foreign relations is a process of managing those risks," Pashinyan said.

Ultimately it is for the people of Georgia to make that decision, Pashinyan said, as "democracy is a strategy for us and we want our people to have opportunity to make the democratic choice."

“We know that it is not possible to be a member of European Union and the Eurasian Economic Union simultaneously and I made it public that we understand that,” he said.

"At a point in future, Armenia will need to make a choice and it is up to the citizens of Armenia to decide whether they want to stay in the European Union or they want to be members of the Eurasian Economic Union, so we are creating an alternative, to create an opportunity for our people to make a choice," Pashinyan added.

“We have complicated relations or no relations with Turkey and we have always had very good relations with Georgia, with Iran, where there are some restrictions connected with international sanctions," he explained.

"Our neighbours are candidate countries for the European Union, meaning Turkey and Georgia, and that is an asset, a benefit for our European policy," Pashinyan pointed out.

“Hopefully Armenia will be the third candidate country in our region,” he added, while announcing that Armenia could formally apply to join the EU in 2027 or 2028.

After the midterms, Europe will not get a weaker Trump but one who turns even more to global politics, warns US expert Josef Braml. Europe cannot wait this Trump out.
The midterm elections curb Donald Trump’s domestic power. But that does not automatically mean a weaker US president. On the contrary: if Congress restricts what he can do at home, Trump is likely to show his power even more in the areas where presidents traditionally have greater freedom to act, in foreign, trade and security policy.

The midterms are first and foremost a domestic reckoning. Even while votes are still being counted, a clear swing towards the Democrats is emerging in both chambers of Congress.

For Trump this is more than just a defeat for his party. This election was a referendum on his second term, on rising living costs, the consequences of his trade policy and, not least, on the Iran war.

But anyone who concludes from this that Trump will now be politically paralysed could be mistaken. His weakening at home may actually increase his willingness to take risks in foreign policy.

A Congress controlled by the Democrats can block legislation, influence budget funds, set up investigative committees and bombard the administration with subpoenas. A Democratic Senate can also hit Trump’s personnel policy where it hurts. The period of largely unhindered Republican control of government is over.

The US system of checks and balances limits a president’s room for manoeuvre more in domestic policy than in large parts of foreign policy. If Trump keeps running into walls at home, the political incentive grows for him to shift to the stage where he can still demonstrate dominance.

That would also fit his narcissistic leadership style. Trump does not readily accept constraints as a normal part of institutional power sharing. Defeats are personalised, responsibility is pushed onto others, strength must be displayed.

Foreign policy offers the ideal stage for that: presidents have considerable room to act, international crises generate maximum attention and real or perceived successes can be attributed to their own will to lead.

This does not mean that Trump will automatically start a new war. But Europe must assume that this president will compensate for weakened power at home by exercising his power abroad all the more visibly.

This is true above all in economic policy. Trump does not primarily see tariffs as an economic instrument but as a tool of political pressure. Europe has already felt this.

After the trade arrangement with the EU, a tariff cap of 15% applies to most European exports to the United States, while substantial special burdens, for example on steel and aluminium, remain in place.

Audi is particularly instructive here. At one point Trump raised the burden on European cars and trucks to 25% and openly linked his policy to the demand that companies should produce in the United States to avoid tariffs.

Audi eventually announced investments of around $4 billion for a US plant. Shortly before the midterms Gernot Döllner even appeared with Trump at a campaign rally in Georgia.

This makes Trump’s geo-economic strategy clear: it is not free trade that decides where companies locate but politically induced cost pressure.

German and European companies are supposed to shift capital and jobs to the United States. After an election defeat Trump might be tempted to double down right here, because he can sell toughness in trade policy as proof of his ability to act.

The geopolitical dimension is even more serious. Europe should not count on a weakened Trump automatically becoming more willing to compromise. Washington has particular means in relation to Ukraine to impose conditions on European allies without needing a major domestic legislative process every time.

The same applies to NATO. Trump has repeatedly questioned US support for the alliance in public. His course on Greenland also shows how far his transactional view of foreign policy goes.

Even at the NATO summit last summer he once again demanded US control over Greenland, in other words over territory belonging to NATO ally Denmark.

This is not a side issue. It illustrates a fundamental difference in how the alliance is understood. Ideally, Europe sees NATO as a community of values and security. Trump thinks of alliances as deals: who pays what, who gets what and what advantage the United States gains from it.

The risk is even greater with Iran. Trump wanted to contain the war, but more than six months after it began there was still no lasting settlement. Iran also demonstrated how vulnerable the global economy is at the Strait of Hormuz.

This creates a dangerous constellation. A president weakened at home might want to stage foreign policy strength more aggressively. It would be speculative to claim that Trump will escalate the conflict for that reason.

Finland, Norway, the Czech Republic, Latvia, Denmark and Sweden have said they will not face Russia at the 2027 championship, after a court ruling overturned a four-year ban on Russian national teams. Germany’s federation said the ruling “fundamentally changes the situation” for it as host nation.
Germany is considering withdrawing as host of the 2027 Ice Hockey World Championship after multiple European federations said they would refuse to play Russia, following a court ruling that overturned a four-year ban on Russian national teams.

The Court of Arbitration for Sport ruled on 30 October that the International Ice Hockey Federation (IIHF) must allow Russian national teams of all age groups to compete internationally, including at the 2027 World Championship scheduled for May in Germany. The IIHF said it disagreed with the ruling but would comply.

German ice hockey federation DEB chair Frank H Lutz said the ruling "fundamentally changes the situation" for the tournament.

"Our position remains unchanged: we reject the return of Russian national teams to international ice hockey tournaments," Lutz said.

"Allowing Russia to participate would have serious consequences for us as the host country in terms of security, as well as politically and economically," Lutz said, adding that DEB was analysing the decision with the IIHF and would provide further information on next steps.

Finland, Norway, the Czech Republic, Latvia, Denmark and Sweden have all announced they will not play Russia at the 2027 championship. Austria said it is considering joining them. USA Hockey did not respond to a request for comment.

Finland's Ice Hockey Federation said it would not play Russia until the country ended the ongoing all-out war against Ukraine.

Chair Heikki Hietanen said Finland was working to coordinate a joint position among as many countries as possible to tell the IIHF that "none of us will play against Russia" in the current situation.

Finnish Foreign Minister Elina Valtonen said that "Russian athletes will not be welcome in Finland next year if Russia continues its aggressive war."

"I will inform my EU colleagues that we intend to restrict the access of Russian athletes to the entire Schengen area," Valtonen said.

Czech Interior Minister Lubomir Metnar said the CAS ruling did not oblige the Czech Republic to issue visas to Russian nationals, as a decree banning short-term visas for Russian citizens remained in force.

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney said on Tuesday that Russia would not be permitted to compete at the World Junior Ice Hockey Championship, to be held in Alberta from late December.

The CAS ruling does not apply to the Women's World Championship, which begins this week in Denmark and will proceed without Russia.

Two tournament sponsors said they would withdraw if Russia competed. AJ Products, a Swedish office furniture company that has backed the championship since 1989, said it would ask for its logo not to be displayed if Russia takes part in a "protest against Russia," chief executive David Bray said.

Finnish tyre manufacturer Nokian Tyres said that "continuing sponsorship in the event of Russia's participation would contradict our values."

The European Commission said on Tuesday that while sports federations have the right to determine how they organise their competitions, "both federations and member states must comply with EU sanctions and restrictive measures."

"Sport should not serve as a tool of propaganda, and normalising the participation of Russian and Belarusian athletes in international competitions while the war in Ukraine continues is not something we want to support," spokesperson Eva Hrncirova said.

Hrncirova said the Commission also did not support the use of Russian anthems, symbols and flags at European sporting events.

The Commission noted that the European Council had decided in October that there should be no normalisation until a just and lasting peace was established in Ukraine.

Twenty-one members of the European Parliament separately called for sanctions and visa restrictions against Russia following the CAS ruling.

Ukrainian Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha‎ said there were no grounds for Russia's return to international sport while the war continued.

Ahmad Bakhshayesh Ardestani, a member of Iranian parliament’s security committee, said Iran would fire on any European base used against it, including in Italy and Germany. State-run media said any new confrontation could reach “the heart of Europe".
Iranian lawmakers have threatened missile strikes on European countries hosting US military bases, as state-run media warned on Wednesday that any renewed confrontation with the US could reach "the heart of Europe".

"If America uses its bases in Europe, including in Italy and Germany, Iran's missiles will be fired towards Europe," Ahmad Bakhshayesh Ardestani, a member of parliament's National Security Committee, told Euronews.

"In the two previous wars we did not do this, but this time we will not let Europe's assistance to America for an attack on Iran go without consequence;" Ardestani added.

Asked whether Europe was entering a new phase of war with Iran, Ardestani said European countries had already been at war with Iran in the previous phase by allowing their bases to be used.

He said Iran would strike any base used against it and would also target "the material and non-material assets of countries that assist America."

A second committee member, Fada Hossein Maleki, said Iran had prepared a new approach and scenario for any renewed conflict that he believed would surprise both the US and European countries.

The IRGC issued a statement on Wednesday — the anniversary of the 1979 seizure of the US embassy in Tehran, marked in Iran as "the day of fighting global arrogance" — pledging "an immediate, effective response without geographical limits to every level and type of threat."

The statement cited orders attributed to Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei calling for updating Iran's "target bank" and placing "the enemy's material and non-material assets within range."

Ahead of Wednesday's anniversary marches, government supporters hung a banner outside the German embassy in Tehran carrying a quote from the secretary of Iran's National Security Council, Mohsen Rezaei, "Europe will pay the price for supporting Israel."

IRGC-affiliated Fars news agency identified those responsible as members of the student Basij at the University of Tehran.

Euronews sought comment from the German embassy in Tehran and Germany's foreign ministry on whether the banner had been placed on the embassy railings. Both were unreachable at the time of publication.

Fars reported separately that Iran had warned Germany its military assets across the region would be treated as "legitimate defensive targets" if Berlin entered open military confrontation with Iran. The claim could not be independently verified.

Tehran has summoned Germany's ambassador in recent weeks over Berlin's support for Israel.

Kayhan newspaper, published under the supervision of a representative of Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei, wrote that Iran's range of potential targets was no longer confined to US bases in the Middle East and could extend to "the heart of Europe," citing an order attributed to Khamenei for a "military response without geographical limits."

Rezaei wrote on X that the US response to Iran's demands had been "not convincing," saying Washington would need to meet Iran's seven conditions in full before reopening the Strait of Hormuz.

Parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf welcomed the Republicans' defeat in Tuesday's congressional elections in a post on X, writing that the American people had "said no to war" and that Iran supported that message.

"If the US government ignores the public will in order to act in favour of managers who pay bribes and engage in international blackmail, it will realise that our 'no' comes with a nightmare," Ghalibaf added.

Asked whether he planned to take a tougher line against Iran, US President Donald Trump gave an ambiguous answer, saying he would examine the matter and act if needed.

Russian strikes on a humanitarian aid site in Ukraine's Zaporizhzhia region have killed 33 people, with more than 50 still missing, on Monday 2 November. Survivors and rescuers are being treated for severe injuries.
… More
Residents had gathered for their weekly humanitarian aid distribution when at least nine FAB-3000 bombs, each weighing around three tonnes, hit the site. Footage shows a huge crater, debris scattered across the area and bodies being recovered from the wreckage. Four police officers and two rescue workers were among those killed, while around 50 people remain missing. Volunteers joined the search despite repeated disruptions caused by Russian drones.

At a hospital in Zaporizhzhia, doctors treated four survivors for burns, fractures and shrapnel wounds. Among them was a 22-year-old woman with burns covering around 81% of her body and a rescue worker who underwent surgery for a broken jaw and arm injuries.

President Volodymyr Zelensky described the attack as "one of the largest Russian mass killings of this war", while regional authorities declared three days of mourning.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and his supporters have repeatedly made unsubstantiated claims that Arab-led parties had “stolen” the election, possibly with foreign assistance.
Israel’s election commission said on Wednesday it had found no fraud in last week’s vote, dealing a blow to Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s attempts to discredit the results.

Netanyahu and his supporters have repeatedly made unsubstantiated claims that Arab-led parties had “stolen” the election, possibly with foreign assistance.

The results showed Netanyahu's religious-nationalist bloc losing its parliamentary majority, while Arab-led parties made substantial gains.

“The review found no evidence of irregularities that compromised the integrity of the election or affected its results,” the committee said in a statement.

The acting director of the elections committee, Dean Livne, said its investigation examined every complaint “individually and thoroughly,” but found no evidence to support allegations that the election results were invalid.

The results, which are set to be formally certified on Wednesday, “accurately reflect the choices voters made at the ballot box,” Livne added.

A separate investigation by the Shin Bet intelligence service into allegations of foreign interference in the elections is ongoing, according to a spokesperson for the elections committee.

After receiving the certified results, Israel’s President Isaac Herzog will begin to meet with each of the parties elected to parliament.

Following two days of consultations, he will choose the candidate, most likely Netanyahu or his chief rival, opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot, who he believes is best positioned to form a coalition government, setting the stage for weeks of negotiations.

Neither Netanyahu's bloc nor Eisenkot's bloc of allies secured a parliamentary majority needed to form a new government.

That raises the possibility of an alliance between the two rivals or an alliance between Eisenkot and some of the Arab-led parties.

If neither man can put together a government, the country would hold a new election early next year.

The US midterm vote on Tuesday ushered in a wave of historic leadership firsts across the country, electoral milestones on a night that saw voters issue a sharp rejection of President Donald Trump's second term.

The first Black female governor, the first openly gay male senator and the first female Native American governor all won groundbreaking elections in the vote that racked up resounding wins for Democrats.

Bill Shiebler, chief strategy officer at the non-profit State Voices, a network of advocates for civic engagement and equity, told the AFP news agency the night was "a huge rebuke against hate and division."

"We're witnessing an electorate that increasingly sees itself in its leaders and the leaders who come out of communities they now represent," Shiebler said.

"I think that's part of a multiracial democracy working as it's intended, not just a novelty."

In the southern state of Georgia, Democrat Keisha Lance Bottoms won the race for governor, making her the first Black woman elected to the top office in any US state.

A former Atlanta mayor, Bottoms defeated billionaire health care executive Rick Jackson, a win that returns the governor's office to Democrats after more than two decades of Republican control.

Democratic Representative Chris Pappas, meanwhile, will become the first openly gay man to serve in the US Senate after winning in New Hampshire. He has served in the US House of Representatives since 2019.

"No one should be discriminated against because of who they are or who they love," he said on his website.

In New Mexico, Democrat Deb Haaland became the first Native American woman elected governor, beating Republican Gregg Hull.

A member of the Laguna Pueblo tribe, she had served as the Secretary of the Interior under former president Joe Biden, the first Native American to serve as a US cabinet secretary.

And in Michigan, leftist Abdul El-Sayed was in a tight, high-profile race aiming to become the first Muslim elected to the US Senate.

The former medical administrator drew huge campaign crowds across Michigan with his promises to make healthcare free.

The sweeping midterm rebuke of Trump also saw a number of state-level electoral firsts, including the first Latina governor in Texas with Gina Hinojosa's victory.

Looking ahead, if the current House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries wins the speakership as expected in the new Congress, he would become the first Black speaker of the House in US history.

"This was a historic election where people decided to believe in themselves and really lean into the hope about the promise of this country, rebuking an effort to snuff out people's voices," Shiebler said.

Trump struck a conciliatory note despite his history of questioning elections, telling The New York Times he was "absolutely willing" to accept the results.

But he sought to distance himself from his own party's poor showing, adding that "I don't view it as a hit on me."

He acknowledged that the Iran conflict had contributed to the defeat, while defending the military campaign and he suggested the rival parties could find common ground on immigration, long one of the most divisive issues of his presidency.

Democrats flipped seats that had long been held by Republicans, scoring breakthroughs in South Carolina, North Carolina, Iowa, Kansas, Ohio, Texas and Maine.

In Maine, former logger Troy Jackson defeated longtime Republican Senator Susan Collins, the party's seventh Senate seat captured from Republicans.

In Kansas, Methodist minister Adam Hamilton became the first Democrat elected to the Senate from the reliably conservative state since 1932. Iowa had not elected a Democratic senator since 2008.

Texas Democrat James Talarico declared he had defeated "Goliath" in winning a Senate seat, while Gina Hinojosa unseated the state's Republican governor, Greg Abbott, ending decades of Republican rule.

Democrats also protected key Senate seats of their own, including Ossoff in Georgia and an open seat in New Hampshire.

The scale of the victory grew further when Abdul El-Sayed defeated former Republican lawmaker Mike Rogers in Michigan, making him the first Muslim elected to the upper chamber of Congress.

El-Sayed won despite a last-minute curveball in his campaign when congresswoman Haley Stevens, who lost the Democratic primary to El-Sayed, came out in support of Rogers at the 11th hour.

The party's House victory saw gains across Pennsylvania, Kentucky, Arizona, North Carolina, Wisconsin and New Jersey, including districts Trump had carried comfortably in 2024.

In one closely watched bellwether, Democrat J.D. Ford ousted Republican congresswoman Victoria Spartz in Indiana, capturing a district Trump won by 17 points two years earlier.

Democrats also won Georgia's governorship, electing former Atlanta mayor Keisha Lance Bottoms as the first Black woman governor in US history.

Republicans had some consolation in Florida, where Trump-backed Byron Donalds became the state's first Black governor and Ashley Moody retained a Senate seat, but only after seeing off surprisingly strong Democratic challenges.

The night was "a big wipeout and a strong repudiation of Donald Trump and his policies," George Washington University professor Todd Belt told the AFP news agency.

Democratic control of Congress will allow the party to pursue investigations into the Iran war, immigration enforcement and allegations of corruption, while blocking Trump's agenda and senior appointments.

Democratic Representative Robert Garcia, now expected to lead the powerful House Oversight Committee, warned Republicans on social media: "LAWYER UP."
