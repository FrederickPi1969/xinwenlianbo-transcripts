The list of states Moscow considers unfriendly was formalised as a form of retaliation in a government order in March 2022, weeks after Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

It covers the US, the UK, Canada, Japan, South Korea, Switzerland, Ukraine, Australia, New Zealand and EU member states, all of which have pushed back against Russia's war of aggression.

This autumn, the Russian government nearly doubled import duties on spirits from unfriendly countries, raising the rate from 20% to 35%, primarily affecting European and North American producers.

Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said the placement of foreign companies' Russian assets under temporary administration was directly linked to those companies' home countries being on the list.

The measure has affected the Russian operations of Nestlé, Auchan, FM Logistic, Leroy Merlin, Metro Cash & Carry, Tarkett, Japan Tobacco International (JTI) and others.

Russia's Supreme Court cited the unfriendly countries designation in a child custody dispute, ruling that returning a child to France could run counter to her interests in part because France appears on the list.

In March 2025, Russian citizen Alexei Ermakov took his daughter on a two-week holiday to Turkey and Slovakia with the consent of her mother, Ukrainian citizen Anna Tishkova, then brought the child to Russia in April.

He subsequently arranged Russian citizenship for the girl. Despite international agreements on returning children to their country of habitual residence, the court declined to order her return to France.

A bill supported in the State Duma would allow vehicles stolen or illegally exported from unfriendly countries to be registered in Russia.

Supporters say it addresses legal gaps caused by the breakdown in cooperation with Western states; opponents say it would weaken property protections for owners in those countries.

In a separate case, the Supreme Court ordered the Interior Ministry to register a vehicle flagged in an international database at Canada's request, ruling that listing in an international stolen vehicles register is not in itself sufficient grounds to refuse registration in Russia.

Putin signed a decree this autumn under which individuals who cannot prove citizenship of Russia or a friendly state or produce a residency permit are automatically treated as persons from unfriendly countries.

A further decree allows Russian debtors to withhold payment from creditors in unfriendly countries, departing from the standard principle of equal treatment of parties to contracts.

Standard principles of private international law require courts to resolve contracts, custody matters and property disputes on their legal merits, regardless of the nationality or political status of the parties.

Russia’s application of a foreign-policy designation to those areas departs from norms in conventions it has ratified, among them the 1980 Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, which obligates signatory states to return children removed across borders without consent.

The new post in Kuwait will be part of the six Gulf countries’ joint military command, based in Riyadh, GCC announced on Tuesday.
The six members of the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) agreed on Tuesday to set up a military command post in Kuwait and station forces there, Secretary-General Jasem Mohamed Albudaiwi said.

Albudaiwi announced the decision after defence ministers from Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates met in Manama. The council did not set an opening date or say how many troops it would send or what their duties would be.

Kuwait lies at the northern end of the Gulf, bordering Iraq and Saudi Arabia and facing Iran across the water.

The new post will be part of the GCC’s Unified Military Command, which the six countries set up in 2013 to plan joint operations. Its main headquarters opened in Riyadh in 2021.

Hours before the ministers met, the Saudi-led coalition in Yemen said it had shot down two missiles fired by the Houthis towards Riyadh.

It also reported intercepting a ballistic missile headed for southern Saudi Arabia and a drone over the country’s Eastern Province.

Albudaiwi condemned the Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia and said Iranian strikes had damaged civilian infrastructure in Gulf countries.

Tehran has repeatedly fired missiles and drones at Gulf states since the US and Israel launched their offensive against Iran in the opening salvo of the ongoing war on 28 February.

Gulf defence ministers have previously agreed to share more intelligence and speed up work on a joint missile warning system. Gulf leaders renewed that call at their April summit in Jeddah.

A day after Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani met Trump at the White House, a Qatari foreign ministry spokesman said both sides were making positive statements about a diplomatic solution but that “we cannot say how close or how far away an agreement is.”
Qatar said on Tuesday it had seen “progress” in talks between the US and Iran, stating that Doha as a key mediator was “working tirelessly to end the uncertainty over the crisis between Washington and Tehran”.

The assessment came a day after Qatar’s Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani met US President Donald Trump at the White House.

Al Hashmi said the White House meeting "was held in a positive atmosphere and was focused on making progress in the talks."

“We cannot say how close or how far away an agreement is,” Ibrahim bin Sultan Al Hashmi, director of the foreign ministry’s Media and Communication Department, told a weekly briefing in Doha.

"We see that mediatory efforts continue, and both sides are making positive statements about a diplomatic solution," he added.

He said Qatari officials had held talks in Tehran and consulted other governments in the region before Sheikh Mohammed travelled to Washington.

Trump said Gulf states, including Qatar and Saudi Arabia, had urged him to hold off on fresh strikes. He said he would do so for now but keep the US naval blockade in place.

Tehran has proposed resuming talks in as little as two days if Washington halts attacks, lifts its blockade of Iranian ports in the Strait of Hormuz and waives some sanctions. Negotiations on Iran’s nuclear programme would come later.

Iranian foreign ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei defended the proposal on Monday. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio had dismissed some of Tehran’s conditions as “outrageous and ridiculous” the day before.

Al Hashmi said the continued Hormuz closure would hit the most vulnerable hardest, calling for the strait — a key route for Gulf oil and liquefied natural gas exports — to reopen to shipping as soon as possible.

Sheikh Mohammed joined ministers from Saudi Arabia, France, Britain, Germany and Italy in Paris on Tuesday to discuss the war on the sidelines of a G7 meeting.

Tokyo says the new equipment will help Manila monitor shipping routes that are also important to Japan.
Japan agreed on Monday to give the Philippine military a coastal radar system and surveillance drones to help it track ships, in a grant worth 4.3 billion Japanese yen (€24 million).

Philippine Foreign Secretary Maria Theresa Lazaro and Japan’s ambassador Katsuro Kitagawa signed the agreement in Manila. The radar will go to the Philippine Navy and the drones to the Air Force.

Japan’s foreign ministry said the equipment would also help protect shipping routes important to Japan, including those through the South China Sea and the Luzon Strait, between the Philippines and Taiwan.

Japan handed over five coastal radar systems to the Philippines in February under a 2023 grant. Tokyo says the new radar will be the first complete defence system it supplies under its Official Security Assistance programme.

Beijing has criticised the programme since its creation, calling it an attempt to build a security network around China under the cover of development aid.

The Philippines and China both claim Scarborough Shoal, where China’s military said it sent ships and aircraft on patrol on 30 October.

In a phone call on 3 November, Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi urged Manila to act cautiously over the dispute. Lazaro described their conversation as “frank and constructive” and said both sides wanted to keep talking.

Beijing has separately described Japan’s expanding military role in the South China Sea as destabilising, and has accused Tokyo of using its security partnerships with regional states to advance US strategic interests.

China claims historic rights to resources across much of the South China Sea. In a case brought by the Philippines, an international tribunal ruled in 2016 that those claims had no legal basis where they exceeded the rights granted by the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea. Beijing has rejected the ruling.

Japan and the Philippines are also discussing transferring an Abukuma-class destroyer to the Philippine navy.

Their defence ministers said in October that they wanted the handover to take place as soon as possible after Japan retires the ship, which it expects to do in the coming years. Philippine crews would receive training first.

The EU approved a separate €15 million package in July to help Philippine forces monitor the country’s waters.

Its first grant to the Philippines under the European Peace Facility will fund coastal radar, infrared surveillance equipment and training.

Trump met at the White House with Qatar Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, a key mediator in the Iran war, in his first meeting with a foreign leader since his party's massive losses in the midterm elections.

The measure was seen as a key test for the global campaign against inequality, but warnings that the super-rich would leave the state won out.
Californians have voted down a proposal for a one-off levy on the state’s billionaires, according to projections by US broadcasters on Monday.

Proposition 40 would have required around 200 of the state’s richest residents to hand over 5% of their total net worth. Most of the money would have gone towards healthcare.

CNN and CBS News both called the result almost a week after polling day. California has the largest population of any US state and is known for counting votes slowly.

The measure had already looked set to fail. With roughly 60% of ballots counted on Wednesday, the “No” side was ahead by 55.6% to 44.4%.

Prop 40 was one of many questions on the ballot in last week’s midterm elections. Those elections brought sweeping gains for the Democrats across the country and heavy losses for President Donald Trump’s Republicans.

Supporters of wealth taxes saw the vote as an important test for a global campaign against widening inequality. If California were a country, it would be the world’s fourth-largest economy, but the gap between its richest and poorest residents is vast.

Campaigners estimated the tax would raise about $100 billion. That money would have gone to the state’s public healthcare system, which has been squeezed by Trump’s budget cuts.

Opponents spent hundreds of millions of dollars fighting the measure, among them Google co-founder Sergey Brin. They argued it would drive the super-rich out of California.

In the run-up to the vote, “No” campaign adverts filled the state’s TV channels. They warned that California would lose future tax revenue if its billionaires moved elsewhere.

Gavin Newsom, the outgoing governor, had said he would vote against it. Newsom is widely expected to run for the White House in 2028.

Joseph Stiglitz, the Nobel Prize-winning economist, sent a statement to AFP through the International Capital Tax Commission before the projections came out. “Significant changes rarely move in a straight line,” he said.

Stiglitz added: “Prop 40 put a tax on billionaire wealth on the ballot in the United States for the first time.”

California has voted on a wealth tax three times before this decade and turned it down each time.

MWC26 returned to Doha as the industry faces a dual challenge: building the networks that will power artificial intelligence and narrowing a digital divide that still leaves millions of people without mobile internet.
The Middle East and North Africa’s mobile economy is projected to reach €421 billion by 2030, but a new industry report highlights a persistent problem: economic growth is accelerating, yet millions of people still are not benefiting from the digital economy.

GSMA’s Mobile Economy MENA 2026 report, released on the opening day of MWC26 Doha, revealed that mobile technologies and services generated an estimated €340 billion in economic value in 2025, supporting 2.2 million jobs and contributing around €35.8 billion to public finances.

Meanwhile, 48% of MENA’s population used mobile internet last year, while 49% remained offline despite living in areas covered by mobile networks.

The figures point to a challenge that extends beyond infrastructure: ensuring people can afford, access and use the technology available to them.

This year’s MWC26 Doha carries added significance as it takes place alongside the International Telecommunication Union’s Plenipotentiary Conference, the first time the two events are being held at the same location.

The two gatherings bring industry leaders and policymakers together at a time when decisions on connectivity standards, digital infrastructure and technological governance will help shape how the global digital economy evolves.

Held in partnership with Qatar’s Ministry of Communications and Information Technology, the three-day MWC26 Doha aims to advance digital connectivity while also creating meaningful access for consumers.

The event is also being held as the region faces continued uncertainty over the future of the US-Iran war, but opening-day turnout has signalled resilience in the ICT industry and that connectivity remains a business priority.

As AI systems rapidly evolve to carry out more complex tasks autonomously, the industry faces growing pressure to provide the computing power, capacity and resilience needed to support them.

Speaking at the opening ceremony on Sunday, Qatar’s Minister of Communications and Information Technology, Mohammed bin Ali Mannai, noted the rise of AI agents was increasing the need for more resilient networks and advanced computing capabilities.

For GSMA Director General Vivek Badrinath, the region’s digital future depends not only on expanding connectivity, but also on ensuring networks, services and devices work across borders and communities.

“Progress only counts if everyone shares in it. That means two things: global standards that keep networks, services and devices working across borders, and a concerted effort to connect the people who are still offline,” Badrinath said at the opening ceremony.

Speaking to Euronews, he acknowledged that the industry had not done well in the past year to make mobile connectivity more affordable to the people who really need it.

“A year ago, we were hoping to be able to bring to the market smartphones in the $50 range. That's very much impossible today with the increase of RAM prices driven by the huge demand for AI. This is an unfortunate side effect,” Badrinath said.

He added that strong demand for AI is depriving smartphone producers of the resources they need to make entry-level phones.

Still, investment forecasts are encouraging. GSMA projects that operators will invest around €80 billion across MENA between 2025 and 2030.

But the challenge is ensuring these investments translate into services people and businesses can use.

While connectivity has become the foundation of most AI functions, the technology is moving fast beyond content generation and chatbots, expanding into fields such as scientific research, healthcare, materials, and energy.

In an interview with Euronews, SandboxAQ CEO Jack Hidary said quantitative AI approaches can help tackle complex problems traditional methods may still struggle to solve.

“The second bucket of AI is AI for the physical world, for the real world. We look at biomedicine, energy, financial services, these big sectors are really areas for LQM and LLMs, so it's great to see the convergence of these two now,” Hidary said.

Fresh from a recent deal with Anthropic and a €449 million R&D award from the US Commerce Department, Hidary believes these milestones will give the company leverage to innovate further.

“It's a very exciting new award that we got to focus on batteries, on magnets, on the things that we need to produce semiconductors in the United States so that we can really power the economy,” he added.

Extending network coverage alone will not be enough to prevent the onset of a second digital divide if governments and policymakers do not commit to removing barriers like smartphone affordability, access to data packages, and the skills needed to use digital services.

These barriers risk becoming more consequential as AI reshapes industries and workplaces, impacting people without the tools or training.

Assistant Resident Representative and Inclusive Growth and Innovation Team Leader at UNDP Egypt Abeer Shakweer echoed GSMA’s call for growth that benefits all segments of society.
