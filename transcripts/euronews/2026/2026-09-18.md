The president has already pursued legal action against other major news organisations, including The New York Times, The Wall Street Journal and the BBC.
President Donald Trump has said he is banning three major news organisations, CNN, MS NOW and Politico, from the White House with immediate effect, accusing them of publishing “fake news”, “fiction” and “lies”.

It is not yet clear what the ban will entail. Trump also warned that restrictions could be extended to other media outlets.

In a post on his Truth Social platform on Friday, the president said the three outlets “constantly write or report fiction and lies” about his administration, although he provided no specific examples.

The move is the latest test of First Amendment protections guaranteeing freedom of the press in the United States.

It is also the latest move by Trump’s second administration to restrict press access. He has already tightened controls over which journalists can join the White House press pool, the small group of reporters who accompany the president into restricted spaces such as the Oval Office and aboard Air Force One.

CNN is part of the traditional five-network television pool that travels with and covers the president. It is not yet clear whether the new ban will affect that arrangement.

In 2025, Trump restricted Associated Press access to some presidential events after the news agency continued to use the name “Gulf of Mexico” rather than Trump’s preferred “Gulf of America”. AP subsequently launched legal action over the restrictions. The case remains ongoing.

During Trump’s first term, CNN reporter Kaitlan Collins was barred from a White House Rose Garden event after officials said questions she asked the president earlier that day were "inappropriate" for the venue.

While it is extremely rare for a US president to bar an entire news organisation from the White House, previous administrations have also imposed restrictions on individual outlets. President Richard Nixon restricted access for The Washington Post during the Watergate scandal in the 1970s.

Officials say the teenager had warned friends after lunch that he was “going to do something” and told them to go home, but they thought he was joking.
Three people are dead after a school shooting in the southern Philippines, including two 14-year-old students and the 16-year-old gunman. At least eight others are reported to be injured.

Authorities say the gunman, a student at Banga National High School in South Cotabato, opened fire on students in a neighbouring classroom.

The Governor of South Cotabato, Reynaldo Tamayo, said the two students killed were a boy and a girl. He added the gunman, also shot himself and died.

Officials say the teenager had warned friends after lunch that he was “going to do something” and told them to go home, but they thought he was joking.

The weapon used in the attack, believed to be a 9mm gun, reportedly belonged to the shooter’s father, a Department of Education employee, and had been stored in a locked vault.

The Mayor of Banga, Albert Palencia, told local media that police found posts made by the shooter in an online community, where young people involved in school shootings are "idolized". Quoting the boy's school adviser, Palencia said the mother of the boy wanted to bring him to a psychiatrist.

Updated: 18/09/2026 - 16:38 GMT+2
Japan's busiest railway station is testing mobile robotic rubbish bins in Tokyo, with the aim of easing the workload of sanitation workers.
… More
Three automated bins are being trialled at Shinjuku Station, where more than 2.7 million passengers pass through the wider complex each day. Standing just over one metre tall and moving at a cautious 20 centimetres per second, the machines can stop quickly around passengers. The trial began on 8 September and runs until 20 November on weekdays between 10:00 and 17:00 on the first basement-level paid concourse.

Run by East Japan Railway in the world's busiest rail hub, the project addresses a setting where fixed bins have been reduced for cleanliness and security, and traditional bins are emptied manually more than 10 times a day. The robots follow pre-programmed circular routes between ticket gates to collect general waste and plastic bottles, returning autonomously to a collection point when full.

Designed to assist human workers rather than replace them, Japan Railway is assessing commuter engagement and safe navigation through busy underground areas, using video and sensor data solely for analysis.

While some passengers have welcomed the concept, tourists have been drawn to the devices' large, googly eyes and train-themed livery featuring the station mascot, Shinjukuma.

Updated: 18/09/2026 - 16:26 GMT+2
Torrential rain and severe thunderstorms struck Tunis on 17 September, flooding roads, disrupting traffic, and forcing schools and universities to suspend afternoon classes from 3 p.m. local time.
… More
The Tunis Governorate announced suspension of afternoon classes across all public and private educational, training, and university institutions, citing warnings from the National Institute of Meteorology and advice from the regional disaster-prevention committee. Between 30 and 50 millimetres of rain were forecast across Greater Tunis in a short period, overwhelming drainage systems in several neighbourhoods.

Water quickly covered roads and pavements, forcing residents to wade through flooded streets. Cars and buses struggled to pass, with some vehicles left stranded and one trapped beneath a tunnel. In the historic medina, residents took refuge in shops and under awnings as torrents of water swept through the narrow streets.

The storm also caused power and internet cuts and sent water into some homes. Footage shared on social media appeared to show power lines catching fire amid the heavy downpour.

The rainfall came after a summer of prolonged heatwaves and follows a year of repeated flooding. In January, at least five people died when Tunisia recorded its heaviest rainfall in more than seven decades.

The latest storms have once again highlighted the pressure that extreme rainfall can place on Tunis’ infrastructure, particularly after long periods of intense heat.

Yabloko, Russia's only publicly anti-war party still operating inside the country, was barred from participating. The party saw a surge in social media support, particularly among young people, after it was initially allowed to stand.

An unnamed European diplomat said the elections were "pretty much a sham" but described them as "a ritual the Kremlin considers necessary," adding that it would seek to ensure a high turnout.

Independent observers have previously documented the use of state resources to influence results, including bussing voters to polling stations, pressuring employees of state institutions to vote for the ruling party, and pushing online voting as it is easier to monitor and control.

"As in the Soviet era, elections must demonstrate the loyalty of citizens," Russian political scientist and Head of Analysis of Transformation Processes at the NEST Centre Nikolai Petrov said.

The election will fill 450 State Duma seats and the governorships of 11 federal regions, with the country's only remaining anti-war party excluded from the ballot and fears of mobilisation overshadowing the vote.

Running from 18 to 20 September, the election will also choose deputies to regional legislatures in 39 federal subjects.

Voters will choose eight of the 11 regional heads directly, while regional parliaments will elect the remaining three. Polling stations have been opened at Russian border crossings with Poland and the three Baltic states.

Half of the 450 Duma seats will be allocated through nationwide party lists and the other half through single-member constituencies.

Voting in Russian elections is also taking place in parts of Ukraine occupied by Moscow's troops, a move that neither Kyiv nor the West recognises as legitimate.

Almost 200 candidates in these elections have taken part in Russia's war in Ukraine, according to electoral commissions.

The Kremlin has promoted them as part of Russia's "true elite," but the initiative is primarily a recruitment tool to boost Moscow's troops on the front lines, with few standing any real chance of being elected.

Ahead of the vote, Putin urged Russians to vote to show troops that "behind our fighters stand millions of people" and to deliver an "answer to those who want to weaken Russia".

Meanwhile, Russians fear another wave of military mobilisation, despite Putin personally denying plans for a call-up twice in three days in September alone.

Independent Russian media have counted 17 official denials of an imminent mobilisation. Russia called up 300,000 reservists in the autumn of 2022 in a move that triggered significant public alarm and a mass departure of men of fighting age from the country.

Tatiana Stanovaya of the Carnegie Russia Eurasia Centre, which is banned in Russia, said she considered a new draft unlikely.

Istanbul police detained Galatasaray veteran Hasan Şaş and Netflix actor Aras Bulut İynemli alongside a rapper, a sports journalist and influencers in the fourth such celebrity drug operation since late 2025. Prosecutors said the investigation covers use, facilitation and supply of narcotics.
A Turkish actor and a footballer who helped the national football team finish third at the 2002 World Cup were among 16 people detained in anti-drug raids across Istanbul on Friday as part of a probe into celebrities, venue operators and social media influencers.

Hasan Şaş, who earned 40 caps for the Turkish national team and played for Galatasaray during its most successful European period, and actor Aras Bulut İynemli, known abroad for Netflix Turkey productions, were detained alongside actor Melis Sezen, rapper Sefo and 12 others.

Bakırköy Chief Public Prosecutor's Office said the investigation concerns allegations of using narcotic and psychotropic substances, facilitating their use and mediating their supply.

Others detained include actors Nilperi Şahinkaya, Melisa Şenolsun and Afra Saraçoğlu, YouTuber Melis İşiten, sports journalist Yağız Sabuncuoğlu, model Yeliz Yeşilçimen, content creator Aydan Osmanlı, singer Anıl Durmuş and businesswoman Hande Demir.

Friday's operation is the latest in a series of similar anti-narcotics raids targeting well-known figures in Istanbul since late 2025.

In December 2025, singer Aleyna Tilki, actor İrem Sak and influencer Danla Bilic were among those taken into custody and later released after biological samples were collected.

In May of this year, authorities detained 20 people from a list of 25 suspects that included actors Serenay Sarıkaya and Onur Tuna, singer Mabel Matiz, and television presenter Mirgün Cabas.

In July, searches and biological sampling were carried out on a further 25 suspects including singers Sıla Gençoğlu and İlyas Yalçıntaş and producer Fatih Aksoy. Yalçıntaş was subsequently arrested.

Russia’s parliamentary election is under way, with voting beginning in the Far East on Friday, 18 September before spreading across the country for three days. Polls will remain open until Sunday evening, when voters will have chosen all 450 members of the State Duma.
… More
Russia has begun a three-day parliamentary election to fill all 450 seats in the State Duma, with about 111 million people eligible to vote. Polling stations opened first in Kamchatka, Chukotka, Magadan and other eastern regions, where local officials urged residents to turn out. The Kremlin is closely watching participation, with Vladimir Putin presenting high turnout as a sign of national unity and support for the war in Ukraine.

The election also includes remote electronic voting in several regions and is being held in Russian-occupied Ukrainian territories, including Donetsk, Luhansk, Kherson and Zaporizhzhia, as well as Crimea. Ukraine and Western governments consider the polls there illegal.

Outside Russia, 333 polling stations are operating in 152 countries, while early voting has already taken place in remote areas and among soldiers. The election comes as the war increasingly affects daily life across Russia, with drone attacks, fuel shortages, economic pressure and renewed fears of mobilisation adding to the tensions surrounding the vote.

Brett Hartl, the government affairs director at the Center for Biological Diversity, said the move "stabs at the very heart of the Endangered Species Act by giving federal approval to the killing of some of America’s most beloved wildlife."
The Trump administration has issued a sweeping new directive that could see the unintentional killing of protected wildlife no longer treated as unlawful.

A memo from Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) director Brian Nesvik distributed to FWS field offices earlier this week called for a reinterpretation of "take," a term relating to the pursuit, killing or capture of a species under the 1973 Endangered Species Act (ESA).

The definition would now be altered to "affirmative conduct intentionally directed against a particular animal or animals," effectively excluding incidental harm caused by economic activities from being considered "take."

"A vessel that inadvertently strikes a whale has not taken it, because the vessel's course was not set against the whale," the memo says, quoting former Supreme Court Justice Antonin Scalia in a 1995 case. "Felling a tree is not a take of the bats roosting in it unless the tree is felled for the purpose of killing or capturing them".

The document, which was obtained and shared by the Center for Biological Diversity, comes after a previous rule change that rescinded the definition of "harm" under the ESA.

"The memorandum accurately reflects the US Fish and Wildlife Service's guidance for implementing the Endangered Species Act following the final rule rescinding the regulatory definition of 'harm,'" the department said.

"Under the guidance, the ESA's prohibition on 'take' continues to prohibit conduct including harassing, pursuing, hunting, shooting, wounding, killing, trapping, capturing or collecting protected wildlife," it added.

Brett Hartl, the government affairs director at the Center for Biological Diversity, said the move "stabs at the very heart of the Endangered Species Act by giving federal approval to the killing of some of America’s most beloved wildlife."

"The Trump administration is desperate to annihilate environmental protections, but this incredibly radical measure is both cruel and profoundly unlawful. Killing endangered wildlife is illegal, whether that’s your purpose or not," Hartl added in a press release.

Dozens of Israelis demonstrated outside the Knesset in West Jerusalem on 17 September, accusing political leaders of using regional tensions to interfere with the 27 October parliamentary election.
… More
Some protesters wore masks bearing Benjamin Netanyahu’s face, while others carried placards warning of an “election disruption war”. A missile-shaped sign suggested that a new security crisis could be used to justify postponing the vote.

Organiser Assaf Sati El-Bar alleged that political leaders were escalating tensions in the region to divert attention or create a pretext for delaying the election. Protester Danny Elgarat, whose brother Itzhak was killed after being taken hostage by Hamas, said the group wanted to make sure Israelis could vote on the scheduled date.

The protest comes as the campaign enters its final weeks. The Knesset was dissolved in July, formally setting the election for 27 October 2026. Under Israeli law, it would be the country’s first national election held on schedule in nearly four decades.

Demonstrators cited possible flashpoints including Al-Aqsa, regional escalation, West Bank demolitions and military operations in Gaza or Lebanon. They argued that any of these could be used as grounds to disrupt the electoral timetable.

There is no confirmed evidence of an official government plan to postpone the election, however. The claims of an attempt to delay the vote were made by protesters and have not been independently established.

Dozens of protesters marched through San Francisco on 17 September, calling on AI companies to “stop the AI race”. The proposal, which the United States had drafted to extend the UN expert panel’s mandate for a year was defeated by a vote of 11-2, with Somalia and Pakistan abstaining because of the Russian and Chinese vetoes.
Russia and China have vetoed a United Nations resolution that would have allowed independent experts to continue monitoring sanctions reimposed on Iran over its nuclear programme, dealing a blow to international oversight efforts.

The United States had drafted the resolution to extend the UN expert panel's mandate for a year. It was defeated on Thursday at the UN Security Council by a vote of 11-2, with Somalia and Pakistan abstaining.

The UN Security Council sanctions, which were reimposed on 27 September last year under the snapback mechanism of the 2015 nuclear deal, remain in effect. They freeze Iranian assets abroad, restrict arms deals with Tehran and penalise the development of its ballistic missile programme.

France, Germany and the United Kingdom triggered the snapback provision, which restored the Security Council sanctions committee and authorised the panel of experts to monitor compliance. Thursday's veto removes that oversight without lifting the underlying sanctions.

The vote comes as the Trump administration pursues a broader campaign to isolate Tehran economically. Separately on Thursday, Washington moved against digital currency networks it says are being used to give sanctioned Iranian entities illegal access to the international financial system.

The moves also come amid a more than six-month-old war in Iran that has reached a stalemate.

The US Treasury and State Departments announced that they had targeted two companies — BitBank and the Pishtaz Simorgh Electronic Trade Co. — and three associates of BitBank’s previously sanctioned chief, Babak Zanjani, who are accused of playing a role in helping Iran evade sanctions.

At the United Nations, Iran’s UN mission thanked China and Russia for vetoing the US resolution, saying on social media that the countries “prevented yet another cynical attempt by the United States and its allies to abuse the Security Council for their political purposes.”

Since Israel and the US struck Iranian nuclear sites in June 2025, UN nuclear inspectors have been unable to verify the status of Iran’s stockpile of enriched uranium.

The UN nuclear watchdog has said Iran maintained a stockpile of 440.9 kilograms of uranium enriched up to 60% purity — a short, technical step away from weapons-grade levels of 90%.

Russian UN Ambassador Vassily Nebenzia reiterated Moscow’s claim that the “snapback” of sanctions against Iran was illegal, and therefore the resolution to extend the mandate of the panel of experts “was deeply flawed and did not have any legal basis.”

Russia and China had strongly urged the US and its allies not to put the resolution to a vote, which would only increase tensions in the council, Nebenzia said, but Washington “decided to opt for confrontation.”

According to the French UN Ambassador Jerome Bonnafont, the current council president, implementing sanctions is “a crucial instrument to encourage Iran” to return to its nuclear obligations. France will study how to ensure sanctions are enforced, he said.

One possibility would be to follow what happened after the North Korea panel of experts was abolished: establishing a monitoring team that includes France, Germany, the UK and the United States.

Washington's deputy ambassador to the UN, Jennifer Locetta, said Washington wasn’t surprised by the vetoes on Thursday because the experts “would have shone the light” on prohibited cooperation between Iran and its partners.

According to Locetta, the vetoes are part of a broader effort to prevent expert monitoring of sanctions and even the appointment of panel members "to stifle reporting that implicates their own activities in support of rogue regimes.”
