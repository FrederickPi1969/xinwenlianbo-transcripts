Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey are pursuing talks with Tehran while strengthening their military readiness under the terms of the Mecca Defence Alliance.
Senior military and security officials from Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey are holding talks with Iranian officials in Tehran as diplomatic efforts to ease tensions between Iran and the United States continue.

Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey are members of the Mecca Defence Alliance, a security alliance established amid regional instability and concerns over threats linked to Iran and its allies.

The three countries have now turned to direct talks with Tehran about regional security, even as the alliance faces growing pressure from attacks by Yemen’s Iran-aligned Houthi rebels on Saudi Arabia.

Saudi Defence Minister Prince Khalid bin Salman, Pakistani army chief Field Marshal Asim Munir and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin are attending the talks in Tehran on Monday, according to Saudi and Iranian officials.

They are reportedly meeting with Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council.

Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baghaei has described the meeting as a potential step towards establishing cooperative regional security mechanisms.

Speaking at his weekly news conference in Tehran, Baghaei reiterated Iran’s longstanding call for what it describes as "endogenous security," a framework that would rely primarily on the capabilities and interests of regional countries.

Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey signed the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement in August 2026. Under the pact, an armed attack against one member is considered an attack against all three, requiring the others to provide assistance.

The agreement brings together Saudi Arabia’s financial resources and influence in global energy markets, Pakistan’s nuclear capabilities and Turkey’s advanced defence industry and NATO membership.

Although the agreement was signed amid growing concerns over Iran and its allied groups, Turkish officials have insisted that it is not directed against Tehran or any other specific country.

Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan said in an Aug. 8 interview with Anadolu that the agreement did not identify any country as a common threat. He explained that countries would not be considered targets unless they attacked a signatory.

The agreement has received approval in Pakistan, while Turkey has submitted it to parliament but has yet to complete the ratification process.

Escalating missile and drone attacks by Yemen’s Houthis against Saudi Arabia have presented the Mecca alliance with its first major test.

At an emergency meeting in Riyadh on Oct. 5, the three countries agreed to move towards implementing their collective defence commitments, including measures to ensure the rapid deployment of agreed military forces and capabilities to Saudi Arabia.

The commitments were outlined in a joint statement published by Turkey’s Foreign Ministry.

Ankara, however, has drawn a line between supporting Saudi Arabia’s defences and joining offensive military operations against the Houthis inside Yemen.

At an Oct. 8 news conference in Ankara, Fidan said the agreement was defensive and ruled out sending Turkish troops to another country to carry out attacks.

Turkey has instead been assessing possible contributions in areas such as air defence, electronic warfare and technical assistance.

The three nations are therefore pursuing direct engagement with Tehran while strengthening their military readiness under the terms of an alliance designed to deter attacks on its members.

Pakistan has played a mediating role between Tehran and Washington in recent months. Saudi Arabia is seeking to contain the threat posed by Houthi attacks on its territory and infrastructure, while Turkey has sought to prevent further regional escalation.

Saudi Deputy Minister for Public Diplomacy Rayed Krimly said in an Oct. 26 post on X that the Tehran visit was part of continued efforts by the Mecca pact countries to reduce tensions and encourage diplomatic solutions.

Russian overnight strikes injured four people in Zaporizhzhia and sparked fires in the Odesa region on Monday. Ukrainian emergency services said an industrial site was hit twice more as firefighters battled the flames.
… More
In Odesa, firefighters were tackling a blaze at a private industrial facility when Russian forces struck the same location twice more, according to Ukraine’s State Emergency Service. The crews took shelter and escaped injury, but fire and rescue equipment was damaged. More than 50 rescuers were deployed, and authorities reported no casualties in the Odesa attacks.

At a second site in the region, four garages were damaged, a fire broke out and one car was destroyed.

In Zaporizhzhia, the overnight attack damaged private homes, a hotel, two buses and outbuildings. Three cars and dry vegetation caught fire, while rescuers searched the area for anyone trapped.

Acting regional military administration head Mykhailo Semikin initially reported two injured people, but later raised the toll to four injured: two women, aged 22 and 30, and two men, aged 40 and 61.

Ukraine’s Air Force said Russia launched 140 Shahed-type attack drones and decoys overnight, including 70 jet-powered drones. It said air defences shot down or suppressed 112 drones, among them 56 jet-powered aircraft, as well as three Kh-59/69 guided missiles.

Foreign investment is rising, but fewer new projects are being announced and funding remains concentrated in a few economies. At the World Investment Forum in Doha, officials and investors are discussing how more countries can benefit and create jobs.
Foreign investment is rising even as the number of new projects falls, according to a UN report released during the World Investment Forum in Doha.

The ninth edition of the forum, organised by UN Trade and Development, known as UNCTAD, and hosted by Qatar, runs from 25 to 27 October. Governments, businesses and financial institutions are meeting to discuss how more countries can attract investment and turn it into jobs.

Published on Sunday, UNCTAD’s Global Investment Trends Monitor found that larger transactions were helping push up investment totals, even as the number of new projects and cross-border deals fell.

In developing countries, announcements of new projects linked to sustainable development were more than 25% below 2025 levels. These are preliminary estimates, using data from the first six to nine months of 2026 to estimate a full-year total.

More than 80% of global foreign direct investment went to just 20 economies last year, according to UNCTAD’s World Investment Report 2026.

Speaking to Euronews at the forum, UNCTAD spokesperson Marcelo Risi said infrastructure, skills and reliable institutions helped explain why some countries attracted more investment than others.

"Investments are not a short-term endeavour [...] so you need to know which rules are there, who’s your partner, really the rules of the game," Risi explained.

Tax incentives were only one part of the calculation, he said. Countries also need the infrastructure and trained workforce to support businesses once they invested.

Mahmoud Mohieldin, the UN special envoy on financing the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, said promoting a country’s opportunities was not enough to persuade investors.

"I know that governments in particular are expecting a lot from the rest of the world, but a great deal of work at home is required," Mohieldin told Euronews.

He pointed to obstacles in entering and leaving markets, unpredictable regulations and the need to prepare projects that investors could finance.

Mohieldin cited shipping and logistics in small island states, alongside Africa’s young population and natural resources, as advantages that could help attract investment.

In countries investors consider particularly risky, public institutions can help by sharing some of the potential losses.

Rwodah Al Naimi, director of investment at the Qatar Fund for Development, said the fund uses guarantees to encourage private investment, while setting limits on its commitments to protect its own funds.

It also shares the financing with other institutions, working mainly through international development banks.

"We don’t go alone. It has to be co-financed as well with other entities," Al Naimi told Euronews.

Job creation is one of the fund’s main measures of whether its investments were benefiting people.

"So any type of investment that we deploy, we always make sure that there is a job creation element," she explained.

This rare move comes amid strengthened security ties between Algeria and Niamey following the thwarting of an attempted coup in Niger in August.
Algeria has approved sending troops to neighbouring Niger to "protect energy installations", in particular oil projects operated by the state-owned company Sonatrach, marking what will be the country's first foreign deployment of military forces since the 1970s.

The rare measure comes as both countries strengthen security ties months after a thwarted mutiny in Niger, which has seen Algeria gift four military helicopters to Niamey.

The move was approved in a closed extraordinary session of the parliament on Sunday, where 148 of the 174 lawmakers were present in the upper house and voted in favour of the deployment.

The parliament's lower house said it also voted "by an absolute majority" to deploy the military unit in Niger, though it did not provide numbers.

Article 91 of the Algerian constitution stipulates that any deployment of troops abroad must be endorsed by two-thirds of the members of each chamber.

The overseas deployment will be a first since Algiers sent troops to support Egypt during the 1973 Arab-Israeli war.

So far, the authorities have not announced the size of the force to be sent or the start date of its mission, while stressing that the deployment will be limited and focused on protecting Algerian investments.

Sunday's vote followed a visit last Wednesday by Niger's Defence Minister Salifou Modi to Algeria, where he met with President Abdelmadjid Tebboune and the country's army chief Saïd Chengriha.

According to Algerian reports, a military cooperation protocol was signed by the two sides during the visit.

Tebboune had paved the way for this step on 3 October, declaring in a televised address that his country was ready to defend Niger. He said, "We will protect Niger whatever it takes," accusing unnamed states of plotting against it.

After providing the helicopters in August, Algeria dispatched four Su-30 fighter jets accompanied by an in-flight refuelling aircraft, to support Nigerien authorities when a coup attempt was foiled in the same month.

Economic interests are a central driver of Algeria’s move, particularly as Sonatrach expands its investments in Niger through its international subsidiary SIPEX, which manages projects in the Kafra oil field in the north of the country, around 85 kilometres from the Algerian border.

The company began drilling one of its wells in the area on 13 August, as part of a project whose oil resources are estimated, according to figures reported by Anadolu Agency, to exceed 2.5 billion barrels, making it one of Sonatrach’s key investments outside Algeria.

Algeria’s interests go beyond the direct oil sector, as Niger forms a strategic corridor for the Trans-Saharan gas pipeline, which will stretch for more than 4,000 kilometres to carry Nigerian gas to Europe via Niger and Algeria.

Algeria started work in June on the section of the pipeline that crosses its territory, and inaugurated a 40-megawatt power plant to supply Niamey and surrounding areas with electricity.

Tebboune has linked the military moves to economic interests, referring to agreements signed with Niger in the oil and mining sectors and other fields, and warning of actors seeking to undermine these projects.

Observers argue that the military support goes beyond protecting oil installations and reflects a broader shift in Algeria’s approach to regional crises, after decades in which the country relied on diplomacy and dialogue and remained wary of military action beyond its borders.

Relations between Algeria and Niger had been strained for months following Algeria's downing last year of a drone from Mali. The incident triggered a diplomatic row, with Niger joining fellow junta-led allies Mali and Burkina Faso in recalling its ambassador from Algiers.

The neighbours, who share a 960-kilometre desert border, have, however, in recent months committed to enhanced economic and security cooperation that includes combatting jihadist violence, cross-border crime and trafficking.

Since July 2023, Niger has been ruled by a military junta led by General Abdourahamane Tiani, who deposed elected president Mohamed Bazoum, held in custody ever since, while the country continues to grapple with security turmoil and ongoing armed attacks.

Trump administration officials are reportedly floating former Hungarian prime minister Viktor Orbán for UN secretary-general, potentially backed by the US, Russia and China, though nothing is confirmed and his selection remains uncertain.
Officials in the Trump administration are advocating the nomination of former Hungarian prime minister Viktor Orbán for the post of UN secretary-general, according to a report by the Wall Street Journal.

The newspaper reports that Orbán could be a joint American, Russian and Chinese candidate, and that sources in all three countries have confirmed the Trump administration is backing his bid.

Orbán lost power in Hungary’s parliamentary election in April, when Péter Magyar’s Tisza party defeated him after 16 years of governing with an absolute majority.

His hard-right, fiercely anti-migration and eurosceptic policies, together with the rule of law backsliding in Hungary, strained relations with Western allies, including the European Union and NATO. Nevertheless, Orbán maintained close ties with Donald Trump, Vladimir Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping.

Since the election, Hungarian authorities have launched fraud and corruption investigations into several members of Orbán’s former government. Two former ministers and the former governor of Hungary’s National Bank are under arrest, and authorities could also examine Orbán’s potential involvement in those cases.

According to the WSJ, no final decision has been taken on the plan. A US State Department official admitted that the idea sounds crazy at first, but said previous talks with Washington suggest Russia and China also support it. The aim is for Orbán to be the joint candidate of the three countries.

“If the United States, Russia and China want the same thing in the UN Security Council, they usually achieve it,” officials from the United States and another country told the newspaper. The second five-year term of the current secretary-general, António Guterres, ends on 31 December.

According to Russian deputy foreign minister Aleksandr Alimov, the US, China or Russia will not nominate Orbán, as permanent members of the UN's Security Council can not do so.

"Neither Russia, the United States, nor China will nominate Orbán because we are permanent members of the UN Security Council and we do not nominate anyone for the position of UN Secretary-General," Alimov told the Interfax news agency, adding that if press reports are true, those behind the idea need to find a nominating country.

"We will discuss all the people officially nominated for the post of Secretary General" Alimov noted.

Euronews has contacted Orbán’s spokesperson for comment but has not yet received a response. The spokesperson previously dismissed press reports about Orbán’s candidacy for the post of deputy secretary-general as “fake news”.

In May, the VSquare investigative newsletter reported that a UN position could be important for the former prime minister because of the diplomatic immunity attached to the role.

According to the WSJ, Orbán would only run if his victory were assured, a scenario that currently seems unlikely.

A candidate must secure the support of at least nine of the 15 Security Council members, while any of the five permanent members can veto the nomination. Besides the US, Russia and China, France and the United Kingdom are also permanent members.

Two European governments have already spoken out against the idea of putting forward Orbán as a potential candidate.

"I do not foresee Poland supporting Viktor Orbán's candidacy for UN Secretary-General," Polish Foreign Minister Radosław Sikorski said in a social media post.

"Viktor Orbán, as UN Secretary-General, would put to good use the masterful veto and blackmail skills he has developed over years of derailing major EU decisions."

From army exemptions for the ultra-Orthodox to a stalled 7 October inquiry, the issues splitting voters have little to do with Gaza, Lebanon or Iran.
Israelis head to the polls on Tuesday in the country’s first national election since Hamas’s 7 October 2023 attack, a vote that is shaping up as a verdict on Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu.

Yet for a country that has spent three years fighting on several fronts, the campaign has been strikingly quiet on how those wars were fought.

Israel’s conduct in Gaza, which led to an international arrest warrant for Netanyahu, has barely been debated. Nor have its occupation of much of southern Lebanon and parts of Syria, or the two wars it launched against Iran. All enjoy broad support among Jewish Israelis.

The main contenders have instead argued over Netanyahu himself: his character, his responsibility for the security failures of 7 October, military exemptions for ultra-Orthodox men and his 2023 attempt to overhaul the judiciary.

There is also little to separate the two main blocs on the Palestinian question. Both are dominated by parties that back Jewish settlement in the occupied West Bank and oppose a Palestinian state, and no leading candidate is calling for a revival of the peace process.

Reminders of the war are everywhere. Stickers bearing the faces of fallen soldiers line train stations, alongside fading yellow ribbons for the hostages, the last of whom came home under a ceasefire deal last year.

According to the Israel Democracy Institute (IDI), a Jerusalem-based think tank, around 68% of Jewish Israelis say their views on 7 October are central to how they will vote. Among Arab citizens, the figure is 32%, with most naming crime as their main concern.

Netanyahu, Israel’s longest-serving prime minister, has pointed to military gains against Hamas, Hezbollah and Iran while placing blame for the 7 October failures on the security establishment.

His critics say he has dodged accountability by blocking an official state inquiry. They also accuse him of delaying a hostage deal and of failing to secure a decisive victory on any front.

The issue that has stirred the most anger is conscription. Military service is compulsory for most Jewish Israelis, but Netanyahu’s coalitions have long depended on ultra-Orthodox parties that insist yeshiva students be exempt.

Resentment has grown as reservists have been called up repeatedly and the army struggles with a manpower shortage. Around 13,000 ultra-Orthodox men turn 18 each year, but fewer than one in ten enlist.

About a quarter of Jewish voters told the IDI the issue would weigh on their choice. IDI president Yohanan Plesner said it was “probably the number one issue” for the anti-Netanyahu camp.

The IDI’s Tamar Hermann said many Israelis see military service as a “kind of permit to citizenship”.

Netanyahu’s main challenger, former army chief Gadi Eisenkot, has pledged to end the de facto exemptions. Eisenkot, a centrist, lost his son and two nephews in the fighting in Gaza.

The judicial overhaul brought hundreds of thousands onto the streets in 2023 and left Israel deeply divided when Hamas attacked. Netanyahu cast it as a check on unelected judges. Opponents saw a power grab by a leader under indictment on corruption charges, which he denies.

After 7 October, much of that protest movement turned its energy towards demanding the hostages’ release. Today, only 18% of Jewish Israelis say the overhaul will shape their vote, a sign of what Hermann described as “public fatigue”. It matters more on the left, where 22.5% cite it.

A law passed last year giving the government more say in judicial appointments will only take effect once the next Knesset is sworn in. The opposition bloc has promised to scrap it.

Israelis vote for party lists rather than individual candidates, and no party is expected to come close to the 61 seats needed for a majority in the 120-seat Knesset. The next prime minister will be whoever can assemble a governing coalition. If no one can, Netanyahu will stay on as caretaker until yet another election.

More than a quarter of Jewish voters say the party leader is a decisive factor. Netanyahu’s backers see a seasoned statesman who has stood up to US presidents and refused concessions they believe would endanger Israel. His detractors see him as corrupt.

Eisenkot’s supporters cite his decades in uniform and his personal losses. The son of Moroccan Jewish immigrants, he would become Israel’s first prime minister of non-European heritage, though he has not campaigned on it.

Living costs are a major concern but have barely featured. Research by Sarit Menahem-Carmi of Reichman University’s Aaron Institute for Economic Policy found that Israel is now more expensive than wealthy European countries such as Austria, Denmark and Sweden.

Wearing extravagant headdresses, glittering gowns and painted skull faces transformed Mexico City's Paseo de la Reforma into a colourful procession on Sunday, 25 October, as thousands took part in the annual Catrinas Parade.

Dancers, couples dressed as skeletal brides and grooms, and even costumed dogs made their way through cheering crowds. Some participants wore illuminated outfits, while others displayed elaborate embroidery and striking headpieces. Make-up artists worked behind the scenes to create the distinctive Catrina look, with full face designs taking between 20 and 40 minutes.

The parade celebrates La Catrina, a skeletal figure created by illustrator José Guadalupe Posada more than a century ago and later popularised by artist Diego Rivera. Once a satire of European-inspired fashion, she has become one of Mexico's best-known cultural symbols.

The event forms part of the build-up to Día de los Muertos, celebrated mainly on 1 and 2 November, when families honour deceased relatives with altars, flowers, candles and offerings.

Mexico City is preparing an even larger parade on 31 October, with more than 4,000 performers, 12 floats and two giant balloons expected. The capital's Zócalo will also feature a 32-metre Catrina surrounded by millions of marigolds.

The festivities combine music, creativity and remembrance, turning the commemoration of the dead into a celebration of life.

Lula broke with his unfavourable record since 1989, winning the runoff after losing the first round, crowning a career that took him from metalworker to Brazil's presidency.
After losing in the first round, Lula da Silva pulled off a historic comeback to be re-elected president of Brazil in the second round. Back in the Palácio do Planalto, which he had never left, the leader of the Workers' Party (PT) defied unfavourable precedent: since 1989, the year when Brazil returned to direct presidential elections and introduced the two-round system**,** no candidate trailing after the first round had ever gone on to win the second.

"I owe this to you. I have always thought that God has been very generous to me, because there are so many people better prepared than I am, so many people with more diplomas than I have, so many people who have read more books than I have, so many people who have studied more than I have," he said in his victory speech, his fourth after a presidential election.

It was a first for Lula, too. In 1989, he finished 13.3 points behind his rival Fernando Collor de Mello in the first round and failed to close the gap in the second.

From factory worker to trade unionist, from trade unionist to politician, from politician to president of Brazil. Nothing has ever seemed impossible for Lula, who once again showed with this election that adversity is not always insurmountable.

"I want to tell you that I am enormously proud. First, because I am the only president of the Republic, a metalworker without a university degree, elected four times, democratically, to call for peace," he told the crowd.

"The day after tomorrow, on the 27th, I will turn 81. And I want to make a pledge in front of the press and in front of you. I am going to deliver the best term any president has ever served in the history of this country," he vowed.

The seventh child of illiterate farmer parents, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva was born on 27 October 1945 in the Brazilian city of Garanhuns, in the north-eastern state of Pernambuco. He was only seven when he moved with his family to São Paulo, and 14 when he was admitted to a technical course in metalworking. Before that, he had already worked as a street vendor, shoeshine boy, and dye-shop assistant. He went on to become a metalworker, a profession he later defended as a trade unionist.

"Lula" began as a nickname and was later officially incorporated into his name – he is the epitome of the worker who becomes a politician: it was as a union leader and organiser of metalworkers' strikes in the São Paulo region, during the military dictatorship, that he made his name. During his years as a metalworker, he lost a finger in a workplace accident, which later earned him the disparaging nickname “nine fingers” among opponents.

It was these strikes, which he led between 1978 and 1980, that paved the way for the founding of the Workers' Party (PT) in 1980. The party would go on to emerge as the main force on Brazil's left after the country's democratisation between 1985 and 1988, with Lula as its leading figure.

After losing the presidential elections of 1989, 1994 and 1998, he finally reached the presidency in 2002, where he remained for two terms. He was the first trade union leader and manual worker to enter the Palácio do Planalto.

Supporters of his political legacy point to the transformation of a country marked by extreme poverty and illiteracy into a more prosperous and more equal Brazil, through social policy measures such as the introduction of the "Bolsa Família" in 2003 and the creation of the "Zero Hunger" programme.

Lula ended his first term with an approval rating of 57% and was re-elected in 2006 with more than 58 million votes, the highest tally in Brazil's history.

The results would ultimately bolster Lula's narrative that the state can play a decisive role in reducing poverty and hunger. In 2014, under the government of Dilma Rousseff, Lula's political successor, Brazil left the United Nations' Hunger Map after a significant reduction in undernourishment in the country. The shift was linked to a package of social and economic policies.

Politically, he has always presented himself as a defender of multilateralism, diplomacy and an international order in which decisions are not determined solely by the power of the major powers.

"War is not the continuation of politics by other means. It is the total failure of politics. In an interdependent world, to lead is to reconcile the interests of the homeland with those of humanity. That is the reflection of someone who represents a nation that cherishes peace and freedom. Someone who fought against a military dictatorship and defended his country against an attempted coup. It is the conviction of a worker elected three times as president who took Brazil off the Hunger Map," he declared on 22 September 2026 in his speech to the 81st United Nations General Assembly in New York.

But the corruption scandals engulfing Lula and the PT would catch up with him, temporarily curtailing his political career.

The "Car Wash" investigation and subsequent trials led by judge Sérgio Moro (later Bolsonaro's justice minister) dominated the second half of the 2010s. It brought down numerous heavyweights and sent dozens of politicians from across the spectrum to prison. The investigation, which exposed a complex bribery and kickback scheme centred on the semi-state oil company Petrobras and the construction giant Odebrecht, was central to fuelling distrust of Brazil's political class and the rise of right-wing populism embodied by Jair Bolsonaro.

Lula was jailed in March 2018, after being sentenced to nine years in prison for allegedly receiving a flat in exchange for political favours. After 580 days in custody, Lula was released in November 2019, following a change in Supreme Federal Court case law on when a sentence may begin to be served.

In 2021, the Supreme Court went further and annulled the former president's convictions in the case, ruling that the court in Curitiba lacked jurisdiction to try him. This cleared the way for the PT founder to return to the presidential race, which he went on to win in 2022.

His victory in the 2026 election marks a new phase in a political story defined by his ability to return to power, even if the road ahead now appears somewhat steeper.

The Brazil Lula now governs is not the one he led in the 2000s: the country remains deeply polarised, Congress is dominated by the centre-right and Bolsonarism still commands significant electoral strength.

Lula's triumph bucks a regional trend of victories for right-of-center presidential candidates allied with Trump. It also means Latin America’s largest economy will likely continue defying Trump’s demands, despite the pressure of increased tariffs.
Brazil’s President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva pulled off a dramatic comeback on Sunday as he clinched a re-election victory with a lead of nearly 4.4 million votes over opponent Flávio Bolsonaro, giving him 51.8% of the total vote.

The 81-year-old leftist defied projections to win an unprecedented fourth term, staving off a conservative wave that has seen numerous South American countries, such as Argentina, Chile, Colombia, and Peru, all shift right, backed by US President Donald Trump.

Bolsonaro quickly refused to concede defeat and asked for the election results to be annulled after an evening of chaos at some polling places that followed a last-minute decision to extend voting by two hours.

The senator is the son of former far-right president Jair Bolsonaro, who is serving a 27-year jail term for attempting a coup after his loss to Lula in the country's last election in 2022.

Lula had requested the extension after he complained that buses had been pulled from circulation in various cities, barring citizens from voting.

Flavio Bolsonaro also requested an extension in some regions due to heavy rains, but he described the decision as "unacceptable" and a "dirty trick".

"It is impossible to recognise the results. I am going to request the annulment of these elections," he said in a brief speech in Brasília.

After a first-round defeat, the left swung into action with massive rallies, declaring "Never Again Bolsonaro" and revamped their social media strategy.

No candidate who lost the first round has gone on to win the second in Brazil since 1989, the first direct election after the end of the country's military dictatorship.

The abstention rate decreased from the first round, falling from 21 percent to 19 percent.

"People went to vote because we managed to convince them that voting was very important for ensuring democracy in the country," Lula said in his first comments after the win.

Fireworks and vuvuzelas rang out in Rio de Janeiro, while thousands of people dressed in red, the color of Lula's Workers' Party, streamed into the streets of Sao Paulo to celebrate.

Lula's campaign leaned heavily on the theme of sovereignty in the face of commercial and diplomatic pressure from Trump's United States.

“Either the homeland remains free, or we die for Brazil,” he said. “This country will not accept being anyone’s backyard," he said as he addressed his supporters.

In Rio de Janeiro, cars beeped their horns and a woman dressed in red — the color of Lula supporters — shouted “You lost! Cry harder!” to four men in yellow and green sipping beers, Bolsonaro's signature colors. A huge roar echoed in the street as the result was confirmed.

Lula heads into a fourth term with almost half of the nation having rejected him, desperate for change.

Lula made a dramatic return to office in 2022 elections after a corruption scandal saw him jailed for over 18 months. The charges were annulled on procedural grounds.

In his third term, Lula oversaw a drop in inflation and unemployment, and lifted millions out of hunger, but many voters felt gloomy about the high cost of living and crime.

Already one of the world's oldest leaders, Lula will be 85 by the end of his new term in 2030.
