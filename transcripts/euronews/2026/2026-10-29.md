Spain is mourning 69 people killed in a high-speed train collision near Toledo. As King Felipe VI visits a hospital treating the injured, rescuers continue searching the wreckage and a minute of silence is held at the crash site.
… More
Opposition People's Party leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo visited the scene, while King Felipe VI travelled to a hospital in Toledo treating survivors of the disaster. The monarch was greeted by hospital staff and other people waiting outside before entering the building.

Meanwhile, rescue teams continued searching the wreckage with sniffer dogs, as cranes lifted damaged carriages and emergency workers combed through debris scattered down a gravel embankment. One more body was recovered overnight, bringing the death toll to 69, according to Castilla-La Mancha regional president Emiliano García-Page, who said 50 people remained in hospital.

The collision occurred on Wednesday morning when a high-speed train operated by state-owned Renfe struck the rear of a stationary Iryo service in central Spain. At least three carriages were thrown down an embankment by the impact.

The government declared three days of national mourning from Thursday, as the country's second deadly high-speed rail disaster this year raised fresh questions about railway safety.

In January, 46 people were killed in another collision involving Renfe and Iryo trains on the same line. The latest crash, just four weeks before a national election, has also disrupted rail travel, with the high-speed route between Madrid and Seville closed and thousands of passengers seeking alternative transport.

President Tokayev outlined the country's priorities for the coming year in his annual address, urging the government to accelerate energy and construction projects, while criticising inefficiencies in public policy.
Kazakh President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev called for a long-term national uranium reserve and faster progress on building two nuclear power plants (NPPs) in his annual state-of-the-nation address on Wednesday.

“I believe it is possible, and perhaps even necessary, to begin construction of the Balkhash NPP as early as next year, and to sign a comprehensive cooperation agreement with the goal of launching construction of the Moyynkum NPP as soon as possible,” he said.

Tokayev highlighted that nuclear energy is one way to achieve an electricity surplus, which he described as essential for digitalising the economy and integrating artificial intelligence into all areas of public life.

He also criticised the government over inefficient energy management and corruption schemes in the sector. During winter shortages, Kazakhstan buys electricity from neighbouring countries at high prices, only to sell it back at much lower rates in summer.

“It’s a rather simple sort of ‘business’, and one that has persisted for decades. I’m assured that this is standard practice in the energy sector,” Tokayev said.

He added that such practices had also led to the deliberate obstruction of power plant construction and commissioning in several regions.

Tokayev opened his address by pointing to an increasingly unpredictable geopolitical environment, which he said “did not exist even during the Cold War."

Growing vulnerabilities along maritime routes, he argued, were increasing the importance of overland transport corridors.

“Kazakhstan must seize this historic opportunity to truly become a leading logistics hub for Eurasia,” he said.

He pointed to the construction of the Aral-Caspian Highway, which began in August. The project is expected to shorten the Trans-Caspian International Transport Route by 1,000 kilometres and reduce cargo delivery times to three days.

The Bakty–Ayagoz railway line is scheduled to launch next year. It will establish a third railway crossing with China and increase freight capacity by 20 million tonnes.

Tokayev said Kazakhstan needed to move beyond its role as a transit country and become an organiser of international freight flows.

He called for developing domestic logistics operators, creating distribution centres, and digitising the transport sector, while also instructing the government to focus on air transit and road infrastructure.

According to the IMF's forecast, Kazakhstan's GDP will exceed €350 billion this year, making it the world's 47th-largest economy. GDP per capita is expected to surpass €17,000.

The industrial sector now accounts for more than 14% of GDP. Tokayev attributed the growth to efforts to diversify the economy.

Small and medium-sized businesses contribute 40% of GDP, while their share of fixed capital investment has approached 70%.

Kazakhstan's gold and foreign exchange reserves, combined with the National Fund's assets, have exceeded €110 billion. Tokayev said the country should use these resources to modernise infrastructure and improve living conditions across its regions.

Domestic bank assets have surpassed €130 billion, while savings accounts have reached a record €94 billion. Around 78% of these savings are held in the national currency, which Tokayev cited as a sign of the tenge's growing value.

The president designated next year as the Year of Nationwide Construction, calling on parliament to prioritise building and modernising public infrastructure, including roads, airports, hospitals and cultural centres.

The regions plan to build around 70 medical facilities and 50 educational, sports and cultural facilities. The National Fund is expected to allocate approximately €12.8 billion for these and other infrastructure projects by the end of 2029. Nearly €3 billion of that amount is earmarked for social projects over the next four months.

Tokayev also highlighted the country's demographic profile, noting that Kazakhstan has around seven million children, accounting for 34% of the population. More than 237,000 children have special educational needs.

Qatar has denied interfering in the election or funding efforts to influence the result, calling the accusations “ridiculous and dangerous”.
Benjamin Netanyahu and his Likud party are alleging election interference and fraud after Israel’s election results left his bloc short of a parliamentary majority.

The final count, expected on Thursday evening, is set to confirm that Netanyahu's bloc has fallen short of the 61 seats needed for a majority. With around 90% of votes counted, Netanyahu’s bloc was projected to win 53 seats, the opposition led by Gadi Eisenkot 48, and Arab-led parties 19.

The result represents major gains for the Arab-led parties, potentially giving them a decisive role in determining whether a government without Netanyahu can be formed.

The election saw record participation by Arab citizens of Israel, many of whom are descendants of Palestinians who remained after the country's creation in 1948. The campaign had seen Netanyahu and his far-right allies repeatedly portray Arab-led parties as a threat.

Netanyahu, who has served as Israel's prime minister on and off since 1996, has accused Arab parties of benefiting from foreign interference and funding. Neither he nor his allies have publicly provided evidence to substantiate those claims.

In a post on the social media platform X, Netanyahu quoted his Likud party, saying: "The elections in the State of Israel were stolen by Arab parties that support terrorism and the elimination of the Jewish state through serious criminal offences of bribery, threats, violence, forgeries, and the use of prohibited software to mobilise voters."

In another post on Thursday morning, he wrote: "Why did Ahmad Tibi visit Qatar 4 days before the elections?" Ahmad Tibi is a Palestinian-Israeli politician and a senior figure in the Ta'al party.

Israel's Central Election Commission has asked the Shin Bet security service to investigate alleged foreign interference. However, the request itself reportedly did not specifically accuse Arab-led parties of wrongdoing. AP also reported that the commission said there was no basis, to its knowledge, for claims of systematic violations of election integrity.

The Arab-led Joint List rejected the accusations, saying Netanyahu’s camp was trying to delegitimise the Arab vote because of its increased political influence. It accused Netanyahu of "working to steal the election".

Ra’am leader Mansour Abbas said he welcomed an investigation into allegations of wrongdoing but said his party had "no connection" with them.

Netanyahu himself has previously faced scrutiny over alleged links between aides and Qatar, after two of his aides were arrested last year on suspicion of receiving payments from the Gulf state.

Qatar has categorically denied interfering in the election. Its Foreign Ministry spokesman, Majed Al Ansari, said: "They accuse Qatar of buying off Benjamin Netanyahu, the Israeli political left and now, in this latest campaign, the Arab vote. These accusations are ridiculous and dangerous in equal measure."

Israel's President Isaac Herzog has also called for a full investigation, while warning against stigmatising the country’s entire Arab community.

The full count is expected on Thursday evening, although the official election results are due to be certified by 4 November.

If no coalition can be formed after several attempts, Israel could face another election early next year.

The political uncertainty is unfolding alongside the possibility of renewed Israeli or US strikes on Iran, which could further complicate coalition negotiations.

From silk-lined limousines to vehicles fitted with bulletproof glass, eight historic popemobiles go on display at the Vatican Museums. The exhibition traces how papal transport has evolved over nearly a century.
… More
The exhibition brings together vehicles used by successive popes, from an opulent 1930 limousine to modern models designed to bring the pontiff closer to crowds while protecting him.

The oldest is a Nürburg 460 presented to Pope Pius XI on 23 May 1930, featuring a luxurious armchair upholstered in raspberry-coloured silk brocade. By 1960, the 300 d Landaulet built for John XXIII had removable rear side windows, allowing him to interact more easily with crowds. Five years later, the 600 Pullman Landaulet introduced a raised roof and folding soft top, enabling Paul VI to stand and greet pilgrims more directly.

The modern popemobile took shape in 1980, when Mercedes-Benz developed the 230 G for John Paul II's visit to Germany. Its tall transparent enclosure offered the pontiff protection while keeping him visible to the public. Later models, including the ML 430 from 2002 and ML 500 from 2012, reflect the continuing evolution of papal transport, with security features such as thick bulletproof glass.

The Vatican's relationship with Mercedes-Benz has also entered the electric era, with a modified G 580 becoming its first fully electric popemobile in 2024. Although other manufacturers have supplied vehicles to popes, this exhibition focuses exclusively on Mercedes-Benz. Vatican Museums director Barbara Jatta says the exhibition aims to showcase a century of papal journeys. Entitled A Century of the Popemobile, it runs until 31 January 2027.

One of the things that the region, Brussels, Moscow and China will be watching is how the new government positions itself. But there are already fairly clear signals from the leaders of the Students’ List.

Jovo Bakić, sociologist and one of the most prominent figures on the Student List, has explicitly said that a new government would not impose sanctions on Moscow. He described Russia as a historically important partner for Serbia, arguing that sanctions imposed by a small country such as Serbia would have little practical effect while carrying considerable symbolic consequences.

Academician of the Serbian Academy of Sciences and Arts and professor at the University of Belgrade’s Faculty of Chemistry, Radomir Saičić, told RIA Novosti that the Students’ List does not plan to impose sanctions on Russia.

“As we stated in our programme, the Students’ List does not advocate imposing sanctions against Russia,” Saičić said.

He noted that Serbia itself had been subjected to sanctions and argued that such measures do not solve problems.

“Problems should be resolved through dialogue, negotiations and agreements, not sanctions,” Saičić said.

Dejan Šoškić, a candidate on the “Students Win” list, and posible new prime minister said that Serbia must develop good-neighbourly relations with Croatia, while noting that there would probably continue to be significant differences in the general perception of what happened in the 1990s, and that the Students’ List does not concern itself with that issue. In an interview with Index.hr, Šoškić announced better relations with Croatia and the European Union, while he did not answer a question about the new government’s position towards Russia.

“The Students’ List advocates full respect for the legal framework in Serbia, including international law. The legal framework in Serbia is therefore quite clear, because the Constitution states that Kosovo and Metohija is an autonomous province of Serbia. On the other hand, key documents of the international legal framework, such as United Nations Security Council Resolution 1244, also affirm this,” Šoškić said.

“I believe that Serbia will certainly be in a position to fulfil the EU criteria much more easily and quickly. Whether it will join the European Union at some point after fulfilling all the conditions will depend, among other things, on whether the European Union offers Serbia full membership, as well as on whether, in a referendum at the end of that process, the citizens of Serbia decide in favour of that option,” Šoškić added.

It is already clear that when it comes to Serbia’s relations with Russia, the new government, just like the previous one, has no intention of aligning itself with Brussels’ policy. European Commissioner for Enlargement Marta Kos spoke with Ilija Srdanović, the lead candidate of the Students’ List, and immediately stressed that a change of government in Belgrade does not remove the obligation to align with the EU’s foreign and security policy. Brussels has indicated that it will assess the future government’s concrete actions, including on the basis of its relations with Moscow.

Marta Kos said that “Serbia will not be able to make significant progress towards EU accession unless it aligns its foreign and security policy with Brussels, including its position on Russia and the sanctions imposed on Moscow over the war in Ukraine.”

The European Commissioner, responding to a question about whether Serbia could become an EU member without joining sanctions against Russia, said that it would be very difficult for a country that does not share the EU’s strategic foreign-policy positions to make progress.

“It will probably not be possible for a country that does not align with us on strategic interests — namely foreign and security policy, or our joint opposition to Russia, particularly in the context of Russia’s war in Ukraine — to make progress. It will be very, very difficult,” Kos said.

The official postition of the Student's list contradicts one of former President Aleksandar Vučić’s central warnings during the campaign: that a Student List victory would eventually mean a dramatic change in Serbia’s policy towards Moscow in terms of imposing sanctions.

For now, there is no indication that sanctions against Russia are about to become part of Serbia’s foreign policy. Given the response from the new authorities, we can conclude that it will be business as usual.

The Student List’s programme also retains Serbia’s policy of military neutrality and rejects NATO membership. Its position on Kosovo remains firmly opposed to Kosovo’s unilateral declaration of independence and to its membership of international organisations.

You could search with a magnifying glass for any difference in policy towards Kosovo between old and new power in Belgrade, and you would not find one. That elephant will therefore remain in the room when it comes to Serbia’s diplomatic relations with the West. We can expect tensions to ease, along with a shift away from harsh rhetoric and threats, but there will be no fundamental change.

This means that the arrival of new government in Belgrade should not automatically be interpreted as a geopolitical realignment in the traditional sense. Professor Radomir Saičić on Kosovo said:

"For us, Kosovo is a temporarily occupied territory. Russia has done more to defend Serbia’s territorial integrity in Kosovo and Metohija than this regime has. Fake patriots accuse the students of lacking patriotism, but it was not the students who signed the Brussels and Washington Agreements, nor did they accept the Franco-German plan."

The student movement is essentially a “big tent” movement, bringing together people with very different worldviews who were united by their opposition to the rule of Aleksandar Vučić and their criticism of the nature of his government, rather than an ideologically clearly defined alliance.

The more likely scenario is a complicated balancing act: closer political and economic cooperation with the European Union, while maintaining military neutrality, avoiding sanctions against Russia and preserving existing energy and diplomatic links with Moscow.

Brazil's defeated right-wing presidential candidate Flávio Bolsonaro insisted Wednesday he is his country's rightful leader, after fleeing to the United States to start a self-imposed exile.

Bolsonaro's defiance on arrival in Florida signaled continued upheaval for Brazil, where leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva won a dramatic runoff election on Sunday.

"I am the elected president, and the criminals who stole Brazil's election are part of a criminal organization that has established a tyranny," Bolsonaro said in the statement published on his X account, slamming "electoral fraud."

"I will lead the opposition from here," he said. In a subsequent statement, he revised his claim, saying he is the "leader," rather than "president."

Bolsonaro touched down earlier Wednesday in a private jet at Opa-Locka Executive Airport near Miami.

US President Donald Trump's hard-right administration has strong links to Bolsonaro and his father Jair Bolsonaro, who is under house arrest in Brazil after attempting to overturn his own electoral loss to Lula in 2022.

Trump reiterated his stand that Brazil's election was "very strange" but told reporters he "won't get involved" in Flavio Bolsonaro's claim to the presidency.

Details of how Bolsonaro, accompanied by his wife and daughters, made it to US soil are still emerging.

He is under investigation in his home country over alleged corruption and money laundering. With his current parliamentary immunity about to expire, he was ordered to surrender his passports but left anyway, reaching the United States via Argentina and Ecuador.

Spain reels from the deadly Toledo high-speed train collision that killed 68 and injured more than 100, sparking grief, protests and calls for resignations.
… More
As night fell over the crash site near Urda, rescue teams continued working among the mangled carriages, using heavy cranes to lift sections of wreckage from the tracks. Footage shows a crane hoisting what appears to be part of a train's wheel assembly, while emergency workers search the damaged carriages under artificial lighting.

The disaster, which occurred when one train struck another that had stopped, is Spain's second deadly rail collision this year and one of Europe's deadliest railway accidents of the century.

In Madrid, dozens gathered outside City Hall in Cibeles Square for a minute of silence in memory of the victims. The tribute, organised by the Spanish Federation of Municipalities and Provinces, was attended by opposition leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo, Madrid regional president Isabel Díaz Ayuso, Mayor José Luis Martínez-Almeida and central government delegate Pilar Trinidad. But the gathering also became a protest against the government, with some participants holding placards reading "Corruption kills" and chanting for Transport Minister Óscar Puente to resign.

At least 68 people have died and more than 100 have been injured in a collision between two high-speed trains near Toledo, central Spain, on 28 October. Fifteen of the injured remain in critical condition.
