A 15-year-old student disguised herself in the target school's uniform before attacking pupils at Şeyh Şamil Imam Hatip Secondary School in Başakşehir on Tuesday morning. The suspect, who attends a different school, was apprehended.
A 15-year-old student stabbed eight pupils at a secondary school in Istanbul's Başakşehir district on Tuesday morning, killing one, Turkish officials said.

The attacker disguised herself in the uniform of the school she targeted before carrying out the attack at Şeyh Şamil Imam Hatip Secondary School in Istanbul's Başakşehir district, Education Minister Yusuf Tekin said.

The attack took place at around 8:35 am local time (7:35 am CEST), according to the district governor. Authorities apprehended the suspect, identified as a 15-year-old 10th-grade student at a different institution.

Health Minister Kemal Memişoğlu said eight students were injured, one critically and two in a moderate-to-serious condition.

"The treatment of our eight wounded ... is continuing in our hospitals, and we are closely monitoring their health status," he said. One student subsequently died, Tekin confirmed.

Istanbul's 3rd Criminal Court of Peace imposed a broadcasting ban on coverage of the incident, with Turkey's media regulator RTÜK citing the need to protect the investigation and "ensure the arrest of suspects".

Imam hatip schools are state-funded religious secondary schools originally established to train imams and prayer leaders.

Under President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan's government, the schools now enrol a substantial share of Turkish secondary students.

Critics have argued the expansion has come at the expense of secular education. There are no indications at this time that the attack was politically or ideologically motivated.

Darya Shipilova, 28, a researcher at the Irkutsk Anti-Plague Research Institute, was admitted to Shelekhov District Hospital in late September and died last Friday night.

Russia's national health watchdog Rospotrebnadzor said biological samples from her body found no microorganisms linked to her professional work and that she died from "pneumonia of unknown aetiology."

The agency said its special commissions had found no accidents involving pathogenic microorganisms at the institute. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov told reporters to rely solely on Rospotrebnadzor's statements.

Between 189 and 197 of Shipilova's contacts were placed under observation and quarantine, according to figures cited by independent and regional media.

Initial tests among those contacts returned negative. More than 60 staff members of the anti-plague institute were effectively confined inside the facility for several days.

Quarantine restrictions were also imposed on hospitals in Irkutsk, including a maternity ward, according to reporting by multiple regional and international outlets.

Patients at one facility said doctors cited plague as the reason. Relatives of a woman in labour were refused entry to the ward despite having paid for partner-assisted birth.

The head of neighbouring Buryatia, Alexei Tsydenov, initially said after Shipilova's death that she had died of plague, then amended this to "possibly plague".

Irkutsk regional authorities posted a message on Friday asking residents not to travel to the satellite town of Shelekhov because of plague, then deleted it.

Governor Igor Kobzev described the cause of death as "pneumonia of unknown aetiology" on Monday.

The World Health Organization said it assessed the public health risk from the incident as low to moderate for Irkutsk region, low for Russia as a whole and very low for the WHO European region.

It said Russian authorities had identified Shipilova's contacts and were monitoring them, and that they had detected no dangerous infectious disease agents among those contacts.

The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control told Euronews it was "closely following the situation" and was in contact with the WHO.

"We are aware of a case of pneumonia of unknown origin in eastern Russia that is suspected to be plague. This suspicion has not yet been confirmed," an ECDC representative said.

US authorities are also monitoring developments, while Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan have strengthened sanitary controls at their borders with Russia.

Uzbekistan has also strengthened its public-health preparedness after reports of a suspected case of a particularly dangerous infectious disease in Russia’s Irkutsk region, while stressing that the diagnosis has not yet been confirmed.

The Iran war has disrupted global trade as regional powers prepare to bolster Saudi Arabia’s defences. Qatar said talks to end the war remained active and backed the Mecca defence alliance, but added that it was too early to discuss joining the pact.
Qatar warned on Tuesday that the “whole world is paying” for the Iran war and called for an immediate end to the conflict through negotiations.

“I can confirm that the negotiations are ongoing,” Ibrahim bin Sultan Ibrahim Al Hashmi, director of the foreign ministry’s Media and Communication Department, told a press briefing in Doha.

“Contacts and visits are ongoing with the aim of making progress in the talks between Washington and Tehran," Al Hashmi said.

“Qatar is open to a visit from the Iranian side to learn about the latest developments regarding the Iranian position,” he added.

Last week, foreign ministry spokesperson Majed Al-Ansari described the strain on the global economy as “unbearable”, warning that the disruption extended beyond oil and gas to supplies including fertiliser ingredients, helium and aluminium.

He said Doha was working to restore freedom of navigation through the Strait of Hormuz as part of efforts to reach an agreement between Washington and Tehran.

The war, which began with joint US-Israeli strikes on Iran in late February, has severely disrupted shipping through the strait -- a crucial route for Gulf exports -- after both Iran and the US imposed their own naval blockades.

Al Hashmi also backed the Mecca defence alliance among Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan, adding that it was "too early to talk about Qatar joining" the military pact.

His comments followed an emergency meeting in Riyadh on Monday, where the three countries agreed to activate their collective defence commitments and rapidly deploy agreed forces and military capabilities to Saudi Arabia.

Al Hashmi condemned what he said was Yemen's Houthis’ irresponsible targeting of Saudi Arabia, Islamic holy sites and civilian facilities, and said Doha “supports Saudi measures to preserve sovereignty and security.”

The Houthis have denied Saudi claims of attacks on Islam's holiest sites in Mecca and Medina.

Qatar’s Emir Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani met Iranian Interior Minister Eskandar Momeni in Doha on Tuesday, with Qatar's news agency saying the meeting focused on regional developments and efforts to ease tensions.

Momeni also met his Qatari counterpart, with Iran's semi-official Tasnim news agency reporting that the talks focused on the fate of three Iranian military pilots that Tehran claims are held by Doha since the war.

Iran has alleged that three pilots were detained after their military aircraft was downed in Qatari airspace in March. Doha denies holding them and says search teams recovered the remains of one pilot.

Momeni said a technical delegation would hold further meetings on the issue following the talks in Doha, Iranian state broadcaster IRIB reported on Tuesday.

The talks between the two interior ministers also focused on security matters and the fight against drug trafficking, Tasnim reported.

Yemeni government forces launched a major offensive to retake areas around the Bab el-Mandeb strait from the Houthis, as Pakistan and Turkey agreed to deploy troops to Saudi Arabia and the Houthis struck Riyadh's international airport, oil facilities and military bases in a widening war.

Yemeni officials said forces allied with the internationally recognised government retook on Monday the crucial town of Dhubab just north of the strait, a vital shipping route connecting the Red Sea with the Gulf of Aden.

They also captured the towns of Bab and Hadeid, the officials said, speaking on condition of anonymity. Witnesses said they saw government tanks and forces entering the towns.

The government push came as part of a major offensive launched early Monday under air cover by a Saudi military coalition.

In turn, the Houthis' military spokesman Brigadier General Yahya Saree said in a pre-recorded statement that the group used ballistic and cruise missiles and drones in attacks targeting King Khalid International Airport in the Saudi capital Riyadh and an oil refinery in the Red Sea city of Rabigh.

Also attacked were Abha airport, military facilities in Khamis Mushait and other "critical sites" in the cities of Najran and Jizan, he claimed.

The US Embassy in Riyadh warned US citizens on social media to be vigilant about possible drone or missile attacks.

The group formally known as Ansar Allah said they had captured more territory in the southwestern part of Yemen, taking a key town in a blow to the Saudi-backed forces as they advanced toward a strategic provincial capital in the region.

The Saudi-led coalition spokesman, Major General Turki al-Malki, said 100 fighter jets supported government forces. He said the coalition secured "air protection" of the Bab el-Mandeb Strait and destroyed hundreds of Houthi targets.

The escalation between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis has revived Yemen's war after a four-year ceasefire.

On Sunday, the Houthis captured the town of Turbah, which sits on a main road linking the provincial capital of Taiz with the port city of Aden, which serves as an interim seat of Yemen's government. Over the years, the town was a major post for government forces defending Taiz.

Earlier, the Houthis seized several villages and areas on Taiz's outskirts, paving the way for the group to encircle Taiz, Yemen's third-largest city.

The Houthis made a swift push on the country's western coast on the Red Sea in early September, capturing the strategic city of Mokha and several Red Sea islands, gains that enabled them to control the Yemeni side of the Bab el-Mandeb, through which 12% of global trade transits.

The Saudi-backed government said Sunday it was mobilising troops across government-held areas.

Rashad al-Alimi, head of the ruling Presidential Leadership Council, said in a televised speech that the operations were aiming at "recovering the remaining territories" controlled by the Houthis. He called on the international community to provide support to his government.

Hours after al-Alimi's declaration, the Saudi military spokesperson al-Malki vowed "full operational support" to Yemeni government forces until the Houthis are "eliminated."

The first Astana AI Film Festival has concluded in Kazakhstan, with Latvian filmmaker Nikolay Shestak taking the Grand Prix. The festival had a total fund of €1.8 million, with half allocated to prize money and the other half to investment in promising AI teams and film production in Kazakhstan.
Latvian filmmaker Nikolay Shestak has won the $450,000 (€401,000) Grand Prix at the first Astana AI Film Festival in Kazakhstan for his film The First Honest Day, created using artificial intelligence.

The First Honest Day explores how people's actions shape who they become, with bad deeds making its characters physically uglier and good deeds changing them for the better.

Shestak, who started directing around four years ago and began making films with AI two years ago, said he did not expect to win.

"Honestly, I didn't expect to win. There were so many incredible works and different genres here that I'm curious myself why the jury chose my film," he said.

Second place in the main competition went to Indian filmmaker Abhik Banerjee for SITARA, earning him $200,000 (€178,000), while Spain's Ernest Desumbila received $100,000 (€89,000) for Looking For Perseida in third place.

Another $250,000 (€223,000) was distributed across five categories in the open competition, with each winner receiving $50,000 (€44,000).

Kazakh filmmaker Myrzakhan Zhumakhan won Best Character for his comedy It Was Just a Joke. China's 2xLabs won Best Story for The Last Span, while Italian filmmaker Jacopo Zanetti received Best Visual for What's the Job?. Another Italian filmmaker, Jacopo Reale, won Best Direction for The Fracture, and US filmmaker Zachary London took Best Concept for Pomegranate.

The films were judged not only on how AI tools were used but also on their artistic vision, storytelling and overall coherence, as well as how organically the technology was integrated into the creative concept.

The international jury included Audrey Azoulay, former Director-General of UNESCO and former French Culture Minister, Ping Ma of China Film Group, Wang Hao of Beijing Film Academy, entrepreneur and investor Balaji Srinivasan, and BytePlus executive Long Li.

This year's competition attracted more than 8,000 submissions from 125 countries, with 25 films selected for the final programme. The projects span 19 genres and were created using more than 80 generative AI models.

The festival had a total fund of $2 million (€1.8mn). Half of it, provided by the Freedom Shapagat Corporate Foundation under Freedom Holding Corp., was allocated to prize money.

Another half was set aside as investment capital for promising AI teams and the development of production in Kazakhstan.

Kazakhstan's President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev, who opened the festival, highlighted how artificial intelligence is changing the industry.

"Filmmakers and production crews no longer need to knock on the doors of production companies or look for sponsors. The barrier to entry has become significantly lower," he said.

At the same time, Tokayev said AI would not replace the creative role of filmmakers, but could become "an indispensable tool for everyone who has devoted their life to creativity."

He also said the festival was timely for Kazakhstan, pointing to the country's young population.

The CEO of the world's biggest oil company warned at a conference that the global oil supply system is reaching its limit and that replenishing global oil stockpiles could take two years.
Saudi Aramco chief executive Amin Nasser has warned that the world’s oil supply cushion is shrinking. The head of the world’s largest oil company said the Iran war has drained stocks that could help offset further supply disruptions.

The effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway that typically handles around 20% of the world’s oil and liquefied natural gas supplies, led to major supply disruptions and higher prices, hitting the global economy.

Seven months into the war, the global oil stockpile system is "already straining," said Nasser, speaking at the Energy Intelligence Forum in London on Monday.

"And with precious little else the world can turn to, the supply resilience cushion is scarily thin," he told the two-day conference attended by leaders from across the energy sector.

G7 countries, in coordination with the International Energy Agency (IEA), agreed on Friday to immediately release 100 million barrels of diesel and crude oil to ease global energy supply concerns caused by the US-Iran war.

But Nasser explained that total oil inventories do not reflect the amount of oil that can actually be supplied to the market. "That's why you find they're struggling with 100 million barrels."

But Nasser warned Monday against looking at headline figures for commercial reserves, noting that "less than 10%" were available for use. A large portion of the reserves reported by countries represent the minimum volume required to keep infrastructure operational, he said.

According to Nasser, global oil inventories stood at around 10 billion barrels at the start of the crisis. Since then, nearly 3 billion barrels of oil supply have been lost, or roughly half the crude and refined products that would normally pass through the Strait of Hormuz during that time. More than 1 billion barrels have been withdrawn from global inventories to help offset those losses, with most drawn from onshore commercial inventories.

Nasser described these inventories as "the last major tool in the box", adding that much of the remaining stock, estimated at less than 6 billion barrels, is “not practically available.”

Nasser said tapping reserves buys the market time but does not fix long-term supply and demand imbalances. He added that these volumes could help the world through one winter, but do not amount to a lasting solution.

He pointed out that rebuilding inventories after the Strait of Hormuz fully reopens could take up to two years.

Despite attacks on ships in the Strait of Hormuz, crude oil exports from the Middle East Gulf region, excluding Iran, returned to pre-war levels in September, according to maritime tracking firm Kpler.

"At least 16.5 mbd (million barrels a day) left the region between 1 and 28 September, matching the pre-war average excluding Iran. That is 10.5 mbd above March's monthly average," said the firm.

Some 40% of those exports now bypass the Strait of Hormuz, compared with 17% before the war, with pipelines in Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates providing key alternative routes. Most crude crossing the strait changes tankers offshore.

Nasser said Aramco was meeting its customers' requirements, citing the company's use of international storage facilities and the speed with which it has repaired infrastructure damaged during the conflict.

Aramco is also seeking additional routes for crude exports and new storage facilities abroad, in order to reduce its reliance on a single channel to reach buyers in global markets.

The three signatories of the August Mecca Joint Defence Agreement met in emergency session in Riyadh and agreed to move immediately to "rapid deployment" of military forces, uniting NATO's second-largest army, the Islamic world's only nuclear state and the largest Gulf power.
Turkey and Pakistan will undertake a "rapid deployment" of troops to Saudi Arabia as part of a new mutual defence alliance, the three countries said Monday, as the Gulf kingdom faces escalating attacks from Yemen's Houthis.

In August, the three countries signed the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement, committing to come to each other's aid if one were attacked.

The pact unites NATO's second-largest army, that of Turkey, with the Islamic world's only nuclear-armed state, Pakistan, and the largest Gulf power, Saudi Arabia.

Their representatives held an emergency meeting in Riyadh on Monday, where they noted recent attacks on Saudi Arabia, in particular those targeting the holy cities of Mecca and Medina, without mentioning the Tehran-backed Houthis by name.

The trio "reaffirmed their unequivocal rejection of any attack or threat against the kingdom's security and territorial integrity", they said in a joint statement.

They also agreed "to move immediately to the practical implementation of the collective defence commitments and to take the necessary measures to provide the agreed military forces and capabilities and ensure their rapid deployment in the kingdom".

The statement did not specify what sort of role the troops would fill once in Saudi Arabia.

Saudi Arabia has faced repeated Houthi attacks targeting its cities, oil infrastructure and ships ever since Yemen's civil war flared back to life in July.

In September, the group launched a new offensive that seized swathes of territory from Yemen's internationally recognised government, which on Monday launched a counteroffensive with Riyadh's support, seeking to retake all Houthi territory, some of it held for more than a decade.

Updated: 06/10/2026 - 9:23 GMT+2
Police fired tear gas as protesters clashed outside Kosovo’s parliament over moves to limit powers of a Hague war crimes court.
… More
Police used tear gas and water cannon as protesters fired fireworks and threw objects during clashes outside Kosovo's parliament in Pristina on Monday. Riot officers moved forward behind shields as fires burned in the streets, and several people were detained.

The unrest followed parliament's first reading of amendments aimed at limiting the powers of the Kosovo Specialist Chambers in The Hague. The court recently convicted four former Kosovo Liberation Army commanders of war crimes committed during the 1998-99 conflict. The proposed changes would cap prison sentences at 15 years, allow appeals to Kosovo's Supreme Court and let convicted people serve their sentences in Kosovo.

Tensions escalated after demonstrators blocked the exits of parliament and government buildings to prevent lawmakers from leaving. The proposed amendments still need a second reading and final approval.

Barcelona’s housing protest turned violent with fires and arrests, highlighting Spain’s affordability crisis ahead of a general strike and snap election in November.
… More
Thousands of people marched through central Barcelona on Monday to demand affordable homes, carrying banners and chanting for free and quality housing. The demonstration later turned tense, as some participants threw objects at police and set rubbish bins on fire. Officers charged the crowd and detained several people as smoke filled the streets.

The march reflected growing anger over high rents, a shortage of homes and the risk of eviction across Spain. People of all ages took part, with demonstrators calling for political action. Trade unions have also announced a one-day general strike over the issue, although the date has yet to be set.

The unrest followed parliament's rejection of emergency measures aimed at easing the affordability crisis. The government has since called a snap general election for November 29, adding to the political pressure over an issue that has become a growing concern across Spain.

Canada’s Prime Minister Mark Carney has pledged to work with the incoming separatist government in Quebec to address the needs and issues of the province, including resisting a hostile US administration, creating jobs and building housing.
The separatist Parti Québécois (PQ) won Quebec’s provincial election on Monday but fell short of garnering enough seats to govern alone, returning to power with a promise to hold another referendum on independence from Canada.

The result returns Paul St-Pierre Plamondon’s party to power for the first time in more than a decade but denies it the majority it sought, forcing the separatists to rely on opposition parties even as they try to revive Quebec’s independence debate.

The party was on track to win nearly 60 seats as the final votes were counted, falling short of the 64 needed for a majority in Quebec’s 127-member National Assembly, the provincial legislature.

A minority government means PQ will need support from opposition lawmakers to pass key legislation and survive confidence votes.

St-Pierre Plamondon acknowledged that voters had denied his party a majority. “Quebecers elected the Parti Québécois asking it to work with the other parties, and I heard that message,” he said.

He has pledged to hold another referendum on independence, though he has said he would wait until US President Donald Trump is no longer in office.

St-Pierre Plamondon framed independence as essential to preserving Quebec’s French-speaking identity, saying it was a “question of survival” for a French-speaking population that represents only a small fraction of North America.

“Quebec just wants to exist on its own like other countries of the world,” he said adding that a referendum would allow locals to decide their own future.

“I continue to dream and visualise that country,” St-Pierre Plamondon said in his victory speech. “That dream will be realised one day.”

He also sought to reassure Canadians outside Quebec. “The project for independence is not about excluding you,” he said. “Quebec will never change its geographical position, so we have an interest in working together.”

Coalition Avenir Québec (CAQ) leader Christine Fréchette conceded defeat, saying voters had made their choice and that she took full responsibility for the “difficult result." The CAQ, which held 79 seats before the election, was at risk of losing every seat in the legislature.

“This was not an election about independence, which is not popular in Quebec right now, but about getting rid of the CAQ, the party in power since 2018,” said Daniel Béland, a political science professor at McGill University in Montreal.

Béland said the minority result is likely welcome news for Prime Minister Mark Carney’s government because it could make it harder for the PQ to prepare for and eventually call an independence referendum.

Carney congratulated St-Pierre Plamondon and signalled that Ottawa would work with the incoming separatist government.

“Building a stronger Québec is core to our mission of building a stronger Canada,” Carney said. He said the two governments would work together to support businesses and workers affected by US tariffs, create jobs and build housing.

The Quebec result comes two weeks before Albertans vote Oct. 19 on whether to hold a referendum on separation from Canada, posing a potentially major threat to Carney’s government.

Trump detailed that additional threats received by Washington prompted their moving of the military aircraft from the UK base they were deployed in.
US President Donald Trump said on Monday that the United States had withdrawn all B-1 bombers from a British airbase because of unspecified threats that he believed were linked to Iran.

The Pentagon confirmed earlier that the bombers had left the RAF Fairford airbase – used by the United States to launch ‘defensive’ strikes on Tehran – even as the British government insisted the site was safe.

"And rather than tempting the enemy – we'll capture the enemy and beat the enemy, but why should we leave them there? So we moved them around a little bit, not very far."

Speculation has swirled about the incident since police were called to the base in southwest England on 27 September after a local farmer reported a group of men with several vans engaged in "suspicious" activity.

Despite a major counter-terrorism probe, five suspects arrested at the time and a British-Iranian national detained afterwards have all since been released on bail.

Investigators did not find any explosives in the vans, but "a quantity of petrol was recovered," according to counter-terrorism police.

Trump would not comment on a report that the threat involved attack drones, saying: "I can't tell you that but if they did they'll suffer greatly."

"We actually know the people that made the threat," said Trump, adding: "They would be linked to Iran."

Two US officials told US media outlets that the bombers were moved after commanders received new threats about an Iran-backed plot.

One US official said the new information was part of the same threat warnings that surfaced three weeks ago, but the situation became urgent in the past 24 hours.

Asked whether the base was safe however, UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham's official spokesman replied "yes."

"We have tried and tested approaches to defend our bases around the country and throughout the world," he told reporters.

Speculation about the motives behind the incident ranged from terror threat to fuel theft, as energy prices in the UK hit record highs this week.

Burnham previously said there were "strong indications that Iran played a part" in the incident, a charge rejected by Tehran.

Trump had previously rebuked British authorities for releasing the arrested men, all in their mid-20s, on bail – pending further investigation.

Iran's embassy in London said it "categorically rejects and strongly condemns" speculation that Tehran was involved.
