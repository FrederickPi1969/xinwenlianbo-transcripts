Updated: 23/10/2026 - 16:15 GMT+2
A multi-storey building under construction has collapsed in Nairobi, leaving a vast pile of rubble. Red Cross workers have arrived at the scene, where excavators are being used among the debris.
… More
A multi-storey building under construction collapsed in Nairobi’s Eastleigh district on Friday 23 October, leaving a vast heap of concrete rubble and twisted debris. The Kenya Red Cross said the structure came down at about 1:40 pm local time along Yusuf Haji Avenue and dispatched emergency response teams to assist search-and-rescue efforts.

Footage from the Kenyan capital showed the scale of the destruction, with large sections of the unfinished structure reduced to piles of broken concrete. An excavator was deployed among the wreckage while Red Cross personnel worked at the site, urging the public to stay clear so rescuers could operate safely.

Several people were feared trapped beneath the rubble, although authorities had not yet confirmed casualties, injuries, or the number of missing persons. The Nairobi County governor’s office noted reports of trapped individuals, while Reuters reported that the ground floor of the building had been occupied.

There were conflicting accounts over the building’s height, ranging between 10 and 18 storeys. No official height or cause of the collapse have been confirmed.

The collapse has once again raised questions about construction standards in Kenya, where similar disasters have repeatedly claimed lives amid concerns over inadequate safety checks and enforcement.

Huge waves, uprooted trees and damaged boats mark Storm Austen's arrival in France and the UK. Gusts of up to 160 km/h have battered Brittany, while fallen branches have blocked roads in southern England.
… More
Storm Austen, the first named storm of the 2026 autumn season, battered coastal areas of France and the UK on Friday 23 October, bringing powerful winds, heavy rain and towering waves. Brittany, Cornwall and Devon were among the hardest-hit regions, with gusts reaching 160 km/h at Pointe du Raz in Finistère, while winds of up to 80 mph were forecast for exposed headlands in south-west England.

Along the Finistère coast, gusts of 120 to 140 km/h whipped up rough seas. At Lanildut harbour, waves several metres high crashed against a navigation marker, yachts rocked violently at their moorings and a pleasure boat was left capsized and partially submerged. Heavy rain flooded parts of the harbour, while fallen branches littered nearby roads.

Further along the coast, large waves pounded the shoreline at Le Conquet, where boats remained moored in the port. In Rennes, emergency crews used chainsaws to remove a large tree uprooted beside the River Vilaine, temporarily blocking traffic on a nearby boulevard.

Across the Channel, fallen branches forced the closure of a road in Hayle, Cornwall, as workers cleared the debris. Strong winds and downpours also swept across southern England, with Devon among the areas exposed to the worst conditions.

The storm's strongest winds and heaviest seas were concentrated along the Atlantic and Channel coasts before the weather system moved eastwards across northern France and southern England.

Ukraine hits Russian data centres in blow to Moscow's AI capabilities
Yandex data centre in Sasovo after a strike by Ukrainian drones, 20 October 2026.

Drones hit three Yandex facilities, a Sber centre and a VKontakte server farm across five Russian regions, causing persistent failures in payments, navigation and cloud services. Experts say restoring the infrastructure could take months or years.
Ukrainian drones struck five data centres belonging to Russia's largest technology companies in October, causing widespread failures in cloud services, payment systems and navigation apps nationwide.

The Ukrainian armed forces hit three Yandex facilities: in Sasovo in the Ryazan region on 7 October, in Kaluga on 9 October, and in Vladimir on 11 October.

On 14 October a Sber data centre in Balakovo in the Saratov region was struck, and on 18 October a VKontakte data centre in the Leningrad region near St Petersburg came under attack.

Yandex, Sber and VKontakte together run much of Russia's consumer internet infrastructure, from taxi apps and card payment processing to cloud storage and messaging.

None of the companies has disclosed the scale of the damage. Russians have reported persistent difficulties paying for goods, ordering taxis, using navigation services and accessing cloud storage.

Experts say restoring the infrastructure could take months or years, and the strikes have set back Russia's artificial intelligence development, Mikhail Klimarev, head of the Internet Protection Society, told AP.

The attacks come as Russia has itself been systematically targeting Ukrainian data centres this autumn.

On 19 October a Russian strike completely destroyed one of the largest facilities of the Ukrainian operator Datagroup in Kyiv, which was declared beyond repair.

The following day a Russian attack damaged Kyiv's GigaCenter. Russia hit several other Ukrainian data centres in September and October.

An earlier Russian strike on a Kyiv data centre in July disrupted internet access, mobile communications and television across the city.

Moscow has targeted Ukraine's IT systems since day one of Russia's full-scale invasion: cruise missiles struck a government server facility on 24 February 2022, and a Russian missile hit the National Data Backup Centre in central Kyiv on 1 March.

Ukraine has since moved most of its data to servers in European countries, limiting the impact of subsequent attacks.

Russian data centres are more vulnerable than Ukrainian ones, analysts say. Under legislation designed to create a so-called "sovereign internet," Russia requires companies to store all data on servers inside the country. That concentration makes the facilities easier to target.

"Russia's problem is that it has to provide air defence over a vast territory, and it does not have enough resources for that," said Nick Reiners, senior geo-technology analyst at Eurasia Group.

"Since all the infrastructure and all the data are located inside Russia, it is relatively easier to disable them." Why air defence systems were not protecting Russian data centres, or failed to do so, has not been explained.

Analysts describe the facilities as soft targets: vast halls filled with servers, power equipment and cooling systems where even a small explosive charge can cause serious damage.

Satellite imagery of the struck Yandex facilities shows fires that may have destroyed microchips and other fragile components.

"After such a strike it is hard to expect anything to have survived: data-centre equipment is fragile, demanding and expensive. Soot from fires is extremely harmful to electronics," Klimarev told AP.

The damage to Yandex is particularly significant for Russia's AI ambitions. The company's three supercomputers, used to develop AI models, rank among the world's top 500. The most powerful, housed in the Sasovo facility, once ranked 19th globally but has since fallen below the top 100.

"Ukrainian drone strikes will probably delay the development of Russian artificial intelligence even further," Connor Martin of the Council on Foreign Relations said. Before the strikes, Russia was already several years behind the US and China.

Restoring the hardware will be complicated by Western sanctions and the global chip shortage. Yandex's supercomputers run on Nvidia A100 processors, which Russia cannot legally import.

Klimarev said the strikes may carry an unintended consequence for Russian internet policy.

Wooden boards are going up across France as shopkeepers brace for possible unrest. With a National Rally congress in Orléans and protests approaching in Lyon, businesses fear damage and disruption.
… More
As Lyon and Orléans prepare for a weekend of demonstrations, shopkeepers are boarding up windows, removing valuable stock and reinforcing entrances.

In Lyon, workers were filmed on Friday installing wooden panels and protective fencing outside City Hall, the Museum of Fine Arts and the Chamber of Commerce. Shops along Rue de la République were also being secured, with carpenters cutting boards and covering windows. In Orléans, businesses along the planned protest route are also taking precautions ahead of a weekend that will see the city host the National Rally’s congress. Nearly 3,000 police officers and security personnel will be deployed as between 20,000 and 30,000 protesters are expected to gather in opposition to the event, raising concerns over possible disruption.

Three miners were killed and five injured, two of them seriously, in an explosion at a privately owned mine in northern Turkey.
The miner who had been trapped following an explosion at a coal mine in northwestern Turkey has also been confirmed dead, local authorities reported, bringing the death toll to three.

The explosion at the mine in the town of Gelik was caused by a "firedamp," or flammable gas, local authorities said.

"Eight people were in the tunnel, seven were rescued. Unfortunately, two of our miners died after being taken to hospital," said Zonguldak governor Osman Hacibektasoglu.

A rescue operation had been underway to reach one person trapped in the mine, who has now been confirmed dead.

Two of the rescued miners suffered burns and were in serious condition. One of the critically injured miners had been transferred to Ankara and preparations are under way to transfer the second miner.

"One of the miners is still inside the mine. We have made contact with him and are trying to get him out," the governor added.

The blast occurred at around 10 in the morning. After an emergency call, teams from the Disaster and Emergency Management Authority (AFAD), as well as medical and fire crews, were dispatched to the site.

According to local newspaper Pusula, the explosion took place at a private mine owned by Demir Madencilik. An investigation has been opened to determine the exact cause of the blast.

Experts interviewed by Euronews explained why improving children's lives requires more than simply expanding access to essential services.
With 412 million children living in extreme poverty, a global summit in Uzbekistan has called for greater investment in children's healthcare, education and protection, alongside closer coordination between services that often operate separately.

This was at the core of the Samarkand Declaration, adopted on Thursday at the end of a two-day gathering attended by representatives from more than 100 countries.

The declaration calls for stronger social protection systems, nationally determined targets and regular reporting on the quality and effectiveness of services for children.

According to UNICEF, two-thirds of the child-related UN Sustainable Development Goal indicators it monitors are off track, raising concerns about progress towards the 2030 targets.

Providing access to essential services does not necessarily guarantee children receive the care and support they need, summit participants said.

"We should invest not sector by sector, but we should complement each other and this will give a better result for the children," UNICEF Regional Director Édouard Beigbeder told Euronews.

Beigbeder also highlighted the importance of investing in social workers and healthcare professionals, rather than focusing exclusively on infrastructure.

Uzbekistan's First Lady Ziroat Mirziyoyeva, who received the WHO Director-General's Award 2026 for her contribution to childhood cancer care, also called for closer cooperation between services.

"Let us integrate medical, social and psychological support so that parents do not have to go from one institution to another in search of assistance," she told the summit.

Turkey's First Lady Emine Erdoğan questioned whether conventional indicators adequately reflect children's experiences.

"However, going to school does not always mean receiving a quality education, not being hungry does not mean being properly nourished, and not being subjected to violence does not mean being loved and valued," she said.

Protecting children from violence means services need to work together, particularly when victims require medical treatment, psychological support, social assistance and legal protection.

Global Head of Child Protection at Save the Children International Rebecca Smith told Euronews that children face violence at home, in conflict zones and increasingly online.

She stressed the importance of ensuring that survivors can seek support without facing stigma.

Uzbekistan used the summit to show how different services can be brought together to support children and families.

“Because for a parent, a child is not the future. A child is the present,” Shahnoza Mirziyoyeva, First Deputy Director of Uzbekistan's Social Protection Agency, told the summit.

In an address to participants, President Shavkat Mirziyoyev emphasised that children's wellbeing should be measured by their quality of life today, not their future economic contribution.

According to figures presented by Mirziyoyev, preschool enrolment has increased from 27.7% to 78%, while the number of inclusive schools has risen fivefold over the past two years.

Visiting First Ladies also attended the opening of the new Imkon rehabilitation centre in Samarkand, which combines preschool education with medical, psychological and educational support for children with disabilities.

The facility can accommodate 150 children and provide outpatient rehabilitation services to up to 100 children aged three to 18 each month.

Lebanon's First Lady Nehmat Aoun, who visited the centre, told Euronews that countries should learn from one another's experiences to improve children's education, care and protection.

The United Arab Emirates reported an oil spill from a tanker off its coast on Friday, as numerous vessels in Gulf waters came under attack in the ongoing Iran war.

The authorities "detected a tanker oil spill affecting Umm Al Quwain's beaches," the Ministry of Climate Change and Environment said in a statement, referring to an emirate north of Dubai.

It said "teams immediately began containment, cordoning, clean-up and remediation... to limit environmental impacts", adding that the emirate's beaches had been closed.

Many residents in the country's north have reported unusual oil-like smells on social media in recent days, including in northern districts of Dubai.

Frequent attacks on ships have been reported in recent weeks near the Strait of Hormuz, control of which remains at the heart of the war between Iran and the US.

The maritime security agency UKMTO reported on 9 October that a vessel had been hit by a projectile off the UAE coast, causing a fire that was later brought under control.

According to maritime security company Vanguard, the vessel was the oil tanker GEM NO.2, sailing under the Panamanian flag.

Roughly one-fifth of the world's oil passed through the Strait of Hormuz before the war that began with US-Israeli strikes on Iran in late February. Both Tehran and Washington have implemented blockades in the strait, while Iran has repeatedly targeted cargo vessels using the southern section of the waterway.

Earlier this week, Iran's IRGC said in a statement that it targets tankers trying to use what Tehran unilaterally designates as an unauthorised route through the strait's southern section, near the Omani coast.

Tehran has no recognised authority under international maritime law to restrict or authorise passage through Hormuz, which is subject to the right of transit passage under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea.

The UAE lies close to the Strait of Hormuz, just 40-50 nautical miles from the waterway's narrowest point.

A television reporter abandoned his shoes and glasses and dived into rising floodwaters to save a driver whose car was sinking fast in Morayfield, Queensland.
Torrential rain has triggered flash flooding across southeast Queensland, Australia, breaking multiple October rainfall records, stranding drivers, forcing school closures and prompting hundreds of emergency calls over two days.

The deluge began on Thursday with a water treatment plant near Moreton Bay, north of Brisbane, recording more than 250 millimetres of rain in 24 hours.

Some locations received nearly 180 millimetres in just three hours. Beerburrum recorded its wettest October day on record, while Toowoomba experienced its heaviest October rainfall in 29 years.

Flooded roads damaged infrastructure, disrupted electricity supplies and forced 14 schools to close.

Authorities urged residents in parts of Caboolture and Burpengary to prepare to evacuate as a fresh wave of rain struck southeast Queensland on Friday, compounding the damage.

One of the most dramatic moments of the crisis unfolded in Morayfield on Thursday, when Australian Broadcasting Corporation (ABC) reporter Will Murray and a bystander jumped into floodwaters to rescue a driver trapped in a rapidly sinking car.

Murray told the ABC that the vehicle began to float and sink within seconds of driving into the flooded road, leaving no time to wait for emergency services.

“My colleague and I sort of started to watch him sink further into the water and thought, ‘well, we don’t have time to wait,’” Murray said.

“So I threw off my shoes, gave my glasses to my colleague and jumped in and started swimming. And thankfully somebody nearby jumped in with me to give me a hand.”

The pair initially tried to break the car’s rear windscreen but were forced to move to the side of the vehicle. What Murray saw through the back window made the urgency clear.

“Couldn’t get that door open. Got up onto the ute tray, looked through the back window and you could see that the water was up sort of shoulder level, sort of towards chin level, just terrifying and angling down,” he said.

They eventually managed to open a door and pull the driver to safety. No injuries were reported.

At least eight weather stations recorded more than 300 millimetres of rainfall between Wednesday and Friday morning, more than three times the volume originally forecast by the Bureau of Meteorology.

Wet conditions are expected to persist into the weekend, with forecasters warning of another peak on Monday and a cold front potentially shifting the weather pattern later in the week.

European Parliament’s point man on Canada, Tobias Cremer, told Euronews that the planned “associate membership” should include an institutional framework formalising closer ties between the EU and Canada.
The EU must turn its offer of “associate membership” to Canada into a meaningful partnership that goes well beyond trade, Tobias Cremer, the European Parliament's standing rapporteur on Canada, told Euronews.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen invited Canada to become the EU's first “associate member” in September, but gave little indication of what the unprecedented status would mean in practice.

The offer comes as geopolitical upheaval draws Brussels and Ottawa closer together. Canada is embroiled in a trade war with its traditional US ally, while the EU is struggling to keep Washington on its side over Ukraine.

The two sides are due to meet at a summit in Montreal at the end of next week, with agreements expected in several areas, including critical raw materials and digital cooperation.

“For the last couple of months, we've been great, Canada and Europe, at writing each other love letters and saying how much we really want to work together,” said Cremer, a German socialist MEP. “But I think now is really the time to start to get serious and turn that love story into concrete commitments.”

He added that “associate membership” must mean the “closest possible partnership that the EU can offer to a non-European country and since Canada is probably the most European of non-European countries that should be very close.”

He called for a “pragmatic approach,” involving sectoral agreements covering trade, defence, digital cooperation, the Arctic and students, but also going further.

“We should go a step further and start embedding all of these great initiatives into something more enduring, into a common institutional framework of coordination and cooperation.”

Cremer suggested that Canada could participate “as an observer” in the Council of the EU, where national ministers meet, and called for the creation of a formal parliamentary partnership assembly.

Such a body could follow the model of the existing EU-UK Parliamentary Partnership Assembly, which brings together 35 members from each side twice a year for sessions that can focus on specific policy areas.

Regarding the UK, however, the MEP described it as a European country for which, “if it wishes so,” the ambition “should not be associate membership, but full membership.”

His comments came as UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham recently floated the idea of rejoining the EU, with polls showing that a majority of Britons want to return to the bloc.

But Cremer also argued for closer cooperation not only with the UK and Canada, but also with countries such as Australia and South Korea.

“I think it is of our interest to bring all of these actors together and say 'hold on, actually the majority of the world wants to play 21st century politics which is focused on collective security, on shared interests, on mutual respect rather than might is right.'”

Around 280 companies from 33 countries gathered at Milipol Qatar, displaying drones, robots and AI systems. Exhibitors discussed changing security needs, while officials examined how to protect essential services and respond to cyberattacks and emergencies.
Security companies from 33 countries gathered in Doha this week for Milipol Qatar, displaying drones, robots and AI systems as the country reviews its defences following the Iran war.

The three-day exhibition, which ended Thursday, covered equipment for policing, emergency response and industrial safety, along with discussions on cybersecurity and protecting essential services.

Alexandros Lakatamitis, general manager of West Bay Petroleum, which brought drones to the exhibition, said government and industry customers were taking security more seriously.

“The security bubble that we’ve enjoyed for so long is no longer there,” he told Euronews.

Lakatamitis, who grew up in Qatar, said security had become a more pressing consideration across government and business, including among the customers his company speaks to daily.

Around 280 companies participated in the exhibition at the Doha Exhibition and Convention Center, according to Major Khalid Saadon Al-Kuwari, a member of the Milipol organising committee. About 70% came from outside Qatar.

Major Al-Kuwari said the event brought together “solutions for whatever challenges that different governments and different countries are facing”.

He said it gave governments an opportunity to examine systems already tested in other countries and consider how they could be used at home.

On Monday, Qatar’s Defence Minister Saoud bin Abdulrahman Al Thani said the country was drawing on lessons from the Iran war to improve protection against ballistic missiles, drones and cyberattacks.

He also outlined plans for a local defence production hub and closer cooperation with regional and international partners.

On the exhibition floor, companies also presented equipment intended for civilian emergencies and hazardous industrial work.

Lakatamitis described how drones could search large areas for missing people. Photographs collected during a flight can be analysed by software to identify possible signs of a person, helping rescuers decide where to concentrate their efforts, he said.

He also pointed to industrial inspections, where a drone fitted with gas-detection equipment could investigate a suspected leak without sending someone into a potentially dangerous location.

Those applications were part of a broader discussion about using AI in security. The second Doha International Conference on AI & Security ran alongside Milipol, with sessions on responsible AI use, autonomous systems and the protection of critical infrastructure.

At a cybersecurity seminar on Thursday, officials discussed how to keep essential services operating during an attack.

Khalid Al Nasr, assistant director of the information security department at Qatar’s National Cyber Security Agency, said the response needed to extend beyond restoring IT infrastructure to ensuring vital government services continued or were restored within an acceptable timeframe.

At the discussion, Al Nasr called for cybersecurity to be incorporated into disaster recovery and crisis management plans, with clear responsibilities to reduce confusion during an incident.

The exhibition also saw fresh international security agreements. Qatar and France signed a cooperation plan between their interior ministries covering 2027–2030, aimed at strengthening coordination and exchanges of expertise.

A joint statement following French President Emmanuel Macron’s visit to Doha said the expanded partnership covered homeland security and civil protection.

On Thursday, Qatar's Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani launched the sixth Watan exercise during a visit to Milipol. The exercise brings together security, military, and civilian agencies to rehearse emergency responses.

The clashes broke out after parliamentary leadership rejected opposition demands to debate ten days of student demonstrations that have gripped the capital Tirana.
Lawmakers in Albania's parliament clashed on Thursday as opposition members of parliament attempted to disrupt a session, while the assembly continued voting on legislation amid tensions in the country.

The clashes broke out after parliamentary leadership rejected opposition demands to debate ten days of student demonstrations that have gripped the capital Tirana.

Opposition MPs held up photographs of students injured by police during previous protests and blocked the parliamentary podium, while the assembly pressed ahead with voting on legislation amid the chaos.

For nearly two weeks now, demonstrators have clashed repeatedly with police in the city centre, hurling rocks, flares, firebombs and fireworks. Officers have responded with batons, water cannons and tear gas.

On Thursday, large crowds of students gathered at the parliament's entrances as the protests entered their tenth consecutive day.

High school and university students are demanding lower tuition fees, greater state investment in education and an end to corruption in the education system.

They have since joined a broader anti-government movement calling for the resignation of Socialist Prime Minister Edi Rama. Rama has, however, vowed to resist.

“A wave of stones, an attempt to overthrow the government and change those in power through the imposition of violence, something that will never be allowed to happen, in any form or for any reason,” he said after meeting teachers and school representatives on Tuesday.

The Albanian prime minister argued that legitimate grievances were being exploited by those deliberately inciting violence.

The student protests follow months of demonstrations organised by the Flamingo movement, which emerged in opposition to a major luxury tourism development on Albania’s southern Adriatic coast linked to an investment firm founded by Jared Kushner, son-in-law of US President Donald Trump.

Interestingly, Albania is not alone. Governments across Europe are facing street pressure over rising fuel costs, deteriorating public services, underfunded schools and a deepening housing crisis.

France and Italy have seen similar protests, while tent camps have sprung up in Spain and Portugal over unaffordable housing. Demonstrations were also planned in Greece this week.

It was the most powerful earthquake to strike the region in five years, according to Chinese media.
A powerful magnitude 7 earthquake has struck China’s Tibet region, killing at least 16 people, injuring 54 and collapsing hundreds of homes in and around the northern city of Nagqu, official media reported.

The quake hit at 10:33 p.m. local time on Thursday, with its epicentre 191 kilometres east-northeast of Nagqu in a sparsely populated, high-altitude area of northern Tibet, according to the US Geological Survey.

It was the most powerful earthquake to strike the region in five years, China’s state broadcaster CCTV said.

Chinese President Xi Jinping called for all-out efforts in rescue and medical relief operations.

Thus far, more than 1,600 people have been deployed for search and rescue operations, with emergency supplies including 1,000 prefabricated housing units, 3,000 tents, folding beds, flashlights and winter clothing delivered to the area.

The USGS recorded at least seven aftershocks ranging in magnitude from 4.3 to 5.2, all at a depth of 10 kilometres. The main quake struck at a depth of 18 kilometres.

Rockfalls triggered by the earthquake forced authorities to close a section of National Highway 317 to all but emergency, rescue and official vehicles while crews worked to clear debris. Local railway authorities halted train services through the affected area as a precaution.

China’s state news agency Xinhua reported that communication has been cut in some areas hit by the quake.

Intermittent snow is forecast across the earthquake zone over the next three days, complicating rescue efforts.
