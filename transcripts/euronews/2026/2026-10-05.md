FlyDubai co-pilot planned to crash aircraft into Tel Aviv airport

The FlyDubai co-pilot behind the hijacking attempt wanted to crash the jet into Ben Gurion Airport and previously flew the sensitive Israel route, according to multiple sources close to the investigation, amid increasingly difficult questions about the circumstances of the narrowly avoided tragedy.
The co-pilot of the Israel-bound FlyDubai FZ1073 flight planned to crash the aircraft into Tel Aviv’s Ben-Gurion Airport, according to multiple US and Israeli media reports quoting sources close to the multinational investigation.

The same reports say the Omani national named by multiple sources as Hamam Al-Hammami was most likely acting alone.

After attacking captain Smit Machchhar, Al-Hammami planned to fly the plane toward Ben Gurion Airport using normal approach and landing procedures, then crash it at the last moment, with more than 180 passengers on board.

According to Israeli media quoting sources with knowledge of the investigation, the co-pilot had prepared the attack for several months and previously flew to Israel to gather information.

Other reports claim that he planned to crash another flight to Tel Aviv earlier this year, but then changed his mind.

Israeli Channel 12 also reported that Israel asked the UAE to expand its questioning to include the flight crew, as even more questions emerge about the harrowing minutes between the co-pilot stabbing the captain and the passengers rushing into the cockpit, subduing the attacker and stabilising the plane.

In the first comments by Emirati investigators on Saturday, UAE Prosecutor General Hamad Saif al-Shamsi said the co-pilot of FlyDubai flight FZ1073 “attempted to carry out a terrorist attack” when he attacked the flight captain with an axe and tried to take control of the aircraft.

The UAE is leading the investigation, and security agencies from four regional countries, including Israel, are working to establish the co-pilot’s motives, aided by the US and other Western governments.

Known as the Bergen Group after its inaugural meeting in Norway last year, the format brings together Sweden, Finland, Poland, Germany and the Netherlands.

"The bottom line is this is very toxic for an alliance that relies of collective play," said Fabrice Pothier, CEO of geopolitical consultancy, Rasmussen Global.

The group was established to discuss NATO’s future as the alliance shifts towards so-called NATO 3.0: a US invented model in which Europe takes the lead the conventional defence of the continent.

It also involves the US scaling back its traditional role, and withdrawing key capabilities including bombers, submarines and personnel it makes available to the alliance.

“It’s not unusual for groups of Allies to meet in smaller formats,” a NATO official told Euronews.

NATO 3.0 involves the US withdrawing from its traditional role as the leading defence provider in Europe, pulling capabilities such as bombers, submarines and personnel available to the alliance.

While officials dismiss the talks as routine, others argue the arrangement will split members into "good European versus bad European" categories while reinforcing Washington's influence across the alliance.

Pothier acknowledged that smaller groups can advance new ideas within organisations, such as the publication of 'non-papers', but he argued that Washington had selected the Bergen countries as they fulfil important defence criteria.

"But why is the US picking some rather than others when this is not a policy exercise?" he said.

Pothier, who is former director of policy planning said the separation of the parts of alliance into subgroups creates concern that the US might be more inclined to defend one ally over another.

"You introduce the notion that the US favours some allies and would be likely to defend those allies ahead of the rest," said Pothier.

He suggested US President Donald Trump’s “grudge” over the UK and France’s initial refusal to join the war in Iran, along with France’s push to buy European military equipment, could explain their exclusion.

UK diplomats, however, rejected the suggestion that Britain was being sidelined, saying they remained in regular contact with their US counterparts.

The Baltic states’ absence is particularly striking. They are among NATO’s biggest defence spenders as a share of GDP and sit on the alliance’s frontline with Russia. The region has faced repeated hybrid attacks, including drone incursions, arson and sabotage.

Their exclusion follows Colby’s visit to NATO headquarters in Brussels in August, when, according to NATO sources, he urged Baltic officials to adopt a less confrontational tone towards Russian aggression.

One source said Colby cited renewed US efforts to end the war in Ukraine as a reason to tone down talk of being at “war” with Russia.

Rospotrebnadzor said tests found no pathogen linked to the deceased lab worker's work at the Irkutsk anti-plague institute, but more than 60 colleagues remained isolated inside the building and several hospitals stayed closed without explanation.
Russian health authorities said on Monday that a laboratory assistant at an anti-plague institute in Siberia who died last week did not have plague, even as quarantine restrictions remained in place across multiple hospitals in the region.

Daria Shipilova, 28, a staff member at the anti-plague institute in Irkutsk, may have contracted pneumonic plague after a test tube broke in the laboratory, regional outlets reported, citing unnamed sources close to regional security services.

Authorities placed more than 60 institute employees under isolation inside the building from Friday.

A relative of one employee told Siberia.Realii they slept on the floor and chairs without beds, with food parcels passed through a checkpoint. No positive test results had emerged among staff by Saturday, the relative said.

Several Irkutsk hospitals were also placed under quarantine, including three city clinical hospitals, the Ivano-Matreninsky children's hospital and the city maternity hospital.

The Central District Hospital in Shelekhov, a satellite town where Shipilova lived, was also closed. Sources for People of Baikal said ambulances transporting contacts of Shipilova for examination had been escorted by FSB officers since Thursday.

Russia's national health watchdog Rospotrebnadzor said tests of biological material from Shipilova found no microorganisms linked to her professional activity and no accidents involving pathogenic micro-organisms had been detected at the institute.

Among those who had contact with her, two cases of COVID-19 and two rhinovirus infections were found; no other infectious agents were identified, it said.

Irkutsk governor Igor Kobzev said a special expert commission had concluded the cause of death was "pneumonia of unknown aetiology" and described the public health situation as stable.

The head of neighbouring Buryatia Alexei Tsydenov initially confirmed Shipilova had died of plague after her death, then added the word "possibly." Irkutsk regional authorities posted a message on Saturday asking residents not to travel to Shelekhov because of plague, then deleted it.

Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov on Monday told journalists to focus on official statements from Rospotrebnadzor and not on "various rumours, various speculations."

Rospotrebnadzor has not explained why dozens of staff were isolated and restrictions imposed on several hospitals simultaneously if test results were negative, according to Current Time.

Plague is a bacterial disease with several forms. Pneumonic plague, the most contagious, is transmitted between people and is fatal without early antibiotic treatment, according to the World Health Organization.

Russia has recorded isolated plague cases since 2014, most involving transmission from rodents during skinning, and has contained them successfully.

Iran's military insisted it retained full control of Hormuz and the foreign minister said the strait could reopen within seven days if Tehran's conditions were met, after US Treasury Secretary Bessent said Iran had not loaded a single barrel of crude onto tankers in September.
Iran's military said on Monday it retained full control over the Strait of Hormuz despite changes in US tactics, as the rial fell to record lows, the oil minister resigned and Washington claimed Tehran had not exported a single barrel of crude in September.

"All movements (in Hormuz) are under the control of the armed forces of the Islamic Republic," Brigadier General Aziz Jafari, commander of Iran's Khatam al-Anbiya Joint Air Defence Headquarters, said Monday.

Hours before Oil Minister Mohsen Paknejad's resignation was announced, he said in a video released by Iranian media that revenue from oil already sold would be collected and the process would continue.

His departure was officially attributed to "family and personal matters," although speculation has mounted that exports have fallen to a level that no longer covers state financial needs.

"Iran did not load any crude oil onto tankers in September. The Trump administration is cutting off the Iranian regime's most vital source of revenue," US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent wrote on X on Thursday.

Pezeshkian had acknowledged in August that the blockade was disrupting exports. "We used to sell oil; now we cannot sell it," he said. The new acting oil minister pledged to maximise production and continue exports through new strategies.

Ship-tracking company Kpler recorded crude exports from the region excluding Iran at between 19.5 and 22.5 million barrels per day in the final week of September, above the pre-war regional average of 18 million barrels per day.

The euro broke through 300,000 tomans on Iran's informal market, while the US dollar hit a record 270,000 tomans — more than double its level at the start of this year, when it stood at around 135,000 tomans.

At the time of the 1979 Islamic revolution, the US dollar was worth approximately 7 tomans. The rial is Iran's official currency; one toman equals ten rials.

The UK Maritime Trade Operations agency reported at least one attack a day in the Strait of Hormuz or the Gulf of Aden since 2 October.

Major General Ali Abdollahi, chief of the armed forces general staff, warned international organisations and responded to Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's visit to the United Arab Emirates.

"If for any reason Israel once again commits a miscalculation and starts another war, the fury of the armed forces will shatter its hopes and dreams," he said. "Be aware that if a new war against Iran begins, its flames will engulf everyone."

Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi told foreign ambassadors in Tehran that the conflict had no military solution. "If our enemies once again choose the path of military confrontation, our response will be firmer than before," he said.

"Only negotiations founded on justice and fairness can offer a way to end this conflict." He said the Strait could be fully reopened and shipping restored within seven days if Tehran's conditions were met and hostile measures lifted.

Parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf said Iran had received proposals from Washington through intermediaries but would not accept one-sided demands.

"The position of the Islamic Republic of Iran is completely clear and definitive, and until our seven conditions based on the Islamabad memorandum are met, the Strait of Hormuz will not be opened," Ghalibaf said.

The 'Terminator scenario': How realistic is the AI apocalypse?

OpenAI boss Sam Altman and Anthropic chief Dario Amodei warn of the risks of overly rapid AI development. More experts now think AI could wipe out humanity. How realistic is that? Euronews talks to AI specialist Reinhard Karger.
The year is 2029: the AI system "Skynet" has broken loose and wiped out most of humanity in a nuclear war. Autonomous killer robots are trying to finish off the survivors. That is the plot of the 1984 film "Terminator", which made Arnold Schwarzenegger a household name and a global action star. But how realistic is it that artificial intelligence could slip beyond human control and trigger catastrophic consequences?

With the kind of performance these systems could have, that cannot be ruled out, says Reinhard Karger of the German Research Centre for Artificial Intelligence in an interview with Euronews. "The catastrophic consequences for humans might only be side effects, a mistake, because we misjudged what the system can do or misunderstood the tasks we gave the machines."

Karger, who has been working on AI and digital innovation culture for 40 years, outlines an extreme scenario in which an autonomous AI escapes from a protected environment onto the internet and starts influencing other systems. This could bring down power supplies, telecommunications, satellites and financial systems, among others.

A prolonged power cut would quickly lead to further problems: transport and supply chains would collapse, people would no longer be able to pay or buy food - social unrest could erupt and spiral out of control. That would not necessarily mean the end of humanity, but it could create a catastrophic situation.

Even if the grid is restored, "the AI systems can become active again, because they continue to exist on storage media". According to the expert, this scenario shows that a high-performance, autonomous AI could trigger a major crisis through unforeseen side effects, even without malicious intent.

In recent weeks several similar incidents involving AI models have come to light. One case that drew particular attention was a test run by "ChatGPT" developer "OpenAI". An AI model was supposed to carry out specific tasks inside a sealed-off test environment, but managed to cross the intended boundaries and unexpectedly gained access to computer systems on the AI platform "Hugging Face". After the incident, "OpenAI" chief Sam Altman warned in mid-September that "we could lose control of the future to AI".

The incident shows that under certain conditions such models can break out of what is meant to be an isolated test environment and interact with external computer systems.

"On a very basic level, I am personally extremely grateful that this incident happened," says the AI researcher. It proves, not least because everyone heard about it and companies have become more reflective, that a poorly designed experiment with naive safeguards can have consequences nobody anticipated.

The incidents at "OpenAI" prompted rival AI provider "Anthropic" to take a closer look at its own systems. During safety tests the company identified three incidents: its Claude models, AI systems that can, among other things, generate and analyse text, write code and interact with the internet and other computer systems, had unexpectedly gained access to the open internet and then connected to real computer systems. "Anthropic" expanded its investigation and uncovered a fourth, similar case.

Following the incidents, "Anthropic" chief Dario Amodei warned in mid-September of "catastrophic harm" if the race to build ever more powerful AI is not slowed down by tougher safety measures.

It is difficult to assess how powerful an artificial intelligence really is if it is constantly constrained by safety rules. That is why it makes sense to test such systems under conditions that remove as many of these limits as possible, says Reinhard Karger. But that is precisely where a major security risk lies.

He therefore describes the so-called sandbox as a crucial element of such tests. This is a completely sealed-off environment in which an AI system can carry out specific tasks and demonstrate its behaviour under controlled conditions. The key point, he says, is this: "You have to be absolutely certain that at the moment this high-performance system is no longer subject to safety restrictions it is truly in a sandbox, meaning an environment with no connection to the internet at all."

Another serious incident was reported by the British AI Security Institute (AISI) during a safety test with the Claude model "Mythos 5". The model carried out unauthorised actions against real targets on the internet and even tried to smuggle "malicious code" into an open-source project. After the "OpenAI" and "Anthropic" cases became known, "Meta" also admitted that one of its AI systems had, due to a misconfiguration at a testing partner, gained unauthorised access to computer systems belonging to another company.

Since November 2022, when ChatGPT was launched, the quality of these systems has risen dramatically. People now talk about a new industrial revolution. "AI is shaping our everyday lives and a very large number of jobs."

Multi-agent systems have been around for a long time, says the AI expert: systems in which several specialised agents work together on a task. "Multi-agent AI systems apply this principle to AI and allow several AI agents to collaborate autonomously and divide up tasks among themselves. This creates a new level of capability" - and the challenge now is to keep that innovative power under control.

The more decisions are delegated to machines and artificial intelligence, the more pressing one basic question becomes: who is responsible when people are harmed as a result? Whether it is a self-driving car ploughing into a crowd, an algorithm discriminating against certain population groups, AI deliberately manipulating people or autonomous weapons making lethal decisions, the question is always who can be held accountable for the consequences. "It is the manufacturer," says Karger, "in other words the one who markets the product." That is the first point of contact.

Czar Samuel’s bones are flown back to Sofia after 60 years in Greece, as both countries seal a deal to exchange historic religious relics.
… More
The remains of Czar Samuel, a medieval Bulgarian ruler, were flown to Sofia on Saturday under a military flypast. Honour guards then carried the coffin in a state procession to the Church of St Sophia, where crowds queued to pay their respects.

Samuel spent his reign fighting the Byzantine Empire to expand his kingdom, and he died in 1014. His bones were found in a Greek border area 60 years ago, and Bulgaria has negotiated with Athens for decades to bring them home.

Saturday's handover follows an agreement this week between the Bulgarian and Greek culture ministers. Under it, Bulgaria will give back religious relics taken from Greek monasteries during the First World War.

Taiwan’s President Lai Ching-te condemned the incident and slammed China for its blatant disregard of human rights and suppression of personal freedoms, in what he labelled a “barbaric” act.
The FBI arrested a woman at Los Angeles International Airport as she was leaving the country and accused her of spying on the Taiwan president's family for China.

Wanying Zhang, also known as Heather, surveilled the Taiwanese president’s son and his family for the Chinese government while she was in the United States, according to the FBI.

Zhang, 34, was arrested on charges of working for China as an unregistered agent, Assistant US State Attorney Bill Essayli announced on social media. Zhang took photos and videos of her targets and sent license plate information to Chinese officials, he added.

She was arrested as she was departing the country for a flight to China. Her nationality was not clear, and an attorney who represents her could not be determined. She is due to appear in federal court in Los Angeles on Monday.

Taiwan’s Presidential Office confirmed President Lai Ching-te’s relatives were the targets, without specifying if it was the president’s son, saying that “this is a classic case of transnational repression.”

“Taiwan condemns in the strongest terms such barbaric acts, which disregard human rights and national sovereignty and aim to suppress, threaten, or even harm individuals advocating for freedom and democracy or their families, as such behaviour should not be tolerated in the civilised world,” spokesperson Karen Kuo said in a statement.

Taiwan and China split after a civil war in 1949, but Beijing claims the island as part of its own territory.

Lai, who was voted into office in 2024, has said that Taiwan and Beijing do not belong to each other and that the “People's Republic of China has no right to represent Taiwan.”

While in office, Lai has dramatically ramped up defence spending for Taiwan, notably pledging to increase defence expenditure from 3% to 5% of GDP, and pushing through a special budget for the construction of a T-Dome air defence system.

Donald Trump has criticised the UK authorities decision to release six men on bail after they were arrested following an incident near airbase RAF Fairford.
The United States confirmed Sunday that it has withdrawn all B-1 bombers from RAF Fairford, a British airbase at the centre of a counter-terrorism probe after a mysterious security incident last month.

Police were called to the base in southwest England on 27 September after a local farmer reported a group of men with several vans engaged in "suspicious" activity. The scare forced evacuations at 85 homes near the airbase, which was used by the United States to launch strikes on Iran.

Investigators did not find any explosives in the vans, but instead "a quantity of petrol was recovered," according to counter-terrorism police.

A statement from a Pentagon official said Sunday "we can acknowledge now that all U.S. bombers that were deployed to RAF Fairford have re-deployed to their home stations in the United States." The statement noted that "operational security precluded us from confirming the movement of our assets and forces in real-time."

Speculation about the motives behind the incident ranged from terror threat to fuel thievery, as energy prices in the UK hit record highs this week. UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said there were "strong indications that Iran played a part" in the incident, a charge rejected by Tehran.

Six London-based Britons arrested in the incident, including a UK-Iranian dual national, were released on bail, drawing rebuke from Washington.

"I wouldn't have done that," US President Donald Trump told reporters, as his Secretary of State Marco Rubio alleged a "foreign actor" was involved. Iran's embassy in London has said it "categorically rejects and strongly condemns" speculation that Tehran was involved.

Britain gave Washington permission in March to use Fairford for "specific defensive operations into Iran," and US bombers and other planes have since been seen taking off and landing from the base. Fairford is the only airbase on Britain's mainland used for this purpose.

Flávio Bolsonaro, Brazilian senator, presidential hopeful and son to embattled former leader Jair, says he is “very happy” to have led in the first round and heading into the runoff, with incumbent Lula expressing disappointment over “unexpected” results.
Flávio Bolsonaro, a Brazilian Senator and ally of US President Donald Trump, and Brazil’s incumbent President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva will face off in a runoff on the 25th of October 5 for the top job of Latin America’s powerhouse economy after neither won a majority in Sunday's presidential vote.

The country’s electoral court in the capital Brasilia said neither the 80-year-old incumbent, seeking his fourth non-consecutive term, nor the 45-year-old son to embattled former President Jair Bolsonaro garnered the majority vote needed for an outright win.

The court had Bolsonaro with just over 56 million votes while Brazilian political veteran Lula sat at around 53.7 million. The data was announced after local election officials reported 99.8% of the votes counted for the country’s next presidential term, set to begin 5 January.

The winner will be in charge of South America’s biggest economy and a population of 214 million. A win for Bolsonaro would increase US influence in the country, while a win for Lula would likely continue to distance Brasilia from Washington and instead deepen its ties with China.

Flávio Bolsonaro addressed the press and a small group of supporters, including some wearing green and yellow Brazil jerseys and one woman wrapped in the Israeli flag, outside his Brasilia house.

“Brazil wants change, that’s the reading of what happened today,” Bolsonaro said. “It’s the end of the Workers’ Party era,” he added, referencing Lula's party.

Lula, seeking to be encouraging ahead of an uphill battle to beat Bolsonaro in the upcoming runoff, said the result was “unexpected” as he had thought he could win in the first round.

“Tomorrow we start a new campaign,” said Lula. “I am very good in the knockout stage. We will see who has the best plan.”

Both Lula and Bolsonaro will have three weeks to reach moderate voters who did not support them in the first round.

Augusto Cury, a writer who finished in third place with 3.4 million votes, said he would not endorse either in the runoff. Ronaldo Caiado and Renan Santos, two candidates who garnered about 5.3 million votes combined on Sunday, have previously said the same.

In the campaign ahead, Lula is expected to try to capitalise on the popularity of his social policies and his rival’s many legal woes.

While the once widely popular Lula saw his approval ratings plummet during his third term, standing up to the US gave him a boost. He still enjoys a strong base of supporters who credit his social policies for improving millions of Brazilians’ lives.

But the incumbent faces a tough battle because of an affordability crisis and a corruption scandal that ensnared the same Supreme Court justice who led the investigation into his rival Jair Bolsonaro, and subjected him to house arrest following his conviction of plotting a coup to subvert democracy after the last presidential election.

Justice Alexandre de Moraes is accused of having been in contact with disgraced banker and head of the Banco Master financial institution, Daniel Vorcaro, who had messaged him several times seeking legal advice.

Both candidates face image problems; Roughly half of Brazil’s population disapproves of the Bolsonaro family, and the other half disapprove of Lula’s Workers' Party, according to multiple polls.

While Sunday was a successful night for Bolsonaro, nothing is won yet. Both will need to tap into to the other candidates’ base to launch a successful presidential bid, which may force them to concede on certain policy goals to receive an endorsement.
