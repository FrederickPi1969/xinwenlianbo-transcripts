Ukraine's city of Oleshky faces starvation as Russia blocks all aid
Ukraine to raise humanitarian crisis in Oleshky at UN General Assembly, says Foreign Ministry

Russia is shooting down food deliveries, confiscating supplies that reach the ground and blocking evacuation of some 2,000 residents still trapped in the occupied Kherson town, where the Council of Europe warns civilians face "a high risk of death" with no food, medicine, water or electricity.
Ukrainian forces have delivered around 4 tonnes of food to the Russian-occupied city of Oleshky in the Kherson region by drone in one of the largest such operations of the war, as roughly 2,000 remaining residents face what international organisations have described as a worsening humanitarian catastrophe.

Oleshky has been under Russian occupation since the early days of Moscow's full-scale invasion in February 2022. No food has reached the city through regular channels since May of this year, according to ground reports.

Meanwhile, Russian forces are blocking civilians from evacuating and actively obstructing drone deliveries.

Oleksandr Prokudin, head of the Kherson regional military administration, said troops are shooting down food-carrying drones, confiscating or reselling supplies that reach the ground, and detaining residents found with humanitarian parcels.

Ukrainian forces have been delivering aid by drone across the Dnipro since March, the regional administration said. The location and timing of individual operations are withheld for security reasons. Ukrainian troops hold positions on the right bank of the Dnipro, several kilometres from the city.

The regional military administration puts the population still in Oleshky at around 2,000, including nearly 50 children, down from 25,000 residents before Russia's all-out invasion in early 2022.

The All-Ukrainian Council of Churches and Religious Organisations estimates more than 6,000 people remain in the city and surrounding area, including around 200 children.

Ukrainian officials say anyone suspected of contact with Ukrainian authorities or armed forces faces immediate execution.

Conditions in the city have deteriorated sharply since the destruction of the Kakhovka hydroelectric dam in June 2023, which flooded large parts of the region, destroyed hundreds of homes and blocked wells and sewage systems. At least 200 to 300 people died in Oleshky in the flooding alone.

Residents now rely primarily on their own gardens for food, face chronic medicine shortages and move between abandoned buildings to avoid drone raids and looting by Russian troops, according to reports from the city.

"People are lying on the roads, rotting, unburied. Today in the basement of the hospital, the very one that has had no electricity for four years now, there are more than 100 corpses," Tetyana Hasanenko, an employee of the Oleshky city military administration, told ABC News.

The 34th Separate Marine Brigade "Borysfen," involved in the drone deliveries, published footage from the city in April that it described as showing a settlement Russian forces had "turned into a ghost town".

"People are forced to eat grass and weeds to survive. The roads are mined, it is almost impossible to get out safely, and shelling and drone attacks continue all around," Musician Artem Pivovarov said.

"There are reports that the bodies of those killed remain on the streets for months, there is no one to collect them."

Kyiv has drawn up a humanitarian evacuation plan that the International Committee of the Red Cross is prepared to join.

Ukraine's human rights ombudsman Dmytro Lubinets said Russia has not confirmed its readiness to allow the mission to proceed. Without agreed access, volunteers cannot enter the city on their own.

Sheep took over Southwark Bridge in London on Sunday, 27 September, as the city hosted its annual Woolmen’s Sheep Drive.
… More
The charity event pays tribute to the historic privileges of the Freemen of the City of London, who once had the right to drive livestock across the Thames without paying a toll. The modern version was revived in 2013.

For Gloria Hunniford, 86, it was a first. The television presenter, who received the Freedom of the City earlier this year, led the flock with a shepherd’s crook decorated with a pink ribbon in memory of her daughter, Caron Keating, who died of breast cancer in 2004.

Around 1,500 Freemen and guests joined the festivities, alongside rare-breed sheep, live shearing demonstrations and a wool fair with about 40 stalls. Some participants dressed as shepherds or Little Bo Peep.

The sheep came from a family-run farm in Bedfordshire and were selected for their calm temperament around crowds.The event raised money for The Woolmen Charity, which supports education, research, scholarships and innovation related to wool, while helping to preserve the heritage of the British wool industry and promote more sustainable practices. It also supported The Lord Mayor’s Appeal, a City of London fundraising initiative that backs charities and projects addressing social challenges, including youth employment, education, inclusion, health and poverty.

Fighting in the north of Ethiopia continued to rage over the weekend, raising fears of a return to a 2020-2022 war in which an estimated 600,000 people died.

Heavy clashes have resumed between the Tigray People's Liberation Front and the federal government since Wednesday.

The TPLF has seized airports and launched attacks into the neighbouring Ethiopian states of Afar and Amhara, saying they were engaged in a "defensive war" against the government of Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed.

The headquarters of Tigrai TV, seen as the key outlet for the rebel authorities in the Tigrayan capital Mekelle, was hit by a drone on Sunday.

Communications have been limited since Friday when telecoms providers reported major disruption to phone and internet services in Tigray, making reporting on the conflict difficult.

Many in Tigray fear a repeat of a blockade imposed by the federal government during the previous war when electricity, banking, and almost all aid were cut off.

A humanitarian source said the Afar town of Erebti, near the border with Tigray, fell to the rebels on Sunday after being pounded by heavy artillery.

The TPLF was now advancing towards the town of Afdeera, some 100 kilometres (60 miles) to the east.

The same source estimated that at least 150,000 people have been displaced in Afar, but said it was impossible to give precise numbers.

"With an uptake in fighting in Ethiopia, we see the risk to civilians spreading at a time when diplomatic attention and scrutiny of the situation have reduced, given the broader dynamics and catastrophes throughout the region as well", said Laetitia Bader, deputy director of the Africa division at HRW.

"The region is already facing devastating human rights and humanitarian crises", she added. "In neighbouring Sudan we are talking about the biggest humanitarian crisis in the world."

Meanwhile, Ethiopia's army commander on Sunday accused Egypt, Eritrea and Sudan of backing armed groups fighting its troops in the north of the country.

Speaking on the army's YouTube channel, army chief Field Marshal Birhanu Jula said, "We are fighting forces that are organised and allied to serve the interests of external forces."

"Neither Shaebia (Eritrea) nor the TPLF can achieve anything alone, even if they are allied, unless they are backed, supported and supplied by Sudan, Egypt and other external powers", he added.

Sudan's military said in a statement late on Sunday it "categorically rejects these allegations and considers them baseless", adding that Sudan was "committed to international law and the principles of good-neighbourly relations".

The TPLF has joined forces with six other armed groups around the country in what it dubbed the Ethiopian Peoples' Forces Alliance for Survival, aiming to overthrow Abiy's government and install a transitional administration.

Seven complexes holding more than 20,000 inmates are planned for 2035, including one in Russian-occupied Kherson, even as the prison population has dropped from 465,000 to 282,000 since 2021, largely because inmates have been recruited as contract soldiers for the war in Ukraine.
Russia plans to build seven large prison complexes by 2035 that will house both remand centres and penitentiary colonies, under a government decree amending the federal programme for the development of the penal system.

The facilities, which Russian journalists have called "superprisons," will together hold more than 20,000 people, 5,380 on remand and 15,120 convicted prisoners.

They will also include courtrooms and offices for prosecutors, defence lawyers and public monitoring commissions. The government plans to use surveillance and security technology throughout.

The complexes are planned for the Kaluga, Astrakhan and Tyumen regions, Buryatia and Zabaikalsky Krai, and the part of Ukraine's Kherson region currently under Russian military occupation.

The first facility is due to open in Tatarstan by 2030. Preliminary estimates put the cost of a single complex at between 13 billion and 20 billion roubles (€134 million and €208 million), with the total programme cost expected to exceed 230 billion roubles (€2.4 billion), according to Russian media reports.

Russia's Justice Ministry says consolidating remand and penal functions in single facilities will cut the cost of holding prisoners by 25–30% and allow the investment to pay for itself within 10 years, mainly by eliminating prisoner transfer costs and reducing staffing requirements.

The planned construction follows a sharp fall in the prison population. At the end of 2021, Russia held 465,000 people in detention but by 2026, that figure had dropped to 282,000 — a decline of nearly 40%.

Head of the Federal Penitentiary Service Arkady Gostev said in May that recruitment of prisoners as contract soldiers for Russia's war in Ukraine had contributed significantly to the reduction.

The recruitment of inmates began in the summer of 2022 — right after Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February of the same year — initially through the Kremlin-run paramilitary Wagner Group and later through the Defence Ministry directly.

Law experts have criticised the plans. They said internal rules at the new facilities were likely to remain unchanged or become more restrictive, and that relocating prisons from city centres to remote sites — particularly in Buryatia and Zabaikalsky Krai — would make it substantially harder for relatives, human rights activists and legal representatives to reach those held inside.

Russia's prison system has faced persistent criticism from international human rights bodies for conditions that fall below international standards.

The UN Special Rapporteur on Human Rights in Russia found in a 2025 report that torture remained "systematic and widespread," with 258 documented cases in 2024-2025. Common methods documented included electric shocks, beatings and mock executions.

The UN Human Rights Council, following a universal periodic review of Russia in 2024, called on Moscow to make torture a standalone criminal offence, strengthen investigation of reported cases and align its legal framework with the UN Convention Against Torture. Russia rejected the majority of those recommendations.

Human Rights Watch documented in 2024 that Russian authorities tortured suspects detained in connection with the Crocus City Hall attack near Moscow in March of that year, recorded the sessions and circulated the footage.

An OSCE monitoring mission found conditions in Russian detention facilities frequently included overcrowding, unsanitary facilities, inadequate food and medical care, and forced labour.

Alexei Navalny died in February 2024 in a remote Arctic penal colony where he was serving a 19-year sentence. The prison was reported by Russian independent media as known for harsh conditions and alleged torture.

Closures driven by per-pupil funding that made smaller rural schools unviable far outpaced demographic decline. Since 2022, all remaining schools hold mandatory weekly classes on Russian "traditional values" and the Kremlin's version of events surrounding Russia's war in Ukraine.
The number of schools in Russia has fallen by 45% since the start of the century, with rural communities losing the most, according to a report by the Higher School of Economics published this month.

Russia's secondary school network has shrunk from 68,800 institutions in 2000 to just under 38,000 in 2026, the report says. The school-age population has fallen by around 13.5% over the same period, from 20.5 million to 17.7 million children.

Russia now has fewer schools than Germany, even though its population is roughly half the size. Germany has more than 40,000 schools in 2026.

Closures have been most severe in rural areas, where the number of state schools has fallen from more than 45,000 to around 21,000, a reduction of more than half.

Urban schools have also declined, from 22,700 in 2000 to 16,300 today. The number of pupils each school serves has grown substantially over the same period: from around 300 pupils per school 15 years ago to around 470 today.

Private schools have increased in number, from 635 in 2000 to 982 in 2026, but account for only 2.5% of total provision.

A shift to per-pupil funding made smaller schools financially unviable: the fewer children a school enrolled, the less state money it received. Schools were merged with neighbouring institutions or closed outright.

By 2010, 26% of Russia's schools had shut. Around 10,000 more closed in the 2010s, and a further 2,300 since the early 2020s.

Teachers and parents protested the mergers. They argued that large consolidated institutions were harder to manage, that neighbourhood schools with distinct characters were being lost, and that overcrowded classes would reduce educational quality.

Sociologists have also linked school closures to village depopulation, according to a 2026 study published in the Russian magazine Nozh.

The study found that when a village school closes, families typically split — one parent relocates to a town with the children while the other stays to work — and the separation becomes permanent.

"By saving on the costs of a single classroom and teacher, you can wipe out a whole thriving local economy," the study said.

The village of Atary in Kirov region's Lebyazhsky district is one example: after the local school was declared unprofitable, young people left, and the settlement is now largely abandoned.

Communist Party MP Dmitry Novikov has called for rural schools to be funded as strategic social infrastructure rather than on a per-pupil basis.

He has proposed a moratorium on closing upper secondary classes in villages and, where closures go ahead, guaranteed year-round transport to schools in neighbouring areas.

Since September 2022, all Russian schools have held a mandatory weekly class called "Conversations about What Matters," with lesson plans supplied directly by the Ministry of Education.

Göktaş's arrest was the latest in a widening crackdown on anyone seen as critical of Turkey's conservative government or its values, which has netted hundreds of politicians, artists, musicians, journalists and LGBTQ+ activists.
A popular Turkish comedian has gone on trial at an Istanbul court on Monday on charges of insulting President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan and other offences during a stand-up performance in June.

Deniz Göktaş, a 32-year-old comedian known for his dry wit and political satire, was arrested by police on 3 July, a week after releasing a YouTube video of his show that was watched nearly 9 million times.

Held in pre-trial detention ever since, Göktaş is facing charges of insulting Erdoğan and incitement to public hatred, with prosecutors later adding an extra charge of praising crime and criminals, according to the indictment.

Göktaş's arrest was the latest in a widening crackdown on anyone seen as critical of Turkey's conservative government or its values, which has netted hundreds of politicians, artists, musicians, journalists and LGBTQ+ activists among others.

In the pouring rain, around 50 protesters with umbrellas gathered outside Istanbul's Çağlayan courthouse under the watch of several dozen police, chanting: "Denis Göktaş is not alone!" an AFP correspondent said.

Several dozen police were also inside as a crowd of journalists and supporters waited to enter the courtroom where Göktaş was expected to attend in person.

Among them were several comedians from Istanbul's TuzBiber comedy club where Göktaş reportedly began his career in 2019.

The comedian is being held at Karatepe high-security prison, about 120 kilometres west of Istanbul and has been able to give an occasional post on X, mostly wry observations about life behind bars.

"After 88 days, I'll be handcuffed for the first time and hear a car horn. I'm definitely not missing the handcuffs," he posted late Sunday, on the eve of his first day in court.

"A 90-minute show spawns a new charge every two months," he wrote in late August, saying they had added the extra charge against him "for a joke about the Daltons," one of Turkey's organised crime groups.

Although the indictment did not clearly outline the sentence, calculations by the Turkish media suggested that if convicted on all charges, he could face up to a decade or more behind bars, Anka news agency reported, with other outlets giving a similar figure.

According to Turkish media, the Ankara-born comedian began his career at TuzBiber in Istanbul and has gone on to perform at venues across Europe and the US.

Thousands of pilgrims filled Lourdes on Sunday evening for a candlelit procession with Pope Leo XIV, as he brought his four-day visit to France to a close.
… More
Holding a candle and a rosary, the pope walked behind a statue of the Virgin Mary through the sanctuary, describing himself as a “pilgrim among pilgrims”. The procession began at the Grotto of Massabielle, where Bernadette Soubirous is said to have seen the Virgin Mary in 1858.

The Rosary is a series of Catholic prayers traditionally divided into five decades, with worshippers reflecting on events from the lives of Jesus and Mary. The prayers were recited in several languages, including French, English, Italian and Spanish, accompanied by the “Ave Maria” of Lourdes.

More than 150,000 candles were lit across the sanctuary, creating one of Lourdes’ most recognisable evening ceremonies.

Earlier, Leo had celebrated an outdoor Mass attended by around 150,000 people, met French bishops and visited sick and disabled pilgrims. He also held private meetings with survivors of clergy sexual abuse.

The United States is required by law to supply Taiwan, which has a democratically elected president and legislature, with weapons for its defence under the 1979 Taiwan Relations Act.
US President Donald Trump offered to sell arms to Beijing in response to concerns over weapons sales to Taiwan, the US ambassador to China said in a Fox News interview that aired on Sunday.

China claims Taiwan is part of its territory and US arms sales to the self-ruled island have long been a thorny issue between Washington and Beijing.

The United States is required by law to supply Taiwan, which has a democratically elected president and legislature, with weapons for its defence under the 1979 Taiwan Relations Act.

US Ambassador to China David Perdue said the issue was raised again when Chinese President Xi Jinping visited the United States last week.

"President Trump continues to say, 'Hey, we sell arms to other people around the world.' He actually asked President Xi would he like to buy some at one point," Perdue said in the interview that aired on Sunday.

"There are continuing conversations about other sales, (Trump) and President Xi talk about that every time they get together," Perdue said, adding the US position has not changed.

In June, China warned Taiwan that seeking independence "by relying on the United States or through military means is a dead end."

Washington had already approved a $11.1 billion (€9.7 billion) arms sale to Taiwan in December, which Perdue said was a sale of "more arms to Taiwan than any other president since 1979."

During his Washington trip, Xi repeated his warning that Trump should show "prudence" over Taiwan, and went further to urge the United States to oppose Taiwan independence explicitly.

In May, Trump suggested an upcoming arms package to Taipei could be used as a negotiating chip with China, raising fears Taiwan's security could be traded away.

North Korean soldiers were among the foreign contingent conscripted to fight alongside Russian troops on various frontlines in the war on Ukraine, after the Kremlin sought assistance from its close allies to bolster its deployments amid struggles with domestic conscription.
South Korea's President’s Lee Jae Myung’s office demanded an apology from Ukraine for denying an agreement to keep the transfer of two North Korean prisoners of war from its territory to Seoul confidential.

The row was ignited by Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy's disclosure last week that his country had sent two North Korean soldiers to the South, adding that they were "not easy to capture alive".

The intensifying dispute risks complicating Kyiv's efforts to obtain weapons from allies, including South Korea, to fight off bombardments from Russia.

Hours after Zelenskyy's disclosure, South Korea's President Lee Jae Myung criticised the action.

"I do not know why the Ukrainian side broke the agreement and made the matter public, but it is very disappointing," he wrote on X.

Zelenskyy's aide Dmytro Lytvyn sought to play down the disclosure, saying that the president had only made public the fact that the soldiers were handed to South Korea. Ukraine is an "open country", noted Lytvyn, saying that it would be “unusual” to hand over the soldiers in secret.

Seong Ghi-hong, senior presidential secretary for public relations, said in a statement late on Sunday that South Korea was "considering additional measures that may be necessary regarding this matter".

"It is in itself a very serious matter that the Ukrainian side violated the confidentiality agreement between the two countries and made the disclosure unilaterally,” he said.

"But the Ukrainian presidential press office's false statement that there had been no confidentiality agreement, seriously undermines trust between the two governments and gravely damages friendly relations between the two countries," he added.

The South Korean Presidential office demands "an official explanation and apology," said Seong.

Ukraine captured early last year two North Korean soldiers among Pyongyang's troops fighting for Moscow. One of them said he served in a reconnaissance unit and while some North Korean soldiers had been trained to use Russian military equipment, he had not.

The soldier said he was conscripted to the military aged 17 and had been serving since then. A South Korean lawmaker had previously said the pair wanted to live a "normal life" in the South of the Peninsula.

Analysts believe Lee Jae Myung’s angry reaction to the confidentiality agreement risks sparking intense feuds with the nuclear-armed north, which could translate into further hostility towards Seoul.

"North Korea has been using the deployed soldiers to create a heroic narrative for regime propaganda, and the South Korean government appears concerned that confirming the prisoners' return to the South and publicising it could further fuel hostility towards Seoul," said Park Won-gon, a professor at Ewha Womans University.

Beyond the ramifications within South Korea, the row also risks complicating Kyiv's requests to the ally for armaments supplies.

South Korea has so far not provided lethal weapons to Ukraine, citing a domestic ban on militarily aiding countries at war. Nevertheless, it had been considering requests for defensive weapons, especially since North Korea deployed troops to support Russia.
