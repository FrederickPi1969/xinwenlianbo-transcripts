Kärcher boss: Less regulation, more action to get Europe and Germany fit again
Hartmut Jenner, Chairman of the Executive Board of Alfred Kärcher SE & Co. KG

The opinions expressed in this article are those of the author and do not represent in any way the editorial position of Euronews.
Kärcher, best known for its pressure washers, recorded €3.5 billion in sales in 2025 and employs 17,000 people worldwide. Its CEO warns of a 'perfect storm' and calls for a fitness plan for Germany and Europe.
For some time now, we in Europe are facing a perfect storm. Geopolitical crises are casting a shadow over the European economy. The unpredictable trade policy of the U.S. keeps threatening, without warning, to raise tariffs and erect trade barriers – a toxic scenario for free global trade and export-oriented companies.

Meanwhile, China is no longer merely a low-cost manufacturer but has become a highly aggressive technological competitor that, with its tremendous speed, threatens to outpace key European industries in future-oriented fields.

What is often perceived as pure technological supremacy or even innovative strength is, however, largely the result of a well-targeted governmental industrial policy. Massive subsidies in key strategic industries, along with a currency policy artificially focused on competitiveness, are leading to noticeable market distortions and overcapacity.

For private companies operating on a market-based basis, this creates an imbalance that makes fair global competition almost impossible.

The consequences for the industrial heart of Europe are alarming. The loss of tens of thousands of industrial jobs and the decline in production volume show that deindustrialization is not an abstract threat – it is happening right now.

When plant closures and production relocations are being negotiated in key German industries such as the automotive and manufacturing sectors, it must be clear even to the most optimistic observer: We are at a critical turning point.

As a family-owned company with 170 subsidiaries in 87 countries, Kärcher has weathered these turbulent times robustly thanks to its balanced business model – combining B2B (editor's note: Business to Business, i.e. corporate customers) and B2C (editor's note: Business to Consumer, i.e. private customers) – and its unconditional focus on customers. We are investing significantly in research and development, setting new standards in autonomous cleaning robots and battery technologies, and driving digitalization across all areas of the company.

However, even innovation leaders cannot withstand increasingly challenging operating conditions and unfair competition in the long term. Considering the massive rise in structural and employee costs, expanding capacity or increasing headcount in domestic production is no longer economically feasible.

While the latest economic forecasts – which expect Germany’s growth to return to 1.3 percent this year – seem cautiously optimistic at first glance, we must not be misled by this modest increase. What we are seeing here is largely “borrowed growth,” artificially driven by special government funds, defense contracts, and subsidies.

A short-term boost fueled by debt is no substitute for a real, self-sustaining recovery in private investments. And the causes of the ongoing weakness are largely homegrown: a creeping reform fatigue and a regulatory frenzy on the part of government institutions that hinders rather than facilitates investment. Instead of promoting entrepreneurship, regulations from Brussels to Berlin are suffocating the courage to embrace change.

The ongoing uncertainty among people in Germany is also having an impact on society. The rise of political extremism is a clear sign that many people are calling for greater reliability and noticeable reforms. To regain confidence in our country’s economic strength and future viability, we now need clear direction and bold action.

For me personally, the attitude towards “working hard” has been ingrained in me since childhood on my parents’ family farm. Success doesn’t just fall from the sky; for me, it consists of 97 percent hard work and dedication, 2 percent talent, and 1 percent luck.

If we want to secure our prosperity and our social standards, we must once again recognize that value creation is the precondition for prosperity – and not the other way around. If you rest, you rust – that applies to people just as much as it does to economies.

That is why we need a real fitness program for Germany. Rigid rules, excessive bureaucracy, high energy costs, and steadily rising non-wage labor costs are poison for our business environment. If the total amount of social security contributions consistently far exceeds the historically critical threshold of 40 percent, then tax breaks, selective government subsidies, and special funds will amount to nothing more than ineffective paper measures as long as the business environment itself remains entrenched in its current state.

While the federal government’s political reform efforts are heading in the right direction, they fall far short of what is needed. Instead of tackling the root of the problem, authorities are allowing a flood of regulations to continue – regulations that tie up valuable employee time and cost our companies millions. Money and personnel that are needed for core business operations and innovation are being wasted on documentation requirements alone. This cannot be solved with cosmetic fixes.

We need the courage to provide real relief. A key lever for this would be, for example, a cultural shift within the administrative system: In the future, government departments must see themselves as digital enablers and service-oriented partners to the economy – pragmatic, fast, and AI-supported. If other countries are already moving toward eliminating reporting obligations overall, this must send a clear signal to Germany and all Europe.

Furthermore, we should not avoid necessary debates on the labor market. Demographic trends require an increase in the overall volume of work in the economy. Incentives for overtime, flexible working hours, practical rules regarding continued pay during medical leave, and a noticeable reduction in bureaucracy when employing skilled workers are essential.

Those who reflexively condemn such debates as an attack on workers’ rights are turning a blind eye to reality and putting precisely those jobs at risk that they claim to be protecting. At the same time, we need a resolute trade policy at the European level that guarantees fair competition and consistently punishes rule-breakers – because free trade only works when everyone follows the same rules.

If we truly want to secure our competitiveness in the long term, we don’t need a bureaucratic approach – we need a return to a can-do mentality: less regulation, more personal responsibility, ambitious investment incentives, reliable framework conditions, and, above all, the courage to tackle issues properly and comprehensively once again. Real change begins in the mind – it’s time to act.

Alfred Kärcher SE & Co. KG was founded in 1935 and generated almost 3.5 billion euros in revenue in 2025. The family-owned company based in Winnenden, Germany, earns 86 percent of this abroad. The Kärcher Group operates 170 companies in 87 countries and employs 17,000 people worldwide. Kärcher's portfolio comprises more than 3,000 products for cleaning and care, with Kärcher high-pressure cleaners being particularly well known.

The US State Department announced on Friday that Washington had green-lit a package of Patriot PAC-3 interceptors worth $2.1 billion as Kyiv continues to defend against Russian missile and drone attacks.

"Five hundred PAC-3 interceptors is a substantial number, and very soon – this winter – they COULD make for a highly effective defence package," Zelenskyy wrote in a post on social media in response.

"However, it has been stipulated that deliveries may begin ONLY IN A FEW YEARS," he continued. "We are asking our American partners to find a solution for the time when there is a real danger and a real need to use PAC-3 interceptors."

Earlier this week, Zelenskyy said Kyiv had been waiting for almost a month for US Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth to sign off on a batch of missiles for both the Patriot and NASAMS systems.

Kyiv has long called for more interceptors to help its forces fend off Russian attacks, and the rapidly approaching winter is pressing home that need. Russia has repeatedly sought to target Ukraine's power grid over wartime winter periods as Moscow has looked to break the country's resolve.

Dwindling stocks of interceptors have already encouraged Russia to ramp up missile attacks in recent months, and there are fears this trend could lead to an especially brutal winter for Ukrainians.

"The United States, our European partners, and other partners around the world have sufficient stocks of PAC-3 interceptors in their warehouses," Zelenskyy said overnight. "Providing them to Ukraine right now – in the winter of 2026–2027 – and replenishing our partners’ arsenals LATER, in THE FOLLOWING YEARS, through this production already contracted and paid for by Ukraine and our partners would be the right approach."

The Patriot missile platform is one of the world's most advanced air defence systems and can be used to defend against a variety of threats, including incoming aircraft and ballistic missiles.

Ukraine receives the majority of its Patriots through NATO’s Prioritised Ukraine Requirements List (PURL) initiative, which facilitates the purchase of military equipment and munitions from the US by allies and partners.

As of June 2026, allies had committed to funding more than $6 billion of defence equipment for Ukraine through the scheme, per NATO.

Changes were not limited to the texts; images and illustrations were also edited in Photoshop, adding hijabs or long robes to female figures, deleting women's photos, and covering or replacing statues, sculptures and artworks.
A set of changes made to a number of school textbooks in Syria has sparked a wave of criticism and reactions on social media, after they included alterations to pictures and artworks, among them adding the hijab to drawn female figures, removing images of women and children, covering statues and replacing paintings and works of art. The revisions have also affected teaching content dealing with political life, citizenship, democratic participation, local administration and the media.

These changes have reopened the debate over the standards used by the Ministry of Education when it reviews curricula, and how far they take into account Syria's social and cultural diversity, amid questions about the nature and aims of the amendments, and whether they fall simply within a review of content linked to the former regime or go further towards reshaping how society is represented in school books.

Comparisons posted on social media between different editions of school textbooks show amendments to material for several grades, including the early years and seventh grade, as well as the sixth-grade primary textbook "Social Studies".

It emerges that this last book, reissued for the current academic year, originally dates back to the 2012–2013 school year, with changes added that include the removal of material linked to the former regime, alongside lessons on local administration, political life, citizenship, the media and democratic participation.

The changes were not limited to texts; they also extended to images and illustrations using Photoshop. Hijabs were added – and in some cases long robes – to drawn female characters, entire pictures of women were deleted, statues were covered, and sculptures and artworks were removed or replaced with others.

The visual alterations to paintings and expressive illustrations became a main focus of controversy, after committees removed the world-famous painting "Mona Lisa" and deleted a drawing showing a girl skipping rope beside two children in textbooks for early age groups.

The revisions also extended to historical figures and symbols. The Phoenician princess Elissar, founder of Carthage, was affected when a painting depicting her on a hunting trip was dropped and replaced with another portrait that had been digitally modified to cover the chin and neck – a step critics saw as imposing contemporary ideological standards on an ancient historical era.

One of the examples that provoked ridicule and debate was dressing a pharaonic statue in a striped shirt, which many users likened to prison uniforms.

A new batch of visual comparisons circulated on social media documented far-reaching changes introduced to the new Syrian school curricula in particular history books, with the new editions showing the removal of prominent national and pan-Arab figures and the alteration of the content of historical passages, amid sharp questions and concerns about systematic ideological and sectarian exclusion.

Activists highlighted information that caused widespread anger and was described as "a blatant falsification of history". The updated curriculum contains passages portraying the "martyrs" executed on 6 May by the Ottoman governor Jamal Pasha, known as the Butcher, as "agents of Britain and France", instead of their historical description as symbols of the national movement resisting Ottoman colonial rule.

The changes also include deleting the picture of Sultan Pasha al-Atrash and replacing it with that of Sheikh Saleh al-Ali, a move that raised fears of exclusion on sectarian grounds. The alterations further removed a photograph of the late Egyptian president Gamal Abdel Nasser.

The revisions reached school maps too, with the district of Iskenderun and the occupied Syrian Golan omitted from one of the maps, as well as the deletion of a paragraph on the role of Syrian women in resisting the French Mandate. This has raised questions about the motives behind these changes and their impact on the historical narrative presented to pupils.

The nature of these changes and how they were carried out unleashed a wave of sarcastic comments on social platforms, where many users felt some of the alterations were random and lacked unified artistic standards to ensure visual coherence within a single textbook.

The most widely shared example in this context is a female doctor character in first-grade books who appears wearing a hijab on one page and without a hijab on another.

The comments did not stop at the artistic side, but also questioned the rationale for removing artworks and illustrated characters from school textbooks, and the criteria used to decide which images should be kept, modified or removed.

Among parents, objections went beyond the quality of the illustrations or the inconsistencies in modifying them, to the implications of these changes and their possible impact on how children view society and diversity.

Speaking to Euronews, Alia, the mother of a lower-secondary pupil, said she had not at first noticed the amendments because of the poor print quality and lack of detail in the drawings, pointing out that the heavy academic content and padding make it difficult for both parents and pupils to focus on visual details.

She argued that editing images of historical figures by adding a hijab raises questions about the purpose of such a step, and felt that replacing the pictures with new drawings would have been a more logical option than tampering with the original works. She warned that imposing a single pattern in the way women are depicted could shape how social diversity is presented to children, instead of promoting values of harmony and coexistence.

She added that priority should be given to developing the curricula and keeping pace with global shifts in education, rather than becoming preoccupied with cosmetic changes that add no clear educational value. In her view, such practices risk deepening divisions and fuelling intolerance instead of reducing it.

Alia went on: "The highest priority in this critical phase should be to fundamentally develop the educational curricula and keep up with the latest global changes in the education sector", considering that focusing on superficial visual adjustments does not serve the learning process or offer pupils any clear educational benefit.

Concluding her remarks to Euronews, she warned that the persistence of such practices within educational institutions could produce the opposite effect, as they help to deepen ideological divisions in society and feed exclusionary and fanatical thinking rather than mitigating it and strengthening shared citizenship values.

In the same vein, Jaber, the father of a primary-school girl, expressed even stronger concerns in his interview with Euronews, arguing that these visual changes cannot be separated from ideological orientations that risk being entrenched in society's consciousness through the education system. Jaber called on international organisations concerned with children's rights to take urgent action to safeguard children's right to receive balanced and neutral education.

What connects an Iranian novelist exploring women's lives, a British children's author creating imaginary worlds and an Azerbaijani writer preserving her country's cultural identity? The answer is stories that cross borders, connect continents, and blend cultures through books.
From the poetry of medieval Azerbaijan to contemporary novels reaching readers from across different continents, Baku's relationship with the written word is rooted in centuries of cultural exchange.

Today, that literary heritage is finding new expression as authors, publishers and readers from Europe, Asia and beyond came together in the Azerbaijani capital for the twelfth year running.

The 12th Baku International Book Fair saw 32 countries taking part in an event which was not simply about discovering the latest books.

It was an opportunity to explore how literature connects hearts and minds across languages, generations, and geographical barriers by blending and sharing ideas and stories.

At a time when technology is transforming how tales are told, published, and consumed, the fair also questioned how books will remain an intrinsic and vital part of our futures.

Azerbaijan's literary traditions stretch back centuries and have been shaped by its position at the crossroads of Europe and Asia.

He was born in what is now Azerbaijan and became one of the most influential literary figures of the Persian-speaking world, with his works travelling the globe. He continues to be celebrated today.

Centuries later, writers and poets include Muhammad Fuzuli, who contributed to a rich literary tradition that developed across different languages and cultural influences.

This heritage provides a historical and rich backdrop to the country's modern relationship with international literature, where translation, publishing partnerships and literary festivals help stories reach audiences far beyond local borders and countries of origin.

For Iranian novelist Parinoush Saniee, literature offers a way of exploring the relationships between individuals and the societies in which they live.

Her internationally recognised novel The Book of Fate follows the life of an Iranian woman through decades of personal and political change, examining themes of identity, family, education and the expectations placed upon women.

Saniee explained that she initially assumed her work would only resonate regionally, but she was quickly surprised by the global reaction.

"When I saw all that reception and various journalists came from different places, I asked them what was interesting about the book for you. They said it was close to us. We also had such experiences. It was very relatable."

Although rooted in Iranian society, the questions raised in her writing resonate far beyond its borders. The works have been translated into numerous languages and demonstrate how literature can offer readers an understanding of lives and experiences different from their own.

She observed this same phenomenon when the novel became a bestseller in Norway, proving that universal themes of suffering and endurance translate seamlessly.

"Human suffering, lifestyles, issues, and problems are overall very impactful for humanity, and we all understand it," Saniee stated. "Therefore, it doesn't matter where it is from."

British author Darren Simpson, meanwhile, approaches storytelling through the imagination of younger readers.

He is known for novels such as Scavengers and The Memory Thieves, where he creates unusual worlds and characters exploring friendships, belonging, identity and the challenges of growing up.

Simpson echoed the sentiment that human emotion makes storytelling cross borders effortlessly, noting that children across the globe seek the exact same things from a narrative.

"When I see children from all around different countries connect to my stories in the same way, it speaks to me about the universal nature of a lot of feelings about people's needs and their desire to connect," Simpson explained.

His works reflect the importance of children's literature at a time when arguably young people have an unprecedented choice of digital entertainment and books are not necessarily the most popular choice. Yet, he highlighted that the local reception at the fair had been incredibly inspiring.

"I have been struck in Azerbaijan by how much passion there is here in the young readers," he added.
