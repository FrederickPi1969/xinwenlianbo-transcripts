Russia expels Hungarian diplomats as Budapest breaks with Orbán era

Moscow ordered Hungarian embassy and consulate staff out just weeks after Budapest expelled 10 Russian diplomats on national security grounds in September, the first such move by Hungary since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.
Russia expelled a group of Hungarian diplomats on Thursday after Hungary ordered 10 Russian diplomats out last month, marking a sharp break from the era of former Premier Viktor Orbán, who maintained closer ties with Moscow than any other EU leader.

Hungary's chargé d'affaires Gábor Gergics was summoned to Russia's foreign ministry and informed that staff from the Hungarian embassy in Moscow and consulates in St Petersburg and Kazan had been ordered to leave within two weeks.

Moscow described Hungary's original 8 September expulsion as a "baseless and openly unfriendly" act.

"Hungary is not interested in an escalation of diplomatic tensions. But we stand by our decision, taken on national security grounds. Our missions continue to operate in full, and Hungary will maintain its diplomatic presence in Russia," the Hungarian foreign ministry wrote on X.

Foreign Minister Anita Orbán said at the time of the initial expulsion that the Russian diplomats "had been engaged in activities in Hungary that are unacceptable for diplomats under the Vienna Convention."

The mutual expulsions follow a shift in Hungarian foreign policy since Prime Minister Péter Magyar took office earlier this year.

At the end of August, the new government's guidance to its delegation at the UN General Assembly officially described Russia as a threat to Hungary and to the "global order," condemned its ongoing all-out war in Ukraine and affirmed support for Ukrainian sovereignty and its right to self-defence.

Hungary is also seeking to improve relations with Kyiv, although Budapest is blocking the opening of new EU accession negotiation clusters with Ukraine to secure concessions on the rights of the country's Hungarian minority.

Orbán, who was in power for 16 years until his party Fidesz' loss in general elections this April, openly sympathised with the Kremlin and refused to join other EU member states in expelling Russian diplomats after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022.

He made multiple visits to Moscow after the invasion and repeatedly used Hungary's veto in the European Council to block Ukraine-related initiatives.

Young students from China and abroad train in tai chi and kung fu on the Wudang Mountains, where Taoist philosophy shapes martial arts and daily life.
… More
A range of mountains in central China has become a top destination for students eager to learn Taoist martial arts. In this part of the Wudang range, young practitioners train in tai chi and kung fu at schools set among temples and forested slopes.

Taoism has deep roots at Wudang. Legend holds that Laozi’s first disciple settled on the mountain more than 2,500 years ago, helping spread teachings that later formed a major branch of Taoism.

On these breathtaking slopes, committed students search for inner stillness through the art of tai chi.

The Supreme Court proposals would allow presiding judges to "clarify" questions during deliberations and reduce jury panels from six or eight members to four, changes lawyers say undermine the independence of a system that acquits at 60 times the national average in non-jury trials.
Russia's Supreme Court is preparing legislation that would allow presiding judges to enter the deliberation room while a jury discusses its verdict and would reduce jury panels from six or eight members to four — changes that could greatly consolidate decision-making power in the hands of state-appointed officials.

Deputy Chair of the Supreme Court Nikolai Timoshin and Supreme Court judge Sergei Zelenin have outlined the main provisions of the planned reform, although the draft law has not yet been published.

Under the proposals, the presiding judge would not be permitted to vote on a defendant's guilt but would be entitled to "clarify" questions arising during deliberations.

Additional provisions would change the procedure for selecting jurors, allow citizens over 65 to serve, restrict the grounds for dissolving an already formed panel and give defendants the right to refuse a jury trial even after a hearing has been scheduled.

Jury trials are rare in Russia but produce acquittals at a rate sharply above the national average. Of more than 640,000 criminal cases heard in Russia in 2025, 643 were decided by a jury, or around 0.1%.

In the first half of 2025, 144 of 444 defendants in jury cases were acquitted, close to one in three, according to Professor Sergei Nasonov of Moscow State University of Law, cited by Current Time. Across the Russian criminal justice system as a whole, the acquittal rate is below 0.5%.

Zelenin has argued that Russia's current jury model draws heavily from the Anglo-American legal tradition and fits poorly with the continental legal system, and that closer interaction between judge and jury would make proceedings more stable.

The proposed model is closer to the European lay judge system, in which a professional judge and non-specialist members sit together as a single panel.

Supporters of the reform also cite the high rate at which jury acquittals are overturned on appeal — between 65% and more than 80% in different years, according to figures cited by the Supreme Court.

Critics argue that grounds for overturning verdicts are typically described as procedural breaches, which can be interpreted broadly: from paperwork errors to remarks by trial participants that courts treat as improper influence on the jury.

Lawyers' objections centre mainly on admitting the judge to deliberations, which they say undermines the jury's independence.

Reducing the panel to four also raises questions about how much weight public participation carries in verdicts.

One source cited by Current Time said the reform amounts to legalising practices already in use informally: "Judges on the ground do not want this, none of the law enforcers need these courts. They fight them as best they can. Now they are simply legalising what they are already doing anyway."

The same source said jurors had frequently exposed weaknesses in prosecution cases, showing that some were "stitched together with white thread," a Russian expression for crude fabrication.

Jury trials have existed in Russia since the judicial reforms of Tsar Alexander II in 1864. They were abolished in the Soviet period, restored in the early 1990s and have been revised several times since.

The Constitutional Court has previously emphasised the secrecy of deliberations and the independence of jurors as fundamental safeguards of a fair trial.

Israeli passengers on the flydubai flight carrying 174 people from the United Arab Emirates to the Israeli city of Tel Aviv have described how the bloodied Indian captain managed to open the cockpit door to let them in before they grappled with the copilot and stabilised a descent so sharp that the plane’s rudder was torn away.

India's Prime Minister Narendra Modi hailed Machchhar as a "HERO" in comments on Thursday.

"In the face of the gravest danger and despite being seriously injured, he showed immense courage and an unwavering resolve to protect the lives of others," Modi said.

"His valour helped save hundreds of lives and avert a great tragedy. India is proud of Captain Machchhar. Praying for his speedy recovery, renewed strength and good health."

US President Donald Trump told reporters at the White House that he and Netanyahu discussed the incident “at length” over the phone and that it seemed as though the copilot “was perhaps a terrorist or perhaps a whack job."

Netanyahu said he spoke with the UAE’s president, Sheikh Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan, who agreed that Israel would join the interrogation of the suspect.

Israel’s foreign ministry said the people on board were from Israel, India and the UAE. The US State Department said four passengers had American citizenship.

Flydubai has said its flights to and from Israel would be suspended while an investigation takes place.

Scientists monitoring the Rhône Glacier in Switzerland are measuring rapid ice loss after a summer of high temperatures.

They track meltwater, use dye to follow its flow and measure how much the surface has dropped.

Swiss glaciers lost 5.5% of their remaining ice volume in 2026, the second-largest annual decline on record. Persistent summer heat waves and a dry winter with little snowfall left the ice with less protection and caused melting to start earlier.

Some glaciers have lost between 2.5 and 4 metres in thickness this year, with losses of up to 10 metres recorded on some glacier tongues.

Tennessee death row inmate Christa Pike was taken to hospital on Thursday after authorities "failed" to carry out her execution, her lawyers said, with witnesses reporting the convicted murderer was breathing despite two injections intended to kill her.

Just hours earlier the US Supreme Court cleared the way for Pike to be the first woman to die by lethal injection in the southern US state of Tennessee in 200 years.

Tennessee corrections officials did not publicly confirm the outcome of the execution, which included two injections of pentobarbital.

"Tonight the State of Tennessee once again failed to carry out a lawful execution," Pike's attorneys said in a statement.

"Christa is being treated at a nearby hospital. We have not been informed as to her condition."

Pike was scheduled for execution at 10:00 am (1700 CET) at the Riverbend Maximum Security Institution, where witnesses including journalists were present for the administration of the injections.

"It appears this did not go how this was supposed to go," said investigative journalist John North of WBIR who was a witness at the execution site, adding "when we left the building, so far as we knew, she was probably still alive."

During the livestream, an ambulance with its lights flashing could be seen leaving the site of the execution, it's unclear if it was transporting Pike.

The execution procedure went ahead hours after the US Supreme Court ruled that it could proceed, with liberal justices Sonia Sotomayor, Elena Kagan and Ketanji Brown Jackson all dissenting.

"The Court's decision...unnecessarily prevents that [lower] court from giving due consideration to Pike's claim," the dissenting justices wrote.

"Worse, the Court grants such extraordinary relief solely to allow the State to execute Pike before litigation over her conviction has properly run its course in the lower courts."

Pike was 18 when she and her boyfriend beat and killed Colleen Slemmer, 19, at a job training camp for young people.

The killing gained national attention after Slemmer's body was found with a pentagram, often seen as a satanic symbol, carved in her chest.

Pike's lawyers argued she should be granted clemency due to her young age and mental health issues at the time of the killing.

"She was 18 when she committed her crime, and she was deeply mentally ill," they wrote in a clemency petition to Tennessee Governor Bill Lee, who said he would not intervene.

The US Supreme Court had rejected an application for a stay of execution on Tuesday, which argued that Pike's history as a victim of sexual assault, post-traumatic stress disorder and a blood condition meant a lethal injection would cause "needless suffering."

Researchers estimate that around 84% of lost body fat is exhaled as carbon dioxide, while the remaining 16% is lost as water.
When body weight falls, some of the fat stored by the body disappears – but where does that mass actually go?

Two researchers from the University of Birmingham explain in an article published in The Conversation that fat loss is linked to a process that begins when the body needs more energy than it gets from food, so it turns to its fat reserves to meet that need.

The body uses part of these fatty acids for essential functions such as building cells, regulating hormones and protecting organs, while the surplus is converted into triglycerides that are stored inside fat cells to be used later as an energy source.

The body mainly stores fat in two ways: subcutaneous fat found just under the skin, and visceral fat that builds up deeper inside the body around the organs.

When the body enters a state of calorie deficit, hormonal and neural signals start to stimulate fat cells to break down stored fat, in a process known as lipolysis.

After triglycerides are broken down, fatty acids travel to the mitochondria inside cells, known as the body's "power stations", where they undergo beta-oxidation.

During this process, fatty acids are broken down into compounds the body uses to produce energy, and these reactions generate carbon dioxide and water as the final by-products of metabolism.

And here lies the surprise, according to the article: most of the fat the body loses does not leave through sweat, but through the lungs while we breathe.

Based on the researchers' calculations, around 84% of lost fat is turned into carbon dioxide and leaves the body when we exhale, while about 16% becomes water that is eliminated through sweat and other bodily fluids.

The researchers note that these percentages remain estimates, as it is difficult to measure all of the body's outputs precisely during fat loss.

If the energy supplied by food is not enough to cover this demand, the body starts using its fat stores.

But this does not mean that breathing rapidly causes fat loss. Exhaling is the route through which the products of fat burning leave the body, not the process that triggers fat burning itself. Maintaining a calorie deficit, through diet and physical activity, remains the key factor that pushes the body to tap into its stored energy.

The measures come as the country’s conservative leader Sanae Takaichi looks to deliver on election promises, which were spearheaded by vast immigration reforms, in her historic supermajority election victory in February.
Japan toughened requirements for foreigners wanting to obtain permanent residency on Thursday, as part of Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's push to tighten immigration controls.

Under new rules that will come into effect in stages, foreign residents wishing to stay in Japan permanently will have to prove their annual income is above average and they can carry casual conversations in Japanese.

Application fees will also rise 20-fold from Thursday. At one immigration office in central Tokyo, hundreds of people queued, hoping to wrap up procedures before the price spike.

"I understand that over-accepting foreigners might dilute Japan's unique culture. But at the same time, given its low birth rate and aging population, Japan has to accept foreigners," said US-born university student Yujie Chono, who was among those waiting.

The 22-year-old told the AFP news agency, in fluent Japanese, that he had been waiting for five hours to file his paperwork. Previous guidelines did not lay out specific thresholds for income, pension or language skills.

Even though Japan has a rapidly ageing population and is suffering labour shortages in many sectors, opposition to immigration is growing, prompting a push by Takaichi for tighter regulations on foreign nationals.

"As the number of foreign residents in Japan increases, the government is facing the fact that some citizens feel anxiety and a sense of unfairness," Takaichi said in an extended post on X on Sunday, adding that she was pushing for "orderly coexistence" with foreign nationals.

Programmes to attract international workers have fuelled public worry, anti-immigration sentiment has spread across social media and false claims have circulated widely, including that a significant portion of welfare payments in Japan goes to foreigners, and that they are making Japanese streets less safe.

Experts and many business leaders warn however that Japan must welcome more foreign workers to sustain itself.

"Japan should stop viewing foreigners merely as a temporary labour force and instead promote their long-term settlement by expanding Japanese-language education and improving their treatment," the Nikkei business daily said in a recent editorial.

Foreigners were a major issue in last year's upper house election which saw the sharp rise of the "Japanese-first" Sanseito party, which describes immigration as a "silent invasion".

The new rules for permanent residency come after the ruling Liberal Democratic Party last year pushed to tighten regulations for business manager visas, meaning entrepreneurs will be forced to leave Japan if they don't meet the new conditions.

Thursday's changes may prevent some people from applying for permanent residency but should not immediately push anyone out of the country.

Under the regulations, the application fee for permanent residency will soar to 200,000 yen (€1,116) from the current 10,000 yen (€56).

Candidates must also show "understanding" of social rules and demonstrate expected pension benefits equivalent to someone who has paid pension insurance for 30 years.

The US-led force has been operating in Iraq for 12 years to dismantle IS terrorist group networks which had threatened to take over the region and plunge it into instability.
A US-led coalition deployed in Iraq more than a decade ago to fight extremists concluded its mission on Wednesday, a move celebrated by the country's leaders and US President Donald Trump despite fears of emboldened pro-Iran armed factions.

Coalition troops deployed to Iraq and Syria in 2014 to fight the so-called Islamic State group in Iraq and Syria, which had seized large swathes of territory, committed massacres, and forced thousands of women and children into slavery.

The group was defeated in Iraq in 2017 and in Syria in 2019, but coalition forces stayed on to battle its remnants.

Washington and Baghdad agreed in 2024 to wind down the mission, and US troops withdrew gradually from bases across the country, restricting their presence to the autonomous northern Kurdistan region.

"This day is a victory for the United States, a victory for Iraq, and a decisive victory over ISIS," wrote Trump in a post on social media.

"I had a choice to stay or to leave, and felt, without question, that it was time for America to go home," he added.

The US-led coalition did not defeat IS alone, as it was heavily aided by Kurdish Peshmerga fighters, who played an important role, as did powerful Iraqi factions, including pro-Iran groups that have yet to disarm.

The Iraqi government believes it will deprive Tehran-backed factions of the excuse they have used to retain their arsenal. But many fear the pullout might boost the pro-Iran factions and draw the country deeper into the Middle East war.

Hours after the US announced the completion of the pullout, supporters of those groups gathered in a flag-lined public space to celebrate, with speeches delivered from a stage decorated with model drones.

Speaking at the event, a military spokesperson for Kataeb Hezbollah said the factions had been in talks on "regulating" their weapons but "have not yet received a response from the Iraqi government".

As such, they were "not bound by any timeline or agreement" to surrender their weapons, he added, and retained "the right to directly respond to any American or Zionist violation of Iraq's sovereignty".

Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi said "a new phase has begun, defined by Iraq's sovereignty, the strength of its institutions (and) the readiness of its forces."

Al-Zaidi, elected this year, has vowed to ensure armed factions hand over their weapons. His government set Thursday as the deadline for factions to disarm, but later said the deadline was meant to mark the start of "regulating" the armed groups.

With several Iran-backed groups refusing to disarm, senior politicians are now hoping to work out a compromise. Factions have put forward conditions that include not only the withdrawal of foreign troops but also an end to American political and economic interference.

Tehran-backed Kataeb Hezbollah has said the withdrawal of "the occupation forces ... is a start towards achieving full sovereignty”, but the group also threatened Zaidi's government.

"A government that doesn't work for the good of its people will be brought down by all possible means," it said.
