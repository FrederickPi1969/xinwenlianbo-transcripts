Russia’s internet shutdowns push state firms back to landline phones

State procurement of landline telephones tripled in the first eight months of 2026 as mobile internet outages hit nearly 80% of the country. Rostelecom’s chief urged Russians to keep one at home “like a fire extinguisher".
Russian state-owned companies spent more than three times as much on landline telephones in the first eight months this year as in the same period in 2025, as mobile internet shutdowns affecting nearly 80% of the country push ministries and businesses to seek alternatives to disrupted networks.

Procurement data cited by the Russian business daily Vedomosti show spending at 118 million roubles (€1.26 million) from January through August, with the number of purchase tenders up 12.5%.

Market participants told Vedomosti that the surge was driven by the restrictions, a shift by Russian ministries and companies to domestically produced software-based PBX systems after Western vendors withdrew from the Russian market following the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, and demand for secure communications channels.

Russia began systematically shutting down mobile internet access in May 2025, when services went offline in more than 40 regions ahead of Victory Day celebrations. What were initially episodic outages quickly became routine.

An independent monitoring project recorded 655 mobile internet outages in June 2025, rising to 2,099 in July, a figure that exceeded the worldwide total for the entire previous year, according to analysts.

The disruptions have hit banking, taxi services, food delivery and navigation, particularly in rural areas.

The authorities have justified the shutdowns as a defence against Ukrainian drones, which they say are guided via mobile internet signals.

Experts have disputed this, saying drones can fly preset routes without a network connection, and the shutdowns have reached regions far from the front line.

In March, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said the presidential administration “uses landline telephones” when asked how restrictions in central Moscow affect the work of the president’s office.

Shortly after, head of state telecoms company Rostelecom Mikhail Oseyevsky urged Russians to return to fixed lines.

Russia has approximately 12.2 landline telephones per 100 residents. In Moscow, around 2 million households use fixed lines provided by MGTS, the city’s main telephone operator.

The Ministry of Economic Development forecasts that the number of all landlines will fall to between 8.2 and 8.5 per 100 residents by 2029.

Supporters waving Ukrainian flags gathered outside a court in Hamburg on Wednesday, 14 October, in solidarity with a former Ukrainian army officer accused of involvement in the 2022 Nord Stream pipeline explosions in the Baltic Sea.
… More
The demonstration took place outside the Hanseatic Higher Regional Court as the trial opened under tight security. Protesters held signs declaring “Russia is a Terrorist” and challenged the decision to bring war crimes charges. One placard drew a distinction between damaging a gas pipeline and bombing buildings or killing civilians.

Ivan Kuritsyn, from Citizens’ Initiative Vilni De Ua, a non-profit organisation set up by Ukrainians displaced by the war, said the demonstrators had come to show their solidarity with Serhii Kuznietsov.

The 50-year-old defendant, identified by German prosecutors as Serhii K., is accused of helping organise the operation that damaged Nord Stream 1 and 2 on 26 September 2022. Prosecutors allege that he led a team of divers, a boat captain and an explosives expert.

He faces several charges, including offences linked to the explosions, property damage, disruption of public services and an alleged war crime involving civilian infrastructure. He has not been convicted.

The pipelines were designed to carry Russian gas to Germany. The trial is expected to run for several months, with hearings scheduled until February.

Kazakhstan seeks Antwerp-Bruges partnership to boost trade via Middle Corridor
Kazakh President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev speaks at the Belgium-Kazakhstan Business Council in Astana.

Kazakhstan is seeking closer cooperation with Belgium on the Middle Corridor, including a potential partnership between the Port of Antwerp-Bruges and the Caspian ports of Aktau and Kuryk, as King Philippe and Queen Mathilde make their first state visit to the country.
Kazakhstan is seeking a partnership between the Port of Antwerp-Bruges and its Caspian ports of Aktau and Kuryk as the country looks to expand trade along the Middle Corridor, President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev said.

Speaking at the Belgium-Kazakhstan Business Council in Astana, attended by King Philippe and Queen Mathilde of Belgium, Tokayev said Kazakhstan was investing heavily in railways, roads, seaports and terminals along the route.

“In this context, Belgium’s rich experience as a major European logistics center is particularly valuable to us. We anticipate a concrete partnership linking the ports of Antwerp-Bruges, with the ports of Aktau and Kuryk. This could open wider markets for our metals, petrochemicals and agricultural products,” he said.

King Philippe also expressed Belgium’s support for the Middle Corridor, calling for closer links between the route and European ports, railways and logistics hubs.

The Middle Corridor, also known as the Trans-Caspian International Transport Route, connects China with Europe through Kazakhstan, the Caspian Sea and the South Caucasus, providing an alternative to traditional east-west routes.

A day earlier, Nurlan Zhakupov, CEO of Samruk-Kazyna, Kazakhstan’s sovereign wealth fund which manages some of the country’s largest transport, energy and industrial companies, told reporters on the sidelines of the official welcoming ceremony for King Philippe and Queen Mathilde at Akorda that talks with the Port of Antwerp-Bruges were underway.

He said Kazakhstan was interested in drawing on the Belgian port’s expertise and eventually reaching concrete agreements and logistics contracts.

Speaking later to Euronews, Zhakupov said the route's viability would ultimately depend on its economic case.

“For the Middle Corridor, it has to make economic sense and deliver an economic benefit. That is why negotiations are ongoing — it is a large and complex process,” he said. “I think concrete results are not far off.”

The Port of Antwerp-Bruges was not represented at the business council, but the discussion focused on the practical challenges facing the Middle Corridor.

Speaking to Euronews after a session on new trade routes between Europe and Central Asia, Ahlers Logistics CEO Dave van den Bos said that while investment in physical infrastructure was already under way, one of the biggest challenges was coordinating handovers between the numerous companies and authorities involved along the route.

As many as 15 different companies or authorities can be involved in a single shipment from Europe to Kazakhstan, van den Bos said.

“Digitisation is the most important,” he said. “Even if the capacity is there, if you’re standing at the border with your document and waiting for someone to give a green light or a stamp, that’s still going to delay quite a bit. Infrastructure is easier to fix than the whole digitisation of it.”

Van den Bos said transit times from Europe to Kazakhstan had fallen from around 80 days to roughly 40–60 days, although port congestion and weather on the Caspian Sea still cause delays.

According to Kazakhstan Temir Zholy, the country’s national railway company, the Middle Corridor transported around 44,000 TEUs in the first eight months of 2026, up 58% from the same period last year.

Kazakhstan is seeking to increase overall freight volumes along the route to 10 million tonnes, a target Tokayev previously announced.

Belgium is among Kazakhstan’s ten largest investors, with more than $15 billion invested in the country over the past two decades, according to Tokayev.

The forum's sidelines saw 17 agreements covering logistics, agriculture and food processing, artificial intelligence, energy, healthcare and manufacturing.

Tokayev also highlighted critical raw materials, stating Kazakhstan already produced 21 of the 34 raw materials considered critical for European industry and supplies Belgium with titanium and molybdenum products.

“By combining our resource potential with Belgian investment, technology and expertise, we can build a strategic partnership in this critically important area,” Tokayev said.

King Philippe and Queen Mathilde's visit is the first state visit by a Belgian monarch to Kazakhstan and the wider Central Asian region. President Tokayev welcomed the royal couple with an official ceremony at the Akorda presidential palace in Astana, followed by talks between the two sides.

The visit comes ahead of the 35th anniversary of diplomatic relations between Kazakhstan and Belgium next year.

“Your visit gives new impetus to preparations for this important anniversary and opens up opportunities to fill our cooperation with concrete results,” Tokayev said, describing Belgium as a long-standing and reliable partner for Kazakhstan in Europe.

Moscow's FSB security service said a Japanese national was arrested on the Far East island of Sakhalin, near a disputed cluster of islands, but did not name the individual.
Tokyo summoned the Russian ambassador on Wednesday to lodge a protest over Moscow's arrest of a Japanese journalist on suspicion of espionage, with the foreign ministry urging "fair and impartial treatment."

Russia has arrested several citizens of what it calls "unfriendly" countries in recent years, accusing them of working for Western intelligence services.

Russia's foreign ministry identified the reporter as Yuki Imai from the Hokkaido Shimbun newspaper on Tuesday, with a spokesperson saying he was accused of "conducting espionage activities on Russian territory."

Moscow's FSB security service said a Japanese national was arrested on the Far East island of Sakhalin, near a disputed cluster of islands, but did not name the individual.

It said the person had worked for Japanese intelligence to collect information about Russia's defences.

On Wednesday, the Japanese foreign ministry said European Affairs Bureau official Kimitake Nakamura summoned the Russian ambassador, Nikolay Nozdrev.

Nakamura "lodged a strong protest with the Russian side over the detention of a Japanese national and the issuance of unilateral statements regarding the case", the ministry said in a statement.

Kremlin spokesperson Dmitri Peskov meanwhile said in a briefing that the security service "is doing its job."

Chief Cabinet Secretary Minoru Kihara said Japan has not been able to contact the individual and did not know about the arrest until the official announcement from Moscow.

Japanese diplomats in Russia have requested "the prompt provision of information" and "humanitarian consideration and fair and impartial treatment," Foreign Minister Toshimitsu Motegi said.

The Hokkaido Shimbun said in a statement that it was aware of the Russian announcement but was "still confirming the details."

"Ensuring the safety of the journalist is our top priority and we are responding accordingly," the newspaper said.

The FSB said it had "opened a criminal case against the Japanese national" and that he had been placed in pre-trial detention, TASS and RIA Novosti news agencies reported, citing the security service.

"Attempts by Tokyo to use its correspondents in Russia...to gather information aimed at undermining the security of our state is regrettable," Russian foreign ministry spokeswoman Maria Zakharova said.

Media rights group Reporters Without Borders (RSF) called for the journalist's "rights to be respected and for his release," saying in a statement on X that the gathering of information is part of journalism.

The FSB published a video showing masked officers storming a hotel room, pushing a man to the floor and handcuffing him.

Ali-Sina Mostafavi, 38, a mountaineer and musician from Kerman, was executed on charges of “enmity against God” after being accused of firing on civilians and soldiers during the opening months of the war. He was also charged with participating in the January protests and sending footage
Iran executed a political prisoner in Kerman on Wednesday on charges of carrying out armed attacks during what the Iranian judiciary described as joint American-Israeli military operations on Iranian soil.

The Mizan news agency, the official outlet of Iran's judiciary, identified Ali-Sina Mostafavi, 38, as one of "the armed agents of the American-Zionist enemy" and accused him of opening fire on civilians in Azadi Square in Kerman on 24 March, wounding one person and a young girl.

According to Mizan, he also fired on conscript soldiers and passing vehicles on 21 April, injuring further civilians.

The judiciary additionally accused him of participating in protests in December 2025 and January 2026 — which the Iranian authorities call the "Dey protests" and describe as a coup attempt — and of sending footage to Persian-language media outlets based outside Iran, which Tehran brands as hostile.

Among the items seized from Mostafavi, said to be from the city of Bam, were a Brno rifle with telescopic sight, a shotgun, a second unlicensed shotgun, 246 rounds for the Brno and 30 Molotov cocktails, the judiciary said.

Mostafavi, who played the tar and had made a solo ascent of Island Peak in the Himalayas and attempted Ama Dablam, reaching 6,600 metres, was held in Shahab prison in Kerman. He was moved to solitary confinement in his final days and his family were summoned for a last visit.

Reza Pahlavi, the son of Iran's last shah, described Mostafavi on X as "a patriotic young man" condemned after forced confessions extracted under torture in what he called show trials, and urged others to speak out for political prisoners facing execution.

The charge Mostafavi faced, moharebeh, or "enmity against God," in Iranian law represents taking up arms against people's lives, property or honour in a way that creates insecurity.

Two days earlier, the Mizan news agency confirmed the execution of Omar Dorastaran, 25, identified by the judiciary as a member of the Sunni militant group Jaish al-Adl.

He was accused of helping group members enter Iran, arranging accommodation and transport, and providing support for attacks on IRGC forces in Sistan and Baluchistan province, including the attack on the Paroud base and March 2024 assaults on military installations in Chabahar.

The 2024 attack on the Rask police post was among the deadliest carried out by armed groups in south-eastern Iran. Eleven security forces members were killed.

Dorastaran, single and from the village of Cheraghan in Rask county, was studying at a religious school in the city of Pishin at the time of his arrest, according to human rights organisations.

Halvash, a local media outlet in Sistan and Baluchestan, reported that he was arrested without a warrant and held for an extended period without access to visits or phone calls, and that security agents sought to extract forced confessions through torture.

Sistan and Baluchistan, one of Iran's poorest provinces, has long been the scene of armed clashes between security forces and Sunni militant and Baloch armed organisations. Fighting in the province has intensified in recent weeks.

Amnesty International on Tuesday condemned the execution of five men within a single week in Iran in cases linked to the January protests, and called for coordinated international pressure on Tehran to halt further executions.

The organisation said the Islamic Republic uses the death penalty systematically to intimidate opponents and raise the cost of protest. Iran executed at least 2,159 people in 2025, according to Amnesty.

Albania's capital, Tirana, saw violent clashes between student protesters and police on Tuesday, 13 October, as demonstrations continued overnight into Wednesday.
… More
Hundreds gathered outside the Education Ministry to demand better university conditions, increased financial support and improved job prospects. The protests escalated as demonstrators threw stones, smashed shop windows and damaged businesses, while police responded with tear gas and water cannon.

According to a police update on Wednesday, 16 people were arrested and 49 others faced criminal proceedings. Six police officers and at least one journalist were injured.

The student-led demonstrations have joined a wider anti-government movement known as the Flamingo Revolution, which is demanding Prime Minister Edi Rama's resignation over allegations of corruption. Protesters also oppose a luxury coastal development linked to Jared Kushner, US President Donald Trump's son-in-law, citing concerns about its environmental impact. The movement takes its name from the pink flamingos that campaigners say could be threatened by the project. The unrest has added to mounting political pressure on Rama's government.

Shakur was a key figure in a hip-hop rivalry pitting the US East Coast against the West Coast. He was just 25 when he was gunned down in Las Vegas.
Duane "Keffe D" Davis, the former Los Angeles gang leader who boasted for years of his involvement in the killing of rapper Tupac Shakur, only to deny it when he came to trial, was sentenced to a minimum 40 years in prison on Tuesday.

The sentence, which will likely see him die in custody, comes three decades after the gunning down of one of the most influential hip hop artists of the late 20th century and after years of criticism over investigations that went nowhere.

Davis, 63, formerly ran a branch of the feared Crips, one of a patchwork of rival gangs that controlled parts of Los Angeles in the 1990s.

He spent years evading justice and seemingly believed he was beyond the reach of the law when he wrote in his memoir that he had ordered the killing of Shakur.

"For three years, I've heard you say some variation of the same thing to law enforcement...in your book and it's something like, 'Yeah, I was involved in this, but I'm different. I've moved on. I got out of Compton. I'm a grandfather now,'" Judge Carli Kierny told Davis as she handed down the sentence in a Las Vegas courtroom.

"You don't get to take a man from his family. You don't get to take his very life, his whole future from him, and just walk away from it. Especially when that man is Tupac Shakur," the judge said.

Shakur was a key figure in a hip-hop rivalry pitting the US East Coast against the West Coast. He was just 25 when he was gunned down in Las Vegas.

A trial heard how Davis ordered the 7 September 1996 killing of the hip-hop artist after his own nephew had been beaten by members of Shakur's entourage at a Las Vegas casino.

The "California Love" artist was shot about two hours after the casino confrontation by a gunman in a white Cadillac that pulled up at a red light alongside the BMW in which the rapper was riding.

Davis handed the gun to someone sitting in the back seat of the Cadillac and ordered that person to shoot Shakur, who died six days later.

The name of that person has not been definitively revealed and Davis is the only person who was in the Cadillac that evening who is still alive.

The killing of Shakur, also known as 2Pac, was one of America's most famous cold cases, prompting endless debate among music fans over who did it and why.

Known for hits including "All Eyez on Me," Shakur, the son of a Black Panther activist, was raised in Harlem and Baltimore, but quickly became the epitome of California hip hop when he signed with Los Angeles-based Death Row Records.

His sister Sekyiwa Shakur paid emotional tribute on Tuesday to her brother, a man whom she said "was born an icon."

She said their mother "consciously raised him to be a human rights activist, someone to stand up for those weaker and smaller than he was, and that's who he was to me and all the family. He was the person that you go to for protection."

Davis offered no words of contrition ahead of the sentence being handed down, vowing only to appeal the guilty finding.

US President Donald Trump posted disturbing footage on his Truth Social account on Tuesday showing a man being fatally mauled by a tiger in Britain, sparking backlash on social media.

He later posted another clip featuring apparently AI-generated footage of people being attacked by big cats, accompanied by synthesiser music.

Africa has recorded the world's fastest improvements in disaster resilience, a new major global study has revealed.

Research from Lloyd's Register Foundation shows that African regions have made the biggest gains in people's ability to withstand and recover from disasters and other major disruptions since 2023.

North Africa, for instance, recorded a five-point increase in resilience, followed by Southern Africa with four points. Eastern Africa and Central and Western Africa each improved by three points.

Expanding internet access, better household preparedness, and improved financial resilience in countries including Kenya, Egypt, Uganda, Algeria, and Nigeria propelled the gains.

The findings, however, expose a significant trial. In Central and West Africa, few disaster-affected adults received any warning, raising questions about whether resilience improvements are translating into better protection for vulnerable communities.

This is the outcome of the fourth edition of the World Risk Poll, which surveyed more than 143,000 people across 140 countries and territories in the year 2025. Conducted by Gallup, the study examines experiences of safety risks, including in countries where official data is limited.

Its Resilience Index measures people's ability to absorb and recover from major shocks on a scale of 0 to 100. The assessment covers four areas: individual resilience, household preparedness, community support and wider societal conditions.

Increased internet access can help people obtain information about potential dangers, while household preparedness can improve their ability to respond when emergencies occur.

The researchers identified Algeria, Nigeria, Kenya, Egypt, and Uganda as countries contributing to regional improvements, although the factors behind the gains varied. The findings, however, do not mean Africa has become the world's most resilient continent. Rather, its regions have recorded the fastest improvements over the period examined.

Despite the progress, the study discloses massive inequalities in access to potentially life-saving information. In Central and Western Africa, for instance, 47% of adults affected by disasters said they had received a warning.

Globally, people in low-income countries were almost twice as likely to have experienced a disaster over the previous five years as those in high-income countries.

Nearly one in three adults in poorer countries reported being affected, compared with 16% in wealthier nations.

Yet only 64% of disaster-affected adults in low-income countries received warnings, compared with 84% in high-income countries. Even when warnings were issued, many people could not respond.

Fewer than half of warned adults in low- and lower-middle-income countries took action before disaster struck, compared with 71% in high-income countries.

Nancy Hey, director of evidence and insight at Lloyd's Register Foundation's Global Safety Evidence Centre, said the results highlighted the importance of preparedness.

The research identifies mobile technology as a major opportunity to close gaps in disaster warning systems.

Worldwide, an estimated 1.1 billion adults experienced a disaster in the previous five years, but almost 220 million received no warning beforehand.

The study also found that people were more likely to act when warnings reached them through several channels.

Only 37% of those receiving alerts through one channel took action, compared with 51% reached through two and 64% through three. These channels include text messages, television and social media.

In Malawi, volunteers mobilised communities to clear storm drains ahead of extreme rainfall in January, according to the International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies.

This initiative illustrates how communities can take practical steps before severe weather arrives.

Caroline Zastiral, acting global lead for disaster risk reduction and resilience, said effective warnings depended on people's ability to respond.

"Warnings lead to action when people have the trust, knowledge, time and resources to protect themselves, their loved ones and their assets," she said.

Turkish police raided the headquarters of the pro-opposition Halk TV news channel, halting its live broadcasts and arresting 11 people, the channel and prosecutors said early on Wednesday.

"Police raid at Halk TV headquarters! A trustee (manager) has been appointed, broadcasts have been halted," the channel wrote in a post on X shortly before 7:00 am (0600 CET).

It was the latest crackdown on the pro-opposition media and came just two weeks after authorities shut down the website and social media accounts of T24, a leading digital news website, citing its "LGBT propaganda."

In a statement to Turkish media, prosecutors said they had issued a warrant for 17 people, 11 of whom had been detained as part of an investigation into Halk's chairman Cafer Mahiroğlu on suspicion of "bribery", "rigging tenders," and "money laundering."

It said companies co-owned or managed by Mahiroğlu had been seized and that the authorities put a government-appointed trustee in charge of them.

Of the remaining six people named in the warrant, four were abroad, one was in a correctional facility and the last was still being sought, it said.

In several posts as dawn was breaking, Halk journalists said they were unable to get into the building.

"The police have sealed off the entrance to our building. That’s the situation...There’s nothing left to say," wrote Onur Dalar on X, his words echoed by a colleague.

"At Halk TV, the management is now under a trustee. Broadcasting systems have been seized. Our channel is literally under blockade. I still can’t get into the institution where I’ve worked for 5 years, none of us can. The police haven’t left the building," wrote Gamze Altunay.

The move came just hours before the start of the defence's arguments in the sprawling corruption trial of jailed Istanbul mayor Ekrem İmamoğlu and some 400 other defendants.

"In recent years, the lawlessness used by the political authorities to overcome every problem has once again struck freedom of the press today...We do not accept this tyranny!" wrote TGS on X.

And opposition leader Özgür Özel also lashed out against a TV channel "watched by millions" describing the government's action as "weak and cowardly."

"Storming a TV channel with a police raid at dawn and appointing a trustee is something only dictators do. Unfortunately, they have brought our country to this state," he wrote.

He said it was no coincidence the raids came just hours before the start of the defence arguments in İmamoğlu's trial.

"They did this on the very day the defence was to begin in Turkey’s biggest political trial. Those who appoint trustees to municipalities and usurp the will of the people have now appointed a trustee to Halk TV, watched by millions. Because they are WEAK and COWARDLY!"

Last year, Halk TV, which has faced repeated sanctions from Turkey's broadcasting regulator, was threatened with possible closure over its critical tone towards the government of President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan.

An Aramco building in Dhahran caught fire, and four people were wounded by intercepted missile debris in Riyadh, as Houthi attacks continued after a strike on the capital’s airport killed 13 people in the worst attack on Saudi soil since 2015.
Saudi Arabia said on Wednesday that an attack on an Aramco building in the kingdom's east sparked a "limited fire," as US President Donald Trump slammed Yemen's Houthis for "behaving very badly."

A Saudi energy ministry official said an administrative building belonging to oil giant Aramco in Dhahran was struck on Tuesday evening, resulting in a "limited fire" but "no casualties or fatalities".

Saudi's civil defence agency separately said on Wednesday that debris from two intercepted Houthi missiles wounded four people in the capital Riyadh, and that 15 homes were damaged.

Earlier on Tuesday, the coalition struck oil sites across western Yemen, in response to the deadliest attack on the kingdom's soil in years.

The strikes in Yemen's Houthi-held capital Sana'a and the western provinces of Dhamar and Saada targeted facilities used to "supply the war effort" by the Houthis to "threaten the kingdom, Yemen, the southern Red Sea and waterways", coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki wrote on X.

A powerful explosion then rang out in the Saudi capital, with a phone alert sent to residents saying "the city of Riyadh is facing a hostile aerial threat".

While walking down a major avenue in Riyadh, a witness said he "saw a projectile cutting across the sky" above a mall, leaving "a yellowish trail of light followed by a trail of smoke. Then, we heard an explosion".

"People were calm, no one was speaking. We were scared and all looking up at the sky," he added, requesting anonymity.

After Houthi strikes on Saudi airports and energy sites in recent days, the Saudi-led coalition issued its first evacuation order in the Yemeni capital since the war flared in July, before attacking the Yemen Petroleum Company facility on Sana'a's outskirts.

A breaking news alert on the Houthi-affiliated Al-Masirah TV channel said "Saudi aggression" targeted the oil firm.

The Houthis had earlier issued a new threat to target Riyadh's international airport, a refinery, and an Aramco facility in Dammam if the strike went ahead, calling for citizens to avoid them.

Many countries have issued travel warnings to their citizens in Saudi Arabia, most recently France, which told its citizens to avoid Riyadh, Jeddah and Dammam airports.

A strike on Riyadh's international airport on Saturday left 13 people dead and operations suspended in the worst attack since 2015.

The airport attack prompted US President Donald Trump to say he was considering joining Saudi strikes against the Houthis.

He wrote on his Truth Social platform on Tuesday that "the Houthis are behaving very badly", accusing them of acting "probably at the behest of Iran".

"I thought they learned their lesson a year ago when we bombed the hell out of them," Trump added, laying the blame for their actions on Tehran.

"They’ve been silent for a long time, but now they’ve emerged again, probably at the behest of Iran, which is a failing nation, with no navy, no airforce, the world’s worst economy, and 301% Inflation, in a very nasty way, hitting Saudi Arabia, and purposefully trying to drive up Oil Prices, probably prior to the US elections — not nice."

The Houthis warned the US against intervention, with political bureau member Mohammed Al Bukhaiti stating they would target American interests in the region.

Bukhaiti said the purpose of the campaign against Saudi targets was to send a message to the crown prince "that he can no longer wage aggression against Yemen from a safe position".

The Saudi cabinet led by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman vowed earlier Tuesday that it "will not allow its security or freedom of navigation in the Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb Strait to be threatened".

The Saudi-led coalition has supported Yemen's internationally recognised government militarily against the Houthis since 2015 in a long-running civil war that has created a dire humanitarian crisis.

Suspected cholera cases tripled there in three weeks, the World Health Organization said Tuesday, warning that the growing disease burden was straining hospitals already treating war casualties.

Russian missile and drone strikes on Kyiv killed at least three people, injured 22 and damaged homes and power plants, leaving over 600,000 without electricity.

Rescue workers combed through the wreckage of damaged buildings in Kyiv on Wednesday after a wave of Russian missiles and drones struck the Ukrainian capital overnight, leaving at least three people dead and 22 injured.

In the Desnianskyi district, an attack on a nine-storey residential block killed two people and injured 21, including a child. Emergency crews rescued 16 residents, while firefighters tackled a blaze spanning 400 square metres. Sixteen of those injured were hospitalised, four in a serious condition.

Another person was killed and one injured in the Holosiivskyi district, where a non-residential building was hit. Psychologists from the emergency services assisted 110 people at the affected sites.

The strikes also targeted Kyiv’s energy infrastructure, with President Volodymyr Zelensky confirming that a ballistic missile hit the CHP-4 heat and power plant. He said Ukraine had received no warning from its partners before detecting the incoming missiles.

The broader overnight attacks on energy facilities left more than 600,000 people without power across Ukraine.
