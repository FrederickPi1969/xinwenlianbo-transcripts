The revised indictment includes several mentions of the involvement of Venezuela’s feared DGCIM military intelligence agency
US authorities charged former Venezuelan President Nicolas Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, on Thursday with conspiring to torture Venezuelan citizens to remain in power.

“He authorized the use of torture against specific individuals and oversaw a system designed to use torture, arbitrary detention and violence as a means of repression against the political opposition,” says the revised indictment.

The indictment alleges that thousands of Venezuelans were “harassed, threatened, arrested, prosecuted, assaulted, tortured, or killed” by people acting on behalf of Maduro’s government.

The alleged abuse included electric shocks, beatings and asphyxiation. Maduro is also accused of approving torture methods and personally threatening to kill a prisoner’s child “as a form of psychological torture.”

The indictment alleges that the torture victims included three US citizens, who were not named in the court documents.

The new indictment adds to the legal pressure on Maduro, who already faces charges of conspiring to smuggle cocaine into the US. He now also faces allegations of human rights abuses committed during his presidency.

Maduro and Flores, have been detained in New York since January, after US forces captured them during an overnight raid on their Caracas home.

Both have pleaded not guilty to drug trafficking charges and could face life imprisonment if convicted.

Maduro has also been under investigation by the International Criminal Court for alleged crimes against humanity linked to his crackdown on political opponents. He has consistently denied wrongdoing.

Updated: 08/10/2026 - 17:25 GMT+2
Heavy autumn snowfall has paralysed roads in Russia’s Magadan region, leaving trucks struggling through snow and disrupting intercity bus services.
… More
Heavy snowfall blanketed Russia’s Far Eastern Magadan region, making roads treacherous and bringing traffic to a near standstill. Trucks struggled to climb snow-covered roads, while intercity buses were delayed or suspended.

Snowploughs were deployed to clear the roads as authorities urged drivers to avoid long-distance travel.

Residents reported waiting at bus stations for services that could not operate because of the conditions. More heavy, wet snow and strong winds were forecast.

Around 280,000 people are registered as missing in Ukraine alone, with 10,000 new cases added each month, part of a 70% global rise since 2019. Forensic experts meeting in Baku said the obstacle was not forensic capacity but governmental reluctance.
Hundreds of thousands of people worldwide remain missing in wars, as experts who met in Baku warned that political will, not technology, is the obstacle to identifying them.

The figures have continued to grow, rising 70% between 2019 and 2024, according to the ICRC.

In Ukraine alone, around 280,000 people are registered as missing as of September 2026, with an estimated 10,000 new cases added every month, according to the same data.

Government officials, forensic experts, and diplomats met at the fourth International Conference on Missing Persons in Baku, under the Baku Dialogue on Missing Persons, to discuss how countries can cooperate to identify the dead and locate those who disappeared in both historical and ongoing conflicts.

Argentinian forensic anthropologist Luis Bernardo Fondebrider, who has worked across 66 countries over 42 years, said the pattern he encounters is consistent.

"In every country, I see the same pattern. The families want justice, want reparation, want memory, and want to know what happened," he said, attributing failure to act to a "lack of political will from the states."

He was unambiguous about governmental pledges. "The speeches about the commitment of the states" were "just words," he said.

"Today, there are almost no governments with the willingness to really investigate how it's happened with the missing and to ask for justice for the perpetrators."

Lord Brady, a member of the UK House of Lords who last visited host country Azerbaijan 16 years ago during the Karabakh conflict, said much had changed since then.

"We've had so much progress in the last few years," he said, adding that technological advances had opened up possibilities that "simply couldn't have been done in the past."

Christian Rivier, head of the ICRC's Central Tracing Agency, told Euronews that technology was only part of the answer.

"We don't need to be blindsided by the fact that technology helps us," he said. Processing the information it generates remained the central challenge, he added.

According to Rivier, it required "the human capacity to understand the information, to make sense of it, to ensure that there is an investigative medical-legal process that follows processes that are efficient and that avoid misidentification."

As of 31 August, 4,010 Azerbaijani citizens remain registered as missing linked to the first and second Karabakh conflicts.

Azerbaijan's State Commission says it has collected DNA samples from more than 11,000 donors to help identify more than 3,700 missing persons.

Rizvan Nabiyev, a member of the State Commission, said cross-border information-sharing was essential for "confidence-building measures" and "trust between societies".

Two commercial vessels were hit within 24 hours — one off Qatar, the first attack in that area in six months, and one off Fujairah in the Gulf of Oman — as IRGC-affiliated outlets suggested Tehran had extended operations beyond the crucial strait.
Iran's armed forces struck two commercial tankers outside the Strait of Hormuz within 24 hours this week, in what appeared to be a deliberate expansion of attacks beyond the strait, as Iranian officials issued a fresh wave of warnings against the United States.

The UK Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO) agency said several projectiles hit the oil and chemical tanker Acers, flagged in Antigua and Barbuda, 51 nautical miles north of Madinat ash Shamal in Qatar on Wednesday. Casualties were reported.

Maritime intelligence firm Vanguard identified the vessel and said it was heading toward the Strait of Hormuz.

In a separate incident on Thursday, Vanguard reported a fire on a tanker some 40 kilometres off Fujairah in the Gulf of Oman, which it identified as likely the Marshall Islands-flagged Dhalgout.

The UKMTO had not recorded the incident, and the cause of the fire was not immediately clear. NASA's FIRMS fire-detection system confirmed the blaze, and US military aircraft — including two KC-46 tankers, a KC-135, a P-8A maritime patrol aircraft and several helicopters — were over the area at the time.

Iran and media affiliated with the IRGC did not officially claim responsibility for either attack. IRGC-affiliated outlets implicitly suggested that Tehran had "extended the scope of its operations against commercial vessels that violate Iranian laws and regulations in the Strait of Hormuz to waters beyond the strait."

Both incidents fall outside the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly one-fifth of global oil and gas shipments normally pass. The strait has been effectively closed since the war began in late February, after Iran and the US imposed concurrent naval blockades.

"The distance between you and us is determined by our missiles," Acting Defence Minister Majid Ebn ol-Reza posted on X on Thursday.

"Yesterday the Gulf of Oman, today the Indian Ocean and tomorrow the Bay of Pigs. If you make a mistake, you will see a new set of dividing lines.," ol-Reza added in an apparent reference to a failed, US-backed military landing on the southwestern coast of Cuba from April 1961 aimed at overthrowing the communist government of Fidel Castro.

Yahya Rahim Safavi, a senior military adviser to Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei, had earlier warned that if the US or Israel attacked Iran again, Tehran could extend the war "as far as the Indian Ocean or other places," adding that Iranian forces had prepared contingency plans for worst-case scenarios.

Ebn ol-Reza was appointed acting minister after Aziz Nasirzadeh, Iran's then defence minister, was killed on 28 February — the first day of the war — alongside several other military commanders. Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian has nominated Mehrdad Akhlaqi as a permanent replacement, and parliament is due to hold a confidence vote on 18 October.

Iran's army spokesman said separately that its forces were ready to carry out pre-emptive operations, if necessary, to prevent what he described as "any aggression or attack by the enemy."

At a midterm election campaign rally in Nebraska, US President Donald Trump defended the decision to go to war with Iran. "This is a small price to pay for keeping the world and our country safe. They can destroy a city. Let them destroy it. Let them destroy Los Angeles, let them destroy San Diego," he said.

At a separate rally in Texas on Wednesday night, Trump walked back those remarks after criticism. "We protect our own cities. We will not allow such a thing to happen," he said.

Drone strikes hit Eritrean vehicles around the northern city of Adigrat overnight, with witnesses reporting destroyed lorries and retreating troops, days after Ethiopia expelled Eritrean diplomats and Eritrea severed ties.
Ethiopian drone strikes have targeted Eritrean forces who had reached a northern Tigrayan city, a conflict monitor and witnesses said on Thursday, amid fears that the renewed fighting in the African country might turn into a wider regional war.

The strikes came as the Tigray People's Liberation Front-led seven-group rebel coalition continued to push against federal forces across northern Ethiopia, with fighting reported in Afar and Amhara regions since the coalition announced its offensive last week.

Eritrean troops crossed the border this week and were positioned around the Ethiopian city of Adigrat in northern Tigray, according to multiple sources.

Conflict monitor ACLED said "Eritrean troops and ammunition" in the Adigrat area were hit by "multiple drone strikes" overnight between Wednesday and Thursday.

"There were many, many drone strikes yesterday. Some Eritrean lorries have been destroyed. They are no longer in the streets," one witness, who spoke on condition of anonymity, told AFP.

Another witness in Adigrat, who also asked for anonymity, said they had heard a "massive explosion during the night".

Relations between the two neighbouring countries, which fought a border war from 1998 to 2000, have deteriorated again in recent months.

Ethiopia accuses Eritrea of backing rebels in its northern Tigray state, and the two countries severed diplomatic ties last week.

The government in Asmara in turn accuses Ethiopia of supporting its own opponents, and of looking for a pretext to invade its territory and seize direct access to the Red Sea.

Eritrean Information Minister Yemane Meskel said on X that Ethiopia was "openly working to bring about regime change in Eritrea by providing shelter and support to certain mercenaries who claim to be opposition".

Eritrea gained independence from Ethiopia in 1993, cutting off the latter from access to the Red Sea.

After years of war and tension, relations improved markedly after Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed rose to power in 2018, an effort he was rewarded for with a Nobel Peace Prize.

Eritrea even fought alongside Ethiopia during the latter's 2020-2022 civil war against the TPLF.

But the relationship deteriorated soon after, with Eritrea voicing anger over the terms of the peace agreement that ended that war and, later on, Ethiopia's claims that it has a right to sea access.

Abiy's rhetoric escalated to claim that allowing Eritrea's independence was a "mistake," as Ethiopia accused Eritrean troops of crimes against civilians during the 2020-2022 Tigray war.

This and other accusations prompted Eritrea to blast Ethiopia for allegedly "floating false flags to justify the war that it has been itching to unleash for two long years".

Seoul said Kyiv's public disclosure of the repatriation of two North Korean prisoners endangered their families in North Korea, where relatives of those who fall into enemy hands face punishment. Ukraine has rejected South Korea's accusations.
South Korea recalled its ambassador to Ukraine on Wednesday over Kyiv's public disclosure of the repatriation of two North Korean prisoners of war, in a continued public dispute between the two countries.

Seoul's foreign ministry announced it was recalling ambassador Park Ki-chan, citing the unauthorised disclosure of information about the handover.

The ministry said it hoped to resolve outstanding issues through continued consultations and that the South Korean embassy in Kyiv would remain open.

The dispute began on 23 September, when Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy stated at the UN General Assembly that North Korean soldiers captured fighting on Russia's side had been repatriated.

Seoul argues the disclosure endangered the soldiers' families in North Korea, where the regime is known to punish the relatives of defectors and those who fall into enemy hands.

Ukraine rejected the accusations. Zelenskyy's adviser Dmytro Lytvyn said at the end of September that no confidentiality agreement existed.

"There was no such agreement, that is not true. And it would be wrong to conceal what happens to particular prisoners," he wrote on social media.

Ukrainian Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha described it as a diplomatic misunderstanding and expressed hope for a swift resolution.

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung had previously warned of "additional measures" if Kyiv did not acknowledge a breach of a confidentiality agreement and issue a public apology.

On Tuesday, South Korean Foreign Minister Cho Hyun said Seoul had received an apology from Ukraine, but it fell short of what his country expected.

Reports indicate that South Korean ports shipped more than 176,000 tonnes of fuel oil, mostly diesel, to Russia in July and August, based on port documents and vessel-tracking data published by The Guardian.

Seven tankers, three of which are subject to UK and EU sanctions, made 14 voyages over the two-month period, according to reports.

Vladyslav Vlasjuk, Ukraine's presidential envoy on sanctions policy, said the timing was no coincidence.

"These deliveries began precisely when Ukrainian strikes on Russian oil refineries reduced processing volumes and caused a fuel shortage in the Russian Federation. In other words, imports from South Korea effectively helped Russia offset the consequences of losing its own refining capacity," he wrote on social media.

Kyiv's reaction came despite Seoul joining the coalition of countries that imposed enhanced export controls on Russia after its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in early 2022.

In 2024, South Korea expanded its list of controlled goods to more than 1,400 items and tightened restrictions on so-called Common High Priority Items, components Russia procures through third countries for use in missiles, drones and other weapons.

Updated: 08/10/2026 - 11:47 GMT+2
Prolonged drought in central Sri Lanka is leaving wells dry, hurting farmers’ livelihoods and pushing wildlife to crowd shrinking water holes.
… More
Shrinking water holes, dry wells and parched fields show the impact of a prolonged drought in central Sri Lanka.

In Medirigiriya, villagers walk with plastic cans and clay pots to collect supplies, while tankers deliver water to communities where wells have run dry.

The dry conditions are also taking a toll on wildlife in Yala National Park. Deer, monkeys and buffaloes gather around what remains of the watering holes, while authorities use tankers to keep them supplied.

Little rain during the current monsoon season, combined with high temperatures, has added to the strain.

Climate experts say El Niño and warmer temperatures linked partly to climate change may be contributing to the conditions.

Texas has executed seven people so far this year, up from five in both 2025 and 2024. A further two executions are scheduled to take place in the state in November.
Texas has carried out the first execution in the US since Tennessee death row inmate Christa Pike survived a failed lethal injection last week.

Jamaal Howard, 46, received a fatal dose of pentobarbital at the state prison in Huntsville and was pronounced dead at 8:03 pm. Howard had been convicted of shooting and killing 42-year-old clerk Vickie Swartout in a convenience store robbery in Silsbee, Texas, in 2000.

Howard is said to have gasped and taken a few breaths as the drug took effect, before making snoring sounds and coughing. All movement stopped within around 30 seconds, and he was pronounced dead 19 minutes later.

Before the execution took place, Howard addressed some members of Swartout's family, apologising and asking for forgiveness.

"I wish that you accept my apology and I wish for your forgiveness," he said. "I'm sorry for what happened. I wish you can learn to forgive me one of these days."

He added that he was "in a good state of mind to find peace and the Lord, so I thank y’all."

Howard's attorneys had unsuccessfully attempted to halt the execution, filing a motion before the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals questioning the quality of Texas' execution drugs. They also argued to the Supreme Court that the execution should not go ahead because Howard was intellectually disabled.

The Texas court denied the request relating to the drugs and a second filing without comment.

The Supreme Court has barred the execution of intellectually disabled people, but it has given states some discretion in determining such disabilities. The state told the court that Howard's attorneys did not provide evidence that would show he met the requirements to claim intellectual disability.

The high court also declined to intervene without comment late on Wednesday, clearing the way for the execution to go ahead.

The Texas Coalition to Abolish the Death Penalty, an advocacy organisation campaigning to end the death penalty in Texas, had called on people to urge Texas Governor Greg Abbott to insist on a reprieve for Howard, so that his lawyers could "obtain critical records from the Texas Department of Criminal Justice about when the drug was bought, how it was stored, and what testing was done and when."

Including Howard, Texas has executed seven people so far this year, up from five in both 2025 and 2024. A further two executions are scheduled to take place in November - death row inmates John Rubio and Reinaldo Dennes.

Shiranthi Rajapaksa has been jailed pending investigation into alleged corruption. Authorities accuse her of misusing donation funds during her time as first lady of the island nation.
The wife of Sri Lanka's powerful former President Mahinda Rajapaksa was detained on Wednesday on allegations of misusing donations received for her charity when she was first lady.

Police questioned Shiranthi Rajapaksa at her home and later took her before a court, which ordered her detention for a week pending investigations. Police then escorted Rajapaksa, who had one eye bandaged from recent surgery, to a vehicle to be taken to a jail.

Rajapaksa was accused of misusing funds that her charity received as a donation from a state bank earmarked for the purchase of a scanner for a government hospital.

The then-head of the bank also was arrested for allegedly circumventing procedures in releasing the money to the charity. The court later released him on bail.

The former first couple's son, lawmaker Namal Rajapaksa, has also been detained on charges of bribery.

Ex-President Rajapaksa and his family were adored by the country's majority ethnic Sinhala Buddhists after he and his brother Gotabhaya Rajapaksa led a military campaign to defeat the now-defunct Tamil Tiger rebels and end a 26-year civil war in the island nation in 2009.

His popularity declined after his re-election in 2010 however, over rampant allegations of bribery, corruption and the stifling of freedoms, leading to his defeat while seeking a third term in 2015.

His family had wielded unbridled power — with his son, brothers, nephews and nieces holding top government positions.

The family made a comeback in 2019 when security emerged as a key concern following Islamic State-inspired suicide bomb attacks that killed 267 people in churches and hotels on Easter Sunday.

Gotabhaya Rajapaksa was elected president later that year, but then the country suffered an economic crash brought on by the COVID-19 pandemic, a tourism downturn and his economic decisions.

Anura Kumara Dissanayake later replaced him and became president, for which he campaigned on an anti-corruption ticket in 2024, promising to take action against anyone suspected of corruption in the Rajapaksa family and administrations that preceded them.
