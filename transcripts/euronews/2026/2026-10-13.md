Qatar’s emir vows that country will emerge stronger as he outlines national strategies

Qatar's emir outlined in a speech to the nation how the Gulf nation has managed to overcome the consequences of the Iran war and will emerge stronger and better equipped to adapt to global changes by diversifying its economy and focusing on non-carbon industries in addition to existing LNG exports.
Qatar’s Emir Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani said on Tuesday that his country "will emerge from this crisis more resilient and better equipped to adapt to global changes," outlining the country's national strategies which now also focus on diversifying the economy towards the "non-hydrocarbon sectors," in addition to the traditional model of liquefied natural gas global supply which Qatar wants to increase.

In a speech to the nation at the opening of the Shura Council’s annual session in Doha, Sheikh Tamim made it clear that "Qatar was attacked on its territory by Iran, in a blatant violation of its sovereignty, the provisions of international law, and the principles of good-neighborly relations."

“Qatar has always preferred to resolve disputes through negotiation and diplomatic and peaceful means," Sheikh Tamim added.

"Our commitment to dialogue and peaceful solutions does not mean being complacent about defending our country," he said. "Our right to defend our sovereignty does not conflict with our determination to prevent a wider war."

Iran has consistently maintained that it was only targeting US interests among its neighbours during the war.

Against the backdrop of widening conflicts across the region, he stressed the need for the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries to cooperate, stating that "the security of the GCC states and the protection of their sovereignty and stability are non-negotiable."

Sheikh Tamim urged "to redouble our efforts to secure navigation in the Gulf and the Strait of Hormuz, and to protect energy and water facilities, as well as civil infrastructure."

Sheikh Tamim also outlined the country's response during the war and its strategies going forward, based on long-term planning before the crisis and the measures Qatar implemented to adapt to the war's consequences.

He addressed "the extraordinary events" that impacted the country and the region, which "demonstrated (...) our ability to respond to crises efficiently and responsibly" and that "resilience isn't born in the moment of crisis; rather, it is the result of the planning that precedes it."

"In this crisis, Qatar has shouldered three responsibilities simultaneously: protecting its sovereignty, fulfilling its role in energy security and the global economy, and keeping the doors of diplomacy open," Sheikh Tamim said.

Based on these responsibilities, Qatar's Emir outlined "four key elements" that formed the country's strategy in addressing the crisis: "first, defending the homeland firmly without being drawn into war; second, maintaining essential services, supply chains, and economic activity; third, contributing to efforts to secure a ceasefire and reopen waterways; and fourth, urging negotiations and seeking opportunities to return to talks to reach peaceful resolutions to the conflict."

Sheikh Tamim acknowledged Qatar's key role in global energy systems, supplying about one-fifth of the world’s liquefied natural gas (LNG), and said that "any disruption affecting it is quickly reflected in markets and consumers everywhere."

"The closure of the Strait of Hormuz, together with the direct damage inflicted on some energy facilities in Ras Laffan Industrial City, disrupted a significant portion of the country’s exports," the Qatari emir said.

Signalling a key development, Sheikh Tamim said Qatar absorbed the shock by focusing on the "non-hydrocarbon sectors", which "continued to grow with their contribution reaching approximately 80% of real GDP."

He explained that the "non-hydrocarbon sectors" alternative "reflects the resilience of the national economy and its ability to sustain growth under exceptional and complex circumstances", and that "the stock of foreign direct investment continued to grow."

He revealed that "the total revenues declined by 42% during the first half of the year, while public expenditure fell by 14%, without compromising essential services or development priorities," as "the state did not succumb to panic."

Russia’s 2027 draft budget allocates a record share to defence and security, as regions quietly divert funds for foster children and orphans to war payments and “coffin money” for soldiers’ families is cut by up to 95% in some areas.
Russia's parliament will vote later this month on a budget that allocates a record 44% of federal spending to defence and security — double the share for social programmes — as the Kremlin presses ahead with a fifth year of all-out war in Ukraine.

The State Duma, Russia's lower house of parliament, is due to begin examining the 2027–2029 draft on 22 October.

President Vladimir Putin's United Russia party won an absolute constitutional majority in September's parliamentary elections, making major amendments unlikely before it passes.

Military expenditure this year has reached 17 trillion roubles (€181 billion) against the 12.1 trillion (€129 billion) originally budgeted, showing the escalating cost of the full-scale invasion Russia launched against Ukraine in February 2022. Defence and security's 44% share of the 2027 budget is the highest on record.

The Finance Ministry's draft lists "fulfilling social obligations to citizens" as its top priority. The figures tell a different story. Social policy spending will fall 7% in 2027, from 7.1 trillion roubles (€76 billion) to 6.65 trillion (€71 billion), covering pensions, payments to veterans, maternity benefits and other support.

The government has pledged to index these payments against inflation. Russia's official inflation rate has exceeded 8% for much of the past two years, and independent economists put the real figure higher.

The Kremlin has announced a 10 trillion rouble (€107 billion) "children's budget" over the next three years, covering maternity payments, child benefits, and housing programmes. The ratio of those funds to defence spending is one to five.

Federal payments to servicemen and their families fall under the "national defence" section, whose spending will rise by roughly a quarter next year.

The federal budget is not the only source of pressure on social spending. Russia's regions partly fund war-related payments, many of which are already running deficits, forcing local authorities to cut civilian budgets.

In late September the Arkhangelsk region redirected more than 63 million roubles originally earmarked for social support — two-thirds from payments to foster parents, the rest from support for large families — to one-off war payments and death compensation.

A few days earlier the Tula region cut 54 million roubles from spending on foster children, low-income households, families with disabled children, housing repairs for orphans and support for labour veterans, redirecting the sum to servicemen's pay and compensation for the dead.

In St Petersburg, authorities have prepared a bill reducing spending on education, transport repairs and medical projects while increasing payments to servicemen.

Regional lump-sum death payments — known informally as "coffin money" — have been cut across Russia since autumn of last year.

The payments are regional supplements to the federal one-time compensation paid to families of troops killed in action, and regions sharply raised them in 2022–2023 as they competed to attract recruits.

In Ulyanovsk region the payment stood at 1 million roubles (€10,700). By February it had been halved, then cut again to 50,000 roubles (€533).

In Bashkortostan, the initial 2 million roubles (€21,300) payment has fallen to no more than 100,000 (€1,100).

Updated: 13/10/2026 - 15:37 GMT+2
Kazakhstan hosted its first Open Asian Falconry Cup on 9 and 10 October in Bozanbay, a village in the country's east, bringing together 176 falconers from 10 countries.
… More
Golden eagles, hawks and saker falcons featured in traditional hunting contests at the inaugural Open Asian Falconry Cup, the main competition of the fifth Shygys Salburyny International Festival.

Participants came from Kazakhstan, Türkiye, Russia, China, Mongolia, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, Georgia, Spain and Hungary. Around 15,000 spectators attended the two-day event, which also featured sighthound races, the taming of unbroken horses, mounted archery, the horseback coin-picking contest, known as "tenge ilu", tug of war and other traditional strength contests.

The festival celebrates qusbegilik, Kazakhstan's centuries-old tradition of hunting with trained birds of prey. Golden eagle hunters, known as "berkutchi", demonstrated their skills alongside other falconers.

Serikbolsyn Zhalgasuly from the Zhambyl Region won the golden eagle competition, while Zaki Makhmudkhan took the hawk title and Temirali Tolegenuly won the saker falcon event.

Organisers hope the international competition will promote Kazakhstan's hunting heritage and encourage wider recognition of these traditions.

Europe must switch to 'war-mode' defence production, Polish defence minister says
Poland's Deputy Prime Minister and Defence Minister Wladyslaw Kosiniak-Kamysz speaks during press conference in Warsaw, Poland, 26 September, 2025.

Polish Defence Minister Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz said that Europe must accelerate the production of ammunition and weapons to match Russia’s pace, warning that failure to do so would negatively impact Europe.
Poland’s Minister of National Defence and Deputy Prime Minister, Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz, said Europe must adopt a “war-mode” mentality in its defence sector or risk harming Europeans in the future.

“I am actually an advocate for the war-mode when it comes to the production in defence sector,” he said at a press conference on the sidelines of the Warsaw Security Forum on Tuesday.

Russia shifted towards wartime defence production before its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, and is projected to spend 7.5% of its gross domestic product on its armed forces in 2026.

In 2025, Russia produced roughly seven million artillery, mortar, tank and rocket rounds, according to a recent Estonian intelligence report. Russian forces currently use roughly 10,000 to 15,000 rounds per day in their continued aggression against Ukraine.

In contrast, Europe’s defence industry has been shaped by decades of underinvestment and fragmentation.

Russia is outpacing the EU in ammunition production, with the 27 member states and Norway expected to produce just two million 150mm ammunition shells this year, according to a leaked report on Europe’s defence readiness.

Rearmament efforts by European countries and NATO members have driven the strongest annual increase in defence spending since the Cold War. Despite this, EU member states’ total defence spending in 2025 was estimated at €418 billion, or 2.2% of EU's total GDP, the report states.

Kosiniak-Kamysz said the “time has come” for Europe to prepare to defend itself by multiplying its defence production.

Failure to do so would be a “costly” mistake for Europeans, he warned, adding: “It's one of the disadvantages, but also it will impact (and) affect us negatively in future generations.”

Poland is among the biggest spenders of its GDP on defence capabilities, spending 4.7% on core defence capabilities, according to the US envoy to NATO, Matthew Whitaker.

Updated: 13/10/2026 - 11:17 GMT+2
At least three people were killed and 37 injured in Kharkiv as Russia launched repeated aerial attacks on the north-eastern Ukrainian city on Monday and overnight. Strikes also caused damage near Lviv, in western Ukraine.
… More
Rescue workers recovered three bodies from the rubble of a four-storey residential building in Kharkiv’s western Kholodnohirskyi district, where an entire section of the block had collapsed. The attack also damaged a five-storey building in the Shevchenkivskyi district. A ground-floor bank and several vehicles caught fire, while windows were blown out in nearby apartment blocks.

Emergency crews, police, bomb-disposal teams and search dogs remained at the sites, looking for people who may still be trapped. A State Emergency Service responder was injured during the operation.

In the western Lviv region, a Russian drone attack set two homes on fire and damaged three cars in Murovane. No injuries were reported there.

Ukraine’s air force said Russia launched five ballistic missiles, nine Banderol loitering munitions and 108 drones from the evening of 12 October. Air defences shot down or electronically suppressed 88 drones, but strikes were recorded at 17 locations and debris fell at seven others.

Ceuta braces for a symbolic royal visit by King Felipe VI and Queen Letizia after a mass migration crisis strained the enclave’s social cohesion.
… More
Spain's royal couple are expected in Ceuta on Tuesday, 13 October, as the North African enclave continues to recover from an unprecedented migration crisis. On 30 and 31 July, more than 70,000 people crossed the border from Morocco, a figure approaching the city's entire population of 80,000.

Most returned within hours, but thousands remained in makeshift camps and emergency shelters for weeks. On Sunday, more than 350 police officers and soldiers took part in an operation to clear the last major informal settlement at El Guano, transferring its residents to reception centres.

The crisis has exposed deep divisions in Ceuta, where Muslims make up around 40% of the population. While some residents have called for tighter border controls, others have criticised hostility towards migrants. In the predominantly Muslim neighbourhood of El Príncipe, locals sheltered women and children, while community representative Ahmed Enfed-Dal condemned attacks on shelters and attempts to obstruct Red Cross food deliveries.

The royal couple are expected to meet local officials and civil society representatives before travelling to Melilla on Wednesday. Their first visit to the two enclaves since 2007 comes ahead of Spain's 29 November general election, with migration a major political issue. Morocco's territorial claims add a further diplomatic dimension to the trip.

Police are investigating after TV footage showed riders being evacuated from the 60-metre high Goliath ride.
One person has died and several others were injured in an incident on a roller coaster at a Six Flags park in the US state of Georgia, prompting the company to temporarily shut nine similar rides at six of its parks on Monday.

A Six Flags spokesperson said the attractions, which resemble the coaster involved at Six Flags Over Georgia, were closed “out of an abundance of caution.”

The park lies about 24 kilometres (15 miles) northwest of Atlanta. Officials have not said how the person died, how the others were hurt or what condition they are in. Detectives from the Cobb County Police Department’s major crimes unit are investigating.

The spokesperson said the park and local authorities had responded immediately, and that Six Flags was cooperating fully with the investigation.

“We are deeply saddened by the guest incident at our park today,” the spokesperson said, adding that the company was supporting the guests and staff affected.

Neither the authorities nor Six Flags named the ride, but local television stations broadcast footage of passengers being evacuated from the Goliath coaster.

According to the park’s website, Goliath opened in 2006 and reaches speeds of 70 mph (about 113 kph) and heights of 200 feet (61 metres). Over roughly three and a half minutes, riders “navigate through razor-sharp turns and soar over colossal hills,” the website says.

“I cannot imagine the horror the patrons and employees witnessed today, and my heart aches for the family who lost a loved one,” said Lisa Cupid, chairwoman of the Cobb County Board of Commissioners. She said the victim’s next of kin had been informed.

Cupid described the park as a major part of the county’s tourism industry, drawing visitors from across the south east of the US.

“We are all grappling with how something so devastating could happen at a place that has given generations of families such happy memories,” she said.

The death is at least the seventh at the Georgia park, according to the Doan Law Firm, which specialises in amusement park accidents. They include a 2006 case in which a man had a suspected heart attack on Goliath.

Clashes near Brussels-Midi station leave around ten arrested, including nine minors, as student and anti-austerity protests continue in Belgium.
… More
Belgian police arrested ten people, including nine minors, after clashes involving hundreds of young people near Brussels-Midi railway station on Monday, 12 October. Officers reported being targeted with stones and fireworks as unrest broke out on the sidelines of a national strike and a student gathering.

Police deployed tear gas and water cannon as groups moved between the station, Place Bara and the Cureghem neighbourhood, occasionally dispersing into Anderlecht and Saint-Gilles before returning. The Clemenceau metro station was temporarily closed, while a car, several scooters and street furniture were set alight. Officers also seized fireworks, flammable liquids and aerosol cans.

The violence came amid growing protests against austerity measures, rising living costs and education cuts. Since 1 October, secondary-school pupils have been demonstrating against plans to save €500 million from French-speaking education by 2029. Trade unions are also opposing Prime Minister Bart De Wever's proposed €10 billion savings programme.

In Liège, more than 200 arrests have been reported since the student protests began. A separate anti-austerity march in Brussels drew around 30,000 people on Friday, according to police, and was also followed by clashes.

Monthly rents in Yerevan now run to €800–900 for a two-room flat, up 23.5% year on year in August, while Tbilisi rents have stabilised after 60–70% jumps following the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. Russian nationals account for 72% of all foreign property transactions in Armenia.
Rental prices in Georgia and Armenia rose 20–30% year-on-year over the summer as Russians fearing a new wave of mobilisation for Russia's war in Ukraine sought accommodation in neighbouring countries.

Georgia remains the most accessible destination: Russian citizens can stay for up to 360 days without a visa and reset the clock with a brief visit to a neighbouring country. Tens of thousands now live there.

In Tbilisi, monthly rents for standard housing start at €450, rising to €2,700 or more for larger flats or upmarket apartments. The average nominal monthly wage in Georgia stands at around 2,165 lari (€750), according to the National Statistics Office.

After a summer peak, rents in Georgia have fallen and broadly stabilised. The sharpest increases came in 2022–2023, when rents jumped up to 70% following Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

Russians accounted for more than 55% of all property transactions involving foreign buyers in 2023, but their share has fallen by a third since.

In 2026, Georgians made up 76% of transactions in Tbilisi's primary housing market, according to investment bank Galt & Taggart, with Israeli buyers at 11% and Russians at 3%. Demand is more diversified in the Black Sea city of Batumi.

Demand is most acute in Armenia, where Russians — and Armenians who had been living in Russia and are seeking to avoid mobilisation — are concentrating mainly in Yerevan.

Monthly rent for a two-room flat in the capital currently runs to €850. Russian citizens can enter without a visa and stay for up to 180 days per year.

According to Armenia's Cadastre Committee, average prices for flats in Yerevan apartment blocks rose 2.9% in August from July and 23.5% year on year. Russian nationals account for around 72% of all property transactions carried out by foreign nationals in Armenia.

A 10% tax, now required when registering lease contracts with the Cadastre, is typically paid by tenants.

In August, foreign citizens took part in 506 purchase and sale transactions nationwide, representing 7.6% of all deals, with 70.2% of those purchases — 177 properties — made in Yerevan.

The average nominal monthly wage in Armenia is 320,763 dram (€790), with substantially higher pay in sectors such as IT, finance and mining.

Belarus has drawn Russians since Moscow's all-out war began, first as a transit point and later as a place to stay.

After partial mobilisation was announced in September 2022, demand for short and medium-term rentals in Minsk and border cities such as Brest and Gomel surged sharply.

With European borders closed to Russians and sanctions tightening, Belarus has since attracted longer-term residents and property buyers.

Russians can apply for a temporary residence permit, with a rental contract serving as proof of accommodation and while some websites are blocked Belarus has not gone as far as Russia in restricting the internet.
