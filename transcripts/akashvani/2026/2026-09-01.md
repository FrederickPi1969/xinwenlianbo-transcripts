India has strongly rejected a ruling by the World Bank-constituted Court of Arbitration over the Indus Waters Treaty, rejecting the tribunal’s authority and calling its decisions legally invalid.

In a social media post, The Ministry of External Affairs (MEA) said India has never recognised the arbitration panel, did not participate in its proceedings and would not be bound by its rulings. New Delhi also reiterated that the Indus Waters Treaty remains in abeyance.

The statement came after the Court of Arbitration in The Hague ruled that India’s reasons for putting the treaty on hold did not justify its suspension or termination. The panel called on India to continue observing its obligations under the 1960 water-sharing agreement.

India, however, rejected the ruling, arguing that the arbitration panel itself was established in violation of the terms of the treaty. The MEA said the panel has no jurisdiction whatsoever over India’s sovereign decisions concerning the treaty or its infrastructure projects.

New Delhi suspended the Indus Waters Treaty following the Pahalgam terror attack in April last year, citing national security concerns and continued cross-border terrorism.

Rescue teams in Nepal are racing against time to reach more than 600 workers believed to be trapped in mud-filled tunnels at the Trishuli-3A hydropower site. Crews are using explosives, heavy machinery and air pumps to clear the tunnels and search for survivors.

According to the Nepal’s disaster management authority, 939 people have died and 3,925 are missing, including 592 foreign nationals. So far, 279 people have been rescued from hydropower sites, while more than 11,000 people have been rescued across the country.

The rescue operation is being supported by Nepalese and Chinese military teams, along with experts from India, South Korea and Australia. Severe flooding and landslides have made it difficult for rescuers to locate the buried tunnels.

India has sent specialised teams to help with both rescue operations and the identification of victims. A 19-member Indian forensic team began work in Kathmandu, assisting with DNA testing to identify recovered remains.

An Indian tunnel-rescue team is also working at hydropower sites in Chilime and Langtang. Experts are helping Nepalese authorities search for 933 workers and employees who have been missing since August 26, with many believed to be trapped inside the tunnels.

At least 11 hydropower projects were damaged by the floods. Families say rescue efforts have been too slow and are urging authorities to act quickly. Downstream in the Chitwan, nearly 300 bodies have been recovered from rivers. Many remain unidentified, so authorities are using DNA testing. Due to a shortage of freezers, some bodies have been temporarily buried until they can be identified.

Nepal has asked several countries for help, including freezers, DNA-testing equipment, drones, bridges and specialised tunnel-rescue tools. A team of 18 Indian forensic experts is assisting with DNA identification.

In Tibet, Chinese state media reports 16 deaths and 546 missing people. China has rejected claims that its hydropower projects caused the disaster, calling such allegations malicious speculation.

Amid the floods in Nepal and the rising water levels of rivers in Bihar, the Animal and Fisheries Resources Department has advised people not to consume fish caught from floodwaters. According to the department, during floods, fish may come into contact with sewage, dead animals and other contaminated substances, increasing the risk of contamination by bacteria, viruses and other pathogens. Consumption of such fish may be harmful to health.

The department has clarified that fish caught from floodwaters should not be used for consumption until the floodwater has stabilised and the condition of water bodies has returned to normal. The department has also appealed to people not to consume fish that appear unknown or unusual. People have been advised to avoid buying, selling or consuming fish caught from floodwaters.
