## Serbia’s Student-Led Movement Ousts Strongman Aleksander Vučić in Shock Election Result

“Students really changed the whole political landscape of the country, and maybe even more important, they changed political imagination. They changed our understanding of what’s politically possible for all of us,” says journalist Lela Vujanić, who speaks to us from Belgrade about the groundbreaking elections. “This was a great democratic opening and democratic awakening, not only for the students, but for the rest of the country also.”

AMY GOODMAN: This is Democracy Now!, democracynow.org. I’m Amy Goodman in Colorado, with Juan González in Chicago.

In Serbia, longtime nationalist leader Aleksandar Vučić conceded defeat following elections Sunday in a contest that pitted Vučić and his right-wing party against a student-led movement that had weakened his hold on power after two years of anti-government protests. The student-led movement first rose up in November 2024 following the collapse of a roof at a train station in the city of Novi Sad, which killed 16 people. The disaster became a focal point for protests over government corruption, cronyism, political violence and democratic backsliding. President Vučić conceded defeat after early results showed the Student List poised to win more than 135 seats in the 250-member parliament, compared with only 105 for Vučić’s party. The historic defeat marks the end of a 14-year rule and a seismic shift in Serbian politics.

Tens of thousands of residents flooded into the streets to celebrate following the concession.

LELA VUJANIĆ: Hi, Amy. Thank you for having me. I was listening the Brazilian segment, and I’m super happy for Lula’s victory and for Brazilian people voting this way.

But, yes, we have something quite historic and extraordinary happening in Serbia, as you said. Yesterday, it was Election Day, parliamentary elections. And our mass civic, social movement, that’s led by Serbian students, was able — that lasts already for two years — was able not only to challenge Vučić and his ruling SNS party, but to win popular vote in a landslide victory, winning 52% of the votes.

What’s also important, that they campaigned individually, I mean, their electoral list called Student List. When I say “individually,” on the other hand, people of Serbia actually did the campaign and campaigned together with the students and with their Electoral List. What’s interesting, that students wiped out everybody else. Former established oppositional parties didn’t pass the electoral threshold, which is actually set very low in Serbia, around 3%. So, as you can see, this changes everything. In a way, students really changed the whole political landscape of the country, and maybe even more important, they changed political imagination. They changed our understanding of what’s politically possible for all of us.

People were celebrating throughout the night across Serbia and Belgrade. There was also a moment of disbelief, because only few people in Serbia yesterday would say that everything — that Vučić will — that everything will resolve peacefully. Most of the people even expected some sort of the state of emergency, election being rigged. So, everybody was quite surprised when the former president got on TV yesterday around 10 p.m. and said, “Yes, students won, and I acknowledge the results.”

JUAN GONZÁLEZ: Lela, I wanted to ask you — the press has described the Student List movement as being leaderless. And could you talk about how it became organized and whether it is, in fact, leaderless?

LELA VUJANIĆ: Thank you. Great question. So, for Serbia, what happened yesterday, it’s extraordinary and historic for Serbia, but I think it’s quite extraordinary globally and that this Serbian case will be studied and researched in the years to come.

So, the movement really is horizontal. And from its beginning, it was based and organized through — we say in Serbian plenums, so popular assemblies, based on direct democracy principles. So, after the fall of the canopy, of newly rebuilt canopy of the train station, students demanded political accountability from political class in the country, and they occupied literally all faculties in the country, across all four university centers in Serbia. They organized based on direct democracy principles, made their decisions through deliberations on plenums, and actually spread the model throughout the country. And citizens also adopted the model and organized based on the same principles, organized in their locales. So, citizens run — and this is still active — popular assemblies in their neighborhoods and in their municipalities. So, yes, this was great, great democratic opening and democratic awakening, not only for the students, but for the rest of the country also.

JUAN GONZÁLEZ: And the impact of these election results on the rest of Europe, especially since Serbia for years has been one of the countries, I guess, closest to Russia of the European countries?

LELA VUJANIĆ: Yes, Serbia has a somewhat specific position. It’s not member of the EU. It’s for 12 years already a candidate country. It didn’t introduce sanctions to Russia. But you need to remember — and Serbia is no stranger to Democracy Now! Democracy Now! reported almost 30 years ago about this controversial NATO bombing of Yugoslavia. Regarding student programs and our future relations of Serbia with other countries and world powers, so, students said no sanctions to Russia. We continue negotiations with Europe. We continue our strategic partnerships with China. And what’s probably the most interesting, that we will not be complicit, or we will not contribute, or we will not participate in the systemic erasure of Palestinian people.

Hebron municipal officials say about 86 acres of homes and buildings have been destroyed by Israel or marked for demolition. About 300 Palestinian families, roughly 1,500 people, have been forced from their homes, while Hebron’s population of 250,000 people have been sealed off by checkpoints. The demolition came just days before Israel’s national election. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu vowed to “destroy this place,” while far-right Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich filmed himself wearing body armor in front of a Palestinian building being demolished. This is Hebron’s Mayor.

Mayor Yousef al-Jabari: “These actions by the Israeli occupation are driven by the elections. We are days, or even hours, away from the Israeli elections. Netanyahu and the radical ministers in his government are trying to win as many votes as possible from the settlers. The Israeli response is disproportionate, and there is no justification for these actions. They have closed off the city of Hebron and ordered the evacuation of an entire neighborhood in a major Palestinian city. There is no justification for these measures.”

At least eight Palestinians were killed in Israeli attacks across the Gaza Strip, including three security guards at Islamic University in Gaza City on Sunday. According to UNESCO, 95% of Gaza’s higher education campuses have been destroyed or damaged since Israel’s war on Gaza began in October 2023. Since last October’s so-called ceasefire, about 1,500 Palestinians have been killed in Israeli attacks. This is Alaa Baroud, whose brother was killed in an Israeli strike on Sunday.

Alaa Baroud: “The war is ongoing. Israel claims there is a truce, but on the ground, what truce? What ceasefire are they talking about? Every day there are martyrs. Every day there are attacks. Every day there is destruction.”

The Guardian is reporting that lawyers for jailed U.S. activist James “Fergie” Chambers say that a Spanish court secretly handed his cellphone to the FBI. Chambers is an heir to the Cox Communications fortune, and he is the first U.S. citizen the Trump administration is looking to extradite after being accused of supporting Hamas, in connection with his donations to Palestinians in Gaza and pro-Palestine advocacy. His attorney, Aitor Martínez, said, “I’ve never seen anything like this in an extradition case. Spanish judicial authorities can’t surrender control over the personal effects of a person sought in extradition just because a foreign government has asked for them.” A Spanish treaty bars the transfer of personal items before extradition is granted.

The U.S. military has granted SpaceX access to some of the Pentagon’s most sensitive and confidential satellite tracking data. That’s according to The Guardian, which reports the new agreement between U.S. Space Force and Elon Musk gives SpaceX access to restricted information used to track satellites and debris on Earth’s orbit. This follows Musk’s efforts to privatize space traffic control. Last year, the so-called Department of Government Efficiency, led by Musk, fired scores of employees at the Traffic Coordination System for Space, while the Trump administration has repeatedly attempted to gut funding to the program.

Meanwhile, officials in Ukraine report that Musk’s internet satellite services Starlink and Starshield are functioning normally again, after the service was altered to drop connections of fast-moving objects over Ukraine, disrupting military aircraft and drone flights. On Saturday, Musk acknowledged he had intentionally throttled service to Starlink, reversing his previous claim that the Trump administration was behind the move. Musk called Starlink’s use in drone attacks a violation of its terms of service.

The number of people on food stamps in the U.S. has fallen by 13% from 41.6 million people in July 2025, before the One Big Beautiful Bill Act took effect, to 36.5 million in June 2026. Advocates say more people are seeking help from food banks or going hungry. Currently, 47 million people, including one in five children, are living in food-insecure households nationwide.

A federal appeals court has ruled in favor of the Trump administration’s policy allowing the indefinite detention of thousands of immigrants without the right to be released on bond. The decision by the 5th Circuit Court of Appeals would mean even immigrants who’ve lived in the United States for decades and have no criminal record could be detained for months — even years — while their cases are reviewed. Other U.S. courts have overwhelmingly opposed Trump’s indefinite detention policy, saying it violates immigrants’ constitutional and due process rights. The ruling centers on Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi, with advocates warning the impacts will be felt nationwide. The American Immigration Council said in a statement, “When courts degrade the meaning of liberty for some, we all suffer the consequences.” The Supreme Court is set to hear a case challenging the policy during this term.

This comes as deaths in ICE custody continue to skyrocket under Trump’s second term. Over the weekend, it was confirmed a 48-year-old immigrant from El Salvador has died while jailed at the D. Ray James ICE Processing Center in Folkston, Georgia. Luis Sandoval was reportedly found on Friday morning with a “ligature around his neck.” He was pronounced dead about an hour later. He’s at least the 60th immigrant to have died in ICE custody since Trump returned to office. Sandoval’s death came amid an ongoing hunger strike inside the Folkston facility denouncing inhumane conditions. Immigrants there have been reportedly threatened with force-feeding and other retaliatory actions. The ICE facility is run by the for-profit prison company GEO Group.

In more related news, a 79-year-old pastor from Uganda has been released from a for-profit ICE jail in Colorado after 13 months in custody. The Rev. Edward Nalwamba reportedly lost 35 pounds and the strength to walk after his confinement at the Aurora ICE Processing Center, which is operated by the GEO Group. He also suffered from pneumonia for three weeks and did not receive any medical treatment. On the day of his release, Nalwamba had to be carried out by three other detainees, as ICE provided no wheelchair.

The Department of Justice has filed a lawsuit seeking to block the results of the upcoming U.S. Senate election in Alaska if Republican incumbent Dan S. Sullivan loses. The lawsuit alleges the appearance of another Senate candidate, Dan J. Sullivan Jr., on Alaska’s ballot would mislead voters. While in Florida on Friday, President Trump confirmed he’d ordered the DOJ to file the lawsuit — less than two weeks ahead of the midterms — saying, “I’m the one that said do it.” Attorneys for Alaska’s Democratic Senate candidate Mary Peltola, and the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee, have filed a motion urging a federal judge to dismiss the lawsuit, which they denounced as “an unprecedented pre-emptive strike on democratic elections.”
