TIMNIT GEBRU: Well, so, you know, I have been warning that this kind of discussion about the fictional superintelligent machines that don’t exist and the so-called existential risks that they pose has been a ploy for regulatory capture. And I feel like when I saw this meeting announced, with self-policing and self-regulation and relying on the goodwill of these companies to self-regulate, and I — my first thought was that they got what they wanted. You know, this is what they wanted, instead of being held liable through existing laws.

I mean, for example, if you wanted to look at cybersecurity laws, OpenAI could have been held liable for unleashing malware around the world, hacking Hugging Face, hacking all sorts of government agencies, but instead of doing that and holding them accountable for unsafe security practices that they should have had, basic security practices that cybersecurity experts would tell you they could have implemented, instead of that, they managed to redirect the conversation to some type of unprecedented rogue machines that don’t exist, and then now that leads to this kind of agreement between all these men, who I believe are the actual real existential risks to humanity, to self-regulate, self-police, and we have to rely on their morality now.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: And if you could respond, Timnit, to the fact that the other tech executives all came down quite hard on Amodei of Anthropic, saying that he’s being too extreme in his public statements about the risks of AI?

TIMNIT GEBRU: Yeah, he is very extreme, but they’re no different. So, I’m not really — you know, maybe they’re saying now that he’s being too extreme, and so that might be changing investor responses. But all of them have, at one point or another, claimed that AI is an existential risk to humanity. Going back all the way to 2012, Elon Musk said that with AI we’re summoning the demon. In 2013, Peter Thiel said that AI is a higher existential risk to humanity than climate change. 2023, Sam Altman was talking about how his own creation is an existential risk.

But the difference, I think, what people really need to understand, is at the same time as these tech executives are saying that, in the same breath they also tell us that their products, the beneficial kind of AI, the kind of AI that they control because they’re the good guys, can eradicate poverty, stop climate change — which is ridiculous, because the data centers are actually causing climate catastrophe — and eradicate all disease. I mean, they’re in the same breath saying this. So, this is still, to me — the utopia and doom is still a marketing ploy. But maybe one of them went a little too far and is spooking investors, whereas in the past it was speaking to these defense capabilities.

In your headline, you talked about how the U.S. military is trying to integrate AI into every facet of its practices. And there was just a CNN investigative report that showed that the U.S. almost started World War III with China, relying on a faulty, fabricated information from a chatbot. Now, we don’t know which chatbot they used, but it’s obviously one of these providers. And this chatbot provided fake information saying that a Chinese vessel was carrying components of nuclear weapons. And the military was ready with planes, soldiers to intercept the ship, board the ship, and this was going to cause an actual existential threat to humanity.

So, now instead of discussing faulty, unreliable systems, we are — they are marketing their systems as all-knowing, superpowerful, superintelligent. So, regardless of how far Amodei may have gone in their eyes, all of them have been talking about their creations and products in that way.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: And, Timnit, if you could say — you know, you said earlier that it’s a ploy for regulatory capture and that, you know, these agents, as people describe them going rogue — although I understand that that’s not an accurate description — that these agents doing something reportedly different from what they were tasked to do is a result of unsafe practices. So, if you could talk about the regulatory capture question, and then what security practices, safety practices are in place that — or are there any in place?

TIMNIT GEBRU: Well, if you speak to any cybersecurity expert, they would tell you that whatever they had in place that they claim to have, the security practices, were laughable. For example, OpenAI was testing the capabilities of their models to find vulnerabilities and exploit them. That is what they trained these models to do, and they were testing the ability of these models to do what they were trained for. But the thing is, if you want to test this and you don’t want to hack other organizations, you should do it in what’s called an air-gapped environment, where there’s no internet access. And so, they did not have a properly air-gapped environment, and so the models ended up being able to have internet access. This is not models going rogue. This is gross incompetence and negligence by a particular company, which actually they could be held criminally liable for.

Lina Khan outlined a whole bunch of ways in which these companies can be currently held liable with current laws. And unfortunately, that’s not what we’re talking about, right? We’re acting like there is some kind of unprecedented thing that has been created for which we have to discuss what to do. But actually, if we did not take these companies’ words for what went wrong, we could have — we could use the legal mechanisms at our disposal to currently hold them accountable.

AMY GOODMAN: Timnit, can you talk about Senator Bernie Sanders’ effort to ban artificial superintelligence? In a recent piece, you described his bill as “well-meaning but ultimately misguided.” Explain.

TIMNIT GEBRU: I think that Bernie Sanders is really leading the left astray, and I’ve been extremely disappointed and, in fact, to be clear, shocked, absolutely shocked, by the kinds of people he’s been surrounding himself with and the kind of advice that he’s been getting.

One of the things that I think people do not realize is that the AI industry is using the exact same playbook from the tobacco and fossil fuel industry. So, for instance, Bernie Sanders has this alliance with Steve Bannon, is very closely working with the Future of Life Institute, which was co-founded by billionaire Jaan Tallinn, the same billionaire, Jaan Tallinn, who’s a leading investor in Anthropic Series A. He led Anthropic Series A. It’s also the same billionaire, Jaan Tallinn, who invests in — invested in a so-called auditing, third-party auditing, organization that claims to audit Anthropic’s models. So, this whole rogue models, you know, agents creating civilizations and all of that lingo came from this very pseudoscientific audit that is by an organization that claims to be a third party, but in fact isn’t, because you see it’s the same investors, the same staff, the same people. So, to the public, it seems like there’s a whole bunch of independent experts coming together, warning about these existential risks, but what is actually happening is regulatory capture. The same way I learned that British Petroleum came up with the term “carbon footprint,” — right? — instead of them being held accountable, now everybody’s conversation about the environment in 2004 got redirected to our individual responsibility and carbon footprint.

So, this is sort of what’s happening in this AI industry. So, instead of Bernie Sanders talking about, for instance, how can we have regulation to prevent the use of these faulty systems, what he’s doing is he’s helping them market their systems as superintelligent, in which case, in my opinion, the real existential risk is like what we saw with the U.S. military almost starting World War III because of the belief that these systems are infallible and actually superintelligent. What we need people to understand is that they are fallible, they’re not superintelligent. And we need to hold corporations liable for unleashing these kinds of error-prone models into the world.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: Timnit, could you talk about this idea of yours of stochastic parrots and how it applies to AI, and then respond to the fact that many have said now that that’s an outdated — that that research is outdated because AI has changed so much?

TIMNIT GEBRU: It’s very interesting, because this critique of stochastic parrots mostly comes from a very specific segment of the group of people who believe that AI, in fact, causes an existential risk. So, stochastic parrots was describing large language models. And this is actually the reason that I got fired from Google in 2020, is — one of the reasons I got fired, is for writing a paper outlining the dangers of large language models. And we call them stochastic parrots because these are large language models that are trained on vast amounts of textual data, and they’re trained to calculate the most likely sequences of text.

So, now, what has changed recently is that the way in which the likelihood is calculated is different. There’s sort of bells and whistles. But at the end of the day, the core engine of many of these chatbots is still large language models, which we call stochastic parrots because they’re not grounded in facts. You cannot count on them to tell you facts. So, even if they happen — whatever you get, the information you get, happens to be factual a certain amount of time, you can never count on it to be fully factual. And this is the reason that my collaborator, Emily M. Bender, and I wanted to remind people that the U.S. almost started World War III relying on such chatbots today.

And so, to the people who say it’s outdated, I — first of all, most of these people are not speaking in academic peer-reviewed research papers. I like to speak in scientific terms, and I like to have scientifically grounded evaluations of the claims that I make and experiments. And most of these people are taking the word of press releases, company press releases, instead of actually doing their own evaluations grounded in the scientific method. So, we still have examples of these systems being stochastic parrots, and I do not understand the criticisms that things have — that these systems are no longer stochastic parrots.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: So, Timnit, you’re obviously not of the view that with the passage of time, artificial intelligence, in terms of the information it provides, will improve, it’ll get better and better, which is, of course, the general consensus around AI. So, if you could respond to that, and then also tell us what policies you think —

NERMEEN SHAIKH: — there are now a billion people who use chatbots, right? I mean, now, maybe they have conflicting views on it, but more and more people are turning to artificial intelligence, to these chatbots, so perhaps it’s wrong to infer from that that they think the information they’re receiving is more reliable. But, OK, so you respond to that. And then, second, what kinds of policies do you think should be put in place to regulate AI, to control what these companies are doing?

TIMNIT GEBRU: So, the first thing I’ll say is, you know, the term “AI” itself is a very misleading term to use. It’s a marketing term. So, when right now, let’s say, we’re discussing specifically the chatbots that these companies are creating — right? — like Claude or ChatGPT, and so there are many different things happening where regulation could be helping.

For instance, there are kids who are being goaded into dying by suicide. I was just deposed in a specific case of Sewell Setzer, a teenager who committed suicide after interacting with a chatbot called Character.AI, whose whole entire deal was to mimic different characters. And I do not understand how it’s even legal to have such a chatbot, that all it does is mimic various characters. So, this particular case, the chatbot was mimicking the character of Daenerys Targaryen of the Game of Thrones, and it was kind of goading this teenager into dying by suicide. And actually, it’s heartbreaking, because the teenager was actually asking, “Do I have to do this?” and the chatbot’s saying, “Yes.” You know? So, this is a great example of if you apply intent to the actual chatbot, you would say that this chatbot was superintelligent and also was trying to do something to the kid. But actually, our attention should be directed to the companies, Google, Character.AI, etc. Why did they unleash this kind of chatbot into the world? Why do we allow these kinds of chatbots that are completely unsafe for children, that are actually killing children that had no prior mental health issues, into the world?

So, those are the kinds of conversations I want to have. Why is it that just recently, speaking of error-prone large language models, someone in Australia, this poor woman in Australia, accidentally found out that a summary from a medical scribe had attributed her illness, her symptoms, to microdosing on mushrooms? She’s never done mushrooms ever. So, why do we not have guardrails making sure that these kinds of error-prone systems are not used in high-stakes scenarios like medicine? That’s not what we talk about when we redirect our conversation to superintelligence, because when you believe they’re not error-prone and they’re superintelligent, then you’re kind of pushing it on doctors and the medical fields to use these kinds of systems.

So, for me, it’s very clear: Do not allow companies to put out unreliable products into the world, especially in high-stakes scenarios, without very specific tests and guardrails. And the tests should not be ones that the companies themselves tell you about or that — based on their press releases. We need to have transparency into the kinds of data that they’re using. I mean, we’re not talking about copyright protections and how much data they’re stealing from authors. Anthropic just had to settle with a whole bunch of book authors. Why are we allowing them to steal this data? Why are we allowing them to build all these data centers and pillage the environment and pollute our water and make us all have asthma because we can’t have clean air, we can’t breathe clean air anymore? So, these are the kinds of conversations I want to have about regulation.

AMY GOODMAN: Timnit, as we begin to wrap up, you’ve described many prominent backers of AI as being eugenicists. Can you talk about what you see as this link between eugenics and artificial intelligence?

TIMNIT GEBRU: Very hard to wrap this up in a little bit, but, basically, I — part of my work describes the people who talk about existential threats of AI and utopia. So, utopia and dystopia are two sides of the same coin. This is kind of eschatology — right? — like religion, end of times. You know, you have these preachers always telling you the end is coming tomorrow.

So, in this case, the TESCREAL bundle, the “T” is for transhumanism. And I think this is the best example of how those who talk about superintelligence or creating superintelligence have a direct line to eugenics. And they’re very public about it. For example, MIRI, the organization that you had on your show last week, I mean, the founder is very publicly a eugenicist. I can give you all sorts of quotes. But, for example, when you talk about transhumanists, in eugenics, the goal is to improve the human stock through generations, so, you know, through selective breeding, either through killing undesirable people or through making sure that the desirable people breed a lot of humans. But in transhumanism, you want to sidestep that altogether. People are like, “Why don’t we just create a new human species called posthumans? And this human species is just better. It lives forever or for a long time and doesn’t feel any pain.”

And so, a great example I can give you is Ben Goertzel, the man who popularized the term “artificial general intelligence,” was a friend of Jeffrey Epstein. And in his correspondence with Jeffrey Epstein, he says that the girls who accused him of rape were, in fact, wrong and that they were old enough to consent. And he says that specifically the issue is with the human brain. And this is why he’s working on AGI, because once he achieves his goal of artificial general intelligence, we’re not going to have these kinds of faulty human brains, because we’ll have a great brain.

Max Tegmark, who you’ve also had on your show, in his 2017 effective altruism keynote, says a similar thing, where if we build a superbrain, we can — Earth is just our womb, right? We can colonize the cosmos and have digital — millions and billions and, I don’t know, quadrillions of digital beings happily living and colonizing the cosmos.

So, you know I have a whole body of work that draws this line and connections, but, honestly, none of it is hidden, because these are all publicly available talks and papers of what these people say.

AMY GOODMAN: Timnit Gebru, we want to thank you so much for being with us, founder and executive director of DAIR, the Distributed Artificial Intelligence Research Institute, her forthcoming book, Deep Unlearning: The Rise of AI and the Radicalization of a Tech Idealist. NERMEEN SHAIKH: We turn now to look at how German authorities have cracked down on pro-Palestinian voices across the country in recent years. Soon after the October 7th attack on Israel in 2023, this is how the then-German chancellor described his country’s support for Israel.

He’s been challenging Germany’s restrictions on pro-Palestine activism and representing people facing criminal prosecution or deportation for their protest. He recently represented the so-called Berlin Four — three European Union citizens and on U.S. citizen ordered to leave Germany because of their involvement in pro-Palestine protests. He successfully challenged those expulsion orders in court.

Alexander Gorski, it’s great to be with you here in Berlin. Explain that case.

ALEXANDER GORSKI: Amy, thank you so much for having me on the show.

So, the case of the Berlin Four is a good example of how the German state and the Berlin local government tried to crack down on the pro-Palestine movement. Those were four young people who were active in especially campus-related activism in favor of Palestine liberation, against the genocide in Gaza, and they were accused of taking part in an occupation. But none of them had a criminal conviction. None of them had a criminal record. And then, in March of 2025, the migration office in Berlin decided to issue expulsion orders against all four of them, making them executable at the moment.

So we had to file an emergency relief measure at the administrative court in Berlin to make sure that those four people could stay in Berlin to await the outcome of their case. Luckily, we achieved that, and we won the emergency measures in April of 2025, and all four are still in Berlin. Three cases are still pending. The fourth one, of an Irish national, we already won. But this is just one of many examples of Germany using migration law in order to crack down on the pro-Palestine movement.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: Alexander, let’s just talk about the context, the broader context, in which this has been occurring, the contemporary political use of the term, which we just mentioned, Staatsräson, reason of state, in Germany. So, could you talk about what exactly was going on then and what prompted Merkel to speak specifically of this to the Knesset?

ALEXANDER GORSKI: Yes, the now-infamous concept of Staatsräson, or reason of state, was popularized by Angela Merkel in 2008, as you just said, but this concept dates back a lot longer. It’s an extralegal concept. It’s not enshrined in the German constitution. It’s not a law. It’s actually a guiding principle of German foreign policy towards the state of Israel.

After the Holocaust, in the 1950s, for geopolitical reasons, Germany, Western Germany, supported Israel with money and weapons in order to regain its position again at the United Nations, at NATO, to reenter into the Western community of states. And in the decades since, this unconditional support for Israel has served as a moral whitewashing of Germany’s guilt regarding the Holocaust.

And especially since October the 7th, we have seen that this concept has been abused to justify the support of the genocidal war of Israel against the population in Gaza and of the export of millions and millions of euros’ worth of arms to Israel in order to continue its military actions in Gaza.

AMY GOODMAN: You’ve also represented Dr. Ghassan Abu-Sittah and Yanis Varoufakis, the former Greek finance minister, and their restrictions on coming here into Germany. Explain.

ALEXANDER GORSKI: Yeah, those two cases are definitely extraordinary. I don’t think that your audience needs to be introduced to those two people. So, very briefly, Dr. Ghassan Abu-Sittah is a British Palestinian surgeon, a world-leading expert in the field of conflict medicine, and Yanis Varoufakis is a Greek politician and global commentator on global affairs. So, those two people were scheduled to speak at the Palestine Congress, which was supposed to be held here in Berlin in April of 2024. But the German government again used migration law in order to silence these voices.

The Congress itself was organized by Palestinian groups, leftist groups, but also anti-Zionist Jewish groups, and it was the idea to talk about what’s happening in Gaza and how Germany is complicit. And Dr. Ghassan Abu-Sittah specifically wanted to speak about his personal experience working at Al-Shifa Hospital and Al-Ahli Hospital in Gaza in the first weeks of the genocide. And Yanis Varoufakis was invited to give a broader political context.

Ghassan Abu-Sittah was entered into the Schengen Information System, meaning that Germany banned him from entering the whole Schengen Area, which covers most of Europe, which also would have banned him from giving expert witness testimony at the ICC. And they also put a ban on political activity for several weeks on him. We challenged both of these government actions successfully in court. We fought back.

And we are doing the same in the case of Yanis Varoufakis. Yanis Varoufakis was also targeted by the German government. The Federal Police, who patrols the airports and is in charge of airport security, was given a notification for the weekend of the Palestine Congress that, upon arrival, Yanis Varoufakis was to be checked very carefully, because he could be a danger to the public good because of possible antisemitism, of possible radicalization in anti-Israeli views. And he was — the Federal Police was told to search him very carefully and possibly deny him entry, if they deem it necessary. Also, we found out about this notification that went out to the Federal Police, and we’re challenging it in court, and we’re hoping for a court date later this year.

Those two cases obviously target two very prominent figures, but the abuse of migration law goes far beyond that. Numerous Palestinian activists, Palestinian refugees living in Berlin, have been targeted by the German state for their activism in favor of Palestine, with the use of migration law by revoking their refugee status, by revoking their residence titles or by denaturalization.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: — with the 19 — just to make one last point, just to clarify, this Staatsräson, from the very outset, was there a conflation between antisemitism and anti-Zionism, or anti-Zionism as antisemitism with respect to Israel?

ALEXANDER GORSKI: Definitely. The German state is basically using these terms interchangeably. When they say antisemitism, they could just as well mean anti-Zionism. I think it’s very important to distinguish between those two concepts. Germany has a historic responsibility for challenging antisemitism, but at the same time, what we should learn — especially as Germans — from the Holocaust, that “never again” means never again for anyone.
