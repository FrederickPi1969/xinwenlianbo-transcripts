## “American Scoundrel”: From Roy Cohn to Stephen Miller, “McCarthyism Lives On,” Says Author Kai Bird

Facing political setbacks during his first term as president, Trump reportedly asked, “Where’s my Roy Cohn?” Bird, who co-authored American Scoundrel with his wife Susan Goldmark, says Trump may have found his Cohn in Stephen Miller, the architect of the administration’s mass deportation policies whom Trump just elevated to chief of staff.

“It’s not really a surprise that, cornered and wounded by the recent election results, he reaches out and pulls up Stephen Miller, who’s very similar in his willingness to go on the offensive and never back down, and to say outrageous things to try to change the narrative,” says Bird.

ANJALI KAMAT: We turn now to look more closely at President Trump’s decision to tap his hard-line adviser Stephen Miller to be his new chief of staff. Miller began the job Monday. Trump picked Miller to replace Susie Wiles after Republicans lost control of the House and the Senate.

In a moment, we’ll be joined by the Pulitzer Prize-winning biographer Kai Bird, co-author of the new book American Scoundrel: Roy Cohn’s Dark Journey from Joe McCarthy to Donald Trump. The book examines the life of Roy Cohn, who served as a chief counsel to Senator Joseph McCarthy during the Red Scare in the 1950s and later mentored a young Donald Trump.

In a new piece for The New York Times, Bird draws parallels between Stephen Miller and Roy Cohn. Bird opens the piece by writing, quote, “’Where’s my Roy Cohn?’ Donald Trump cried out early in his first term in the White House. Mr. Cohn, the lawyer and fixer who mentored Mr. Trump during his ascent through New York real estate — and who taught him never to back down, never to apologize and always to threaten opponents with multi-million-dollar lawsuits — was then dead for 31 years.”

Kai Bird goes on to write, quote, “Forty years after his mentor’s death, Mr. Trump found his Roy Cohn. The similarities are almost spooky.”

AMY GOODMAN: Kai Bird joins us now from Washington, D.C., author of many books, including American Prometheus: The Triumph and Tragedy of J. Robert Oppenheimer, which won the Pulitzer Prize. He co-authored his new book on Roy Cohn with Susan Goldmark, his wife and chief researcher.

Kai Bird, welcome to Democracy Now! Thanks so much for being with us. So, the response of President Trump to the massive defeat in the midterm elections was to take Stephen Miller, already one of his top advisers, and make him chief of staff, the architect of the anti-immigrant crackdown. The title of your most recent piece in the Times is “It’s Roy Cohn All Over Again,” starting with Donald Trump crying out, “Where’s my Roy Cohn?” Take it from there.

This is a great moment in history. It’s very troubling, though, that he has chosen someone like Stephen Miller, who resembles, in a spooky way, Roy Cohn, the man who mentored the young Donald Trump and taught him — you know, taught him his politics, taught him how to deal with the press, the courts, and to never back down, to never apologize and to engage in lie after lie, the big lie. ANJALI KAMAT: And, Kai Bird, you talk about loyalty. KAI BIRD: Yes. Well, Donald Trump has always, you know, valued loyalty above everything else. This is something that Roy Cohn gave him. You know, Roy was a Mafia lawyer. He represented all five mob families in New York City in the 1970s. And, you know, he liked his mob clients, and he valued them because they were straight with their word and they were loyal. And he taught this kind of — this mantra to Trump, too. And that’s what matters to Donald Trump, is absolute loyalty.

And so, it’s extraordinary that Stephen Miller would go out in front of the press and make such a statement. It doesn’t bode well. I mean, I think we’re looking at two really rocky years ahead of us, with Trump wounded and cornered in the White House. But now he’s doubled down by appointing this attack dog who reminds us of Roy Cohn.

ANJALI KAMAT: Kai Bird, your book is titled American Scoundrel: Roy Cohn’s Dark Journey from Joe McCarthy to Donald Trump. And you write that “McCarthyism is Roy’s enduring legacy.” So, talk about what you mean. Take us from Joe McCarthy all the way to the present.

KAI BIRD: Yes, well, Roy was appointed chief counsel to Senator Joe McCarthy in 1953. He was only 27 years old, and he was McCarthy’s brains. And he became famous. He became a celebrity in the spring of ’54 during the Army-McCarthy hearings, and his image was splashed across the cover of Time magazine.

And, you know, when I first started studying McCarthyism 40 years ago in some of my previous books, you know, it was regarded by most historians as a low point in American history, but something that we had gotten past, that we had overcome. McCarthy was defeated. But it turns out, in retrospect, that McCarthyism was never defeated. Roy Cohn planted the seeds of a sort of politics of resentment and divisiveness, and it’s reared its head again and again during the Nixon era, during the Reagan era. And now in the last 10 years, we’ve had a vicious renewal of McCarthyism.

And it’s much worse than the period that — the witch hunts that we lived through in the early '50s. You know, there are many more victims. Thousands and thousands of civil servants have been fired. Our major institutions, Ivy League universities are bending the knee, major law firms, corporations. It's a witch hunt that has many more victims and is much more serious and consequential than what we endured during the McCarthy era.

So, there’s a straight line between Joe McCarthy and Donald Trump, and the connection is Roy Cohn. And it’s extraordinary that he, this Mafia lawyer, this lawyer for not only Joe McCarthy, but for all five mob families in New York City, then becomes the lawyer for Donald Trump for 13 years. And he really molds him and teaches him. And it was an enduring friendship, too. You know, you don’t think of Donald Trump as someone who’s having close, enduring friendships, but this is the one exception. He really admired Roy. They saw in each other narcissistic personalities, and they fed off of each other. And Donald Trump today, sitting in the Oval Office, he truly misses Roy Cohn.

So, it’s not really a surprise that, cornered and wounded by the recent election results, he reaches out and pulls up Stephen Miller, who’s very similar in sort of his willingness to go on the offensive and never back down, and to say outrageous things to try to change the narrative. So, again, I think we’re in for a really rough road in the next two years.

AMY GOODMAN: You also talk about what happens with Donald Trump when Roy Cohn is dying of AIDS and how he actually rejected him, dropped him, though he would then show up at the memorial, at the funeral.

And this was in March of ’86, just four months, five months before he died of AIDS in August.

And Donald Trump then turned up at his memorial service. He didn’t give a eulogy, but he sat in the back of the room. And according to Peter Manso, a journalist who knew both men well, Manso saw, witnessed Donald Trump quietly weeping, which is, again, an extraordinary thing to see human emotions from Donald Trump. But again, this, I think, underscores the deep-seated relationship between the two men and the influence that Roy Cohn had over the future president.

KAI BIRD: Yes, well, Roy Cohn was actually personally responsible for getting Irving Kaufman appointed as a judge, and he even played a role in getting Kaufman assigned to the Rosenberg case. And then, he was close friends with him, and he just broke all the rules and had ex parte conversations repeatedly with Judge Kaufman, encouraging him to not only convict the Rosenbergs, but also to hand down death sentences for both Julius and Ethel. And Ethel, there was really very thin evidence that she had materially done anything in the way of espionage. So, it’s a real tragedy and a crime. But Roy Cohn, for the rest of his life, went around telling people that he had no regrets and that if — had he been given the opportunity, he would have pulled the switch himself. It’s just an extraordinary story. He repeatedly perjured himself in many — in the years to come.

And I don’t think you can understand Donald Trump without understanding Roy Cohn. Donald Trump is sort of an ephemeral character. Journalists and biographers try to capture who he is, but he’s a con artist. But if you read the biography of Roy Cohn, you suddenly — something clicks, and you suddenly get an insight into the soul of Donald Trump. It’s an extraordinary story.

KAI BIRD: Yes, absolutely. McCarthyism lives on, and you can hear it in the rhetoric of Stephen Miller and Donald Trump when they talk about radical leftists and communists. Well, that’s ridiculous. You know, the Communist Party was not a threat in the early '50s. It was a minuscule party then, maybe 10,000 party members, and half of whom were FBI informants. But, you know, it's very sad.

This is a great country in many ways, a country built on immigration and a country devoted to a free press and multiculturalism. And yet every few decades or generation or so, we go through a period of paranoia, when scoundrels like Roy Cohn and Stephen Miller rise up and push the buttons of fear and question the latest wave of immigrants and question the free press. And it’s very dangerous. It’s very worrisome. It sort of represents — they represent the dark side of American history.

## “Birds of War”: New Film Tells a Story of Love and Journalism from Syria to Lebanon

ABD ALKADER HABAK: Yeah, I mean, like for us, when we were, like, covering the war in Syria, it was like super important for us, like, to deliver a story, no matter what. ABD ALKADER HABAK: Yeah, of course, especially, like, we are operating, like, in a war zone, like in frontline. And that, for us, we were — like, when we were there, like, we don’t think about this. Janay spent 10 years inside the BBC, watching from the newsroom side what gets kept, what gets cut, and whose name goes at the top of the story.” So, talk about that, how you met. JANAY BOULOS: Yes, yeah. And it was quite competitive, like it is in the newsroom. So we always wanted more footage. Every hour, there’s a headline. We wanted new footage. We wanted new headlines. And obviously, when Habak and other local journalists were filming for me, I was getting the footage. I was cutting the report the way editorially fit the agenda. And this sort of extractive model that, “Yeah, now we want footage, now we want the news,” and then the next day we move on, really created sort of a dilemma for me as a journalist, and it led me to question what is it that I want to do and what is important for me.

ANJALI KAMAT: Habak, you eventually did leave Syria, in 2017, and you live in London now. What did it feel like to witness the fall of the Assad regime all these years later, in 2024? ABD ALKADER HABAK: I mean, like, witnessing the fall of the Assad regime on the TV, it was something — I mean, it was something really good, but at the same time, I was, like, super angry, because at the same time, angry, sad, as like — it’s like, you know, a lot of feeling at the same time, because in my mind, like, I never, ever, ever imagined, like, I’m going to witness that, like, on TV, far away. It’s like, in my mind, like, I should be in the streets with the people.

And what makes me more angry, I went to Turkey like a month before the Assad regime fell, and I wanted to cross the border to Syria. But, unfortunately, I spent a month there trying to cross the border, but then, like, the Turkish authority, like, didn’t allow me because I have a British passport at that time. So, yeah, it was, like, really unfair on the people, like, who was living abroad, like, to not witness this on the ground, actually.
