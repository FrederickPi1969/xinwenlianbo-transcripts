Under the 1951 Defense of Greenland Treaty, the United States already has wide latitude to station forces and build military bases in Greenland. The U.S. currently has one active military base on Greenland, about 160 troops on the ground. During the height of the Cold War, the U.S. maintained 17 military installations in Greenland with over 10,000 troops. Under the new deal, the U.S. would be allowed to build two new bases.

The new agreement, signed shortly after President Trump’s speech to the U.N. Describing as, quote, “a deal that could last forever,” the Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen praised the deal as strengthening NATO and staving off the growing presence of Russia and China in the Arctic.

PRIME MINISTER METTE FREDERIKSEN: This deal is good for the United States. MUHAMMAD SHEHADA: Thanks so much, Amy. Great to finally see you in person, one of my favorite heroes.

So, the idea of the plan was to divide the reconstruction, rehabilitation, Israeli withdrawal, decommissioning of Hamas, and Palestinian statehood into three phases. The first one was related to early recovery, so it would see Israel suspending its attacks, allowing 600 trucks per day of food and humanitarian aid into Gaza, rehabilitating basic infrastructure — water, electricity, hospitals, roads, sanitation — and basically opening up the Rafah border crossing for people to be able to travel in and out. The second phase was the one that relates to Hamas decommissioning and Israeli withdrawal from Gaza, as well as the reconstruction, the rebuilding of the enclave. So far, only one item of the 20 items on the Trump plan has been fulfilled, and that is the release of every single Israeli hostage in Gaza and the remains of dead hostages. Israel either violated its obligations completely or fulfilled some of them extremely partially, to the point that it does not have any significant impact on the ground. AMY GOODMAN: Why did Palestinians sign this deal? You’re a Palestinian from Gaza.

MUHAMMAD SHEHADA: I’ve asked Palestinian negotiators and Arab mediators, as well, what was the highest expectation behind the deal, and the answer is unanimous. The other dimension is that they were blackmailed very openly by the Trump administration, that if they don’t sign onto that deal before the Nobel Peace Prize was announced on Friday in that October 2025, that Netanyahu would get a total free hand to decimate Gaza and annihilate it. MUHAMMAD SHEHADA: Precisely, but he managed to steal it anyways from Machado.

MUHAMMAD SHEHADA: Well, Netanyahu is doing two things. Number one, he is using Gaza, the destruction there, the settler terrorism that we see in the West Bank, as his election campaign. So, he’s promising the Israeli public that if he’s reelected, he will finish the job in Gaza. We’re seeing him, his Minister of Defense Israel Katz, his Minister of Finance Bezalel Smotrich, all of them promising, one after the other, that Israel’s plan for Gaza remains resuming military operations back to full scale, doing even more destruction than what we have seen before, because now Israel doesn’t have any hostages in there, so they can accelerate the damage and destruction exponentially, and basically ethnically cleansing Gaza completely and rebuilding Jewish-only settlements in the enclave. So, that is number one.

The other thing is that Netanyahu is trying to show that he’s stronger than the United States and the international community, that he cannot be pressured by Trump. So, when the Trump plan was announced and the Trump roadmap was then announced this year, I think in July, when Hamas accepted to disarm, lay down its arms, in return for Israel’s withdrawal, Netanyahu used it for his theatrics to pretend to be stronger than Trump, to pretend to be the Winston Churchill of Israel, that he can stand on his own against a storm and not bend at all. And one of the reasons for this is because opposition parties in Israel are attacking Netanyahu from the right, not from the left. So, Gadi Eisenkot, the next premier in Israel, supposedly, the one with the highest chance of winning and becoming prime minister, has been telling the Israeli public that Netanyahu will, quote, “squander” and waste away the achievements, the military achievements, of the IDF in Gaza and capitulate to Trump and fulfill his roadmap. So, Netanyahu is stuck in the position of pretending that, “No, I am actually stronger than Trump, and I can prevent any progress.”

From our perspective, it’s basically we see that he would revitalize the Palestinian street. He would reestablish the connection between the leadership and the public and energize our movement again. And that’s precisely why Netanyahu told John Kerry in 2013 that he would never release him, because he’s the only one that can unify Palestinians.

Releasing him at this stage is the single most consequential event in reviving Palestinian politics, that has been long murdered by a veto from the United States, from Israel, from the international community, that heavily invested in maintaining the divide between Gaza and the West Bank, Hamas and Fatah, the diaspora and Palestinians in the occupied territory. I saw Ro Khanna already advocating for his release, and that is the right thing to do at this stage.

AMY GOODMAN: Finally, NAZA has just been released, the documentary by Yuval [Abraham] and Rachel Szor, a film that portrays the Israeli military’s use of secret AI-powered systems to deliberately and systematically target and kill civilians in Gaza, received this record ovation at the Venice Film Festival, exposing Where’s Daddy? and Lavender, something Yuval has been reporting, and others, Palestinians and Israelis, have been talking about, but all in this film — Yuval Abraham. MUHAMMAD SHEHADA: So, the movie NAZA, it perfectly captures the distinction between disproportionate warfare, in which you are attacking an enemy and then civilians in high numbers are getting killed, and genocide, in which the civilian population is the target. If you watch the movie, you can’t help but come out with the impression that Hamas, in that instance, Hamas militants that were being targeted, were basically the excuse or the alibi or even the collateral damage, that you see Israeli soldiers that are saying that they were authorized to drop a bomb that would kill 500 civilians, most of whom are children and women, to get to one junior Hamas militant that even may or may not be there in the first place. And the idea is to use that militant as an excuse for maximum damage. That’s why we saw the chief of staff of the IDF at the start of the war, Herzi Halevi, saying that “the focus for us is on the damage, not on the accuracy.” That’s why we’re seeing Israeli politicians and military generals and commanders throughout the genocide that were telling their soldiers explicitly, “Destroy everything. Don’t leave anything standing. Maximum destruction.”

I even met Israeli soldiers here in Copenhagen. A colleague of mine brought them here to testify before European decision-makers and politicians. And when I met them, their testimony was very simple. The problem is that most decision-makers refused to even meet with those soldiers to hear the testimony, because they wanted to maintain plausible deniability.
