## Stanford Student Newspaper Wins Suit over Trump’s Crackdown on Pro-Palestine Speech

“What we were hearing from the noncitizens in our newsroom and from noncitizens across campus — current students, former students, alumni — was that they were genuinely frightened because of what they saw in the headlines, what they saw happen to Rümeysa Öztürk,” says George Porteous, editor-in-chief of The Stanford Daily. Rümeysa Öztürk is the former Tufts University graduate student arrested and detained by ICE after co-authoring an op-ed encouraging Tufts to divest from Israel in her university’s student newspaper.

“The chill is the point,” adds Will Creeley, legal director at the Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression, or FIRE, the organization that represented The Stanford Daily in court. “That’s a First Amendment violation every day of the week. It’s flatly un-American.”

AMY GOODMAN: A federal judge has ruled the Trump administration has violated the constitutional rights of international students in seeking to deport them for their pro-Palestinian activism and criticism of Israel. In her ruling, U.S. District Judge Noël Wise wrote, quote, “In the United States, free speech, including the freedom to criticize the government and its leaders, is not a sign of our democracy’s fragility. It is evidence of its strength. That strength is diminished when members of our society — citizens and noncitizens alike — must self-censor and 'behave' or suffer the government’s retaliation,” she said.

The lawsuit was filed by The Stanford Daily, the student newspaper at Stanford University, which claimed some of its writers on student visas declined to cover pro-Palestinian protests or report on topics related to Israel, fearing deportation. Student journalists also requested their published articles on such topics be removed from the paper’s website.

The case raises broader questions about the climate of fear and political repression facing international students during the Trump administration. One of the most striking examples is Rümeysa Öztürk, the Turkish doctoral student at Tufts University. Last year, masked plainclothes immigration agents snatched Öztürk off the streets of Somerville, Massachusetts. A year earlier, she had co-written an opinion piece for the Tufts student newspaper, The Tufts Daily, critical of the university’s response to campus protests over Gaza and the school’s failure to uphold student Senate resolutions on Palestine. Tufts University later stated the op-ed did not violate university policy, that no complaints had been filed about it, and that Rümeysa Öztürk had been in good academic and immigration standing. Nonetheless, her student visa was canceled, and she spent more than six weeks in an ICE jail in Louisiana. An immigration court eventually ruled in Rümeysa’s favor, saying the government failed to meet its burden of proof to deport her.

However, the message from the Trump administration to international students across the nation remained clear: Their ordinarily protected free political expression could now potentially cost them their immigration status and their freedom.

Well, for more, we’re joined by two guests who successfully challenged the Trump administration in court. Here in New York City, George Porteous is with us, editor-in-chief of The Stanford Daily. And in Moorestown, New Jersey, Will Creeley, legal director of FIRE, the Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression, which represented The Stanford Daily in court.

GEORGE PORTEOUS: Well, I think what it’s shown me is that it really has to begin locally, because we were dealing with a problem inside of our newsroom with our peers, and this was about solidarity, and also just the practical need to continue doing our jobs and to continue reporting the news. So, it’s shown me that it really does begin with everyday people. It begins with students, too. Students, I think, have enormous power to stand up to what this administration has done in terms of civil liberties and constitutional rights. And we have real power, is what this ruling has shown us.

GEORGE PORTEOUS: You know, it’s still, I think, too early to see. But I’m hopeful that this ruling, because it’s a very strong legal precedent, will carve out more room for noncitizens to feel that they can participate in democracy on campus.

AMY GOODMAN: Will Creeley, you are legal director at FIRE, the Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression. Talk about why you approached The Stanford Daily, why this is such a significant decision.

With this administration, again and again, the chill is the point. These actions that they’re taking, these wanton violations of the First Amendment, are designed to get folks like George and his colleagues at The Stanford Daily to feel scared, to think twice, to ask that their names not appear on news reporting or opinion pieces. ## “They Got Zero Notice”: 2,500 Workers Laid Off as Tyson Foods Shutters Meatpacking Plant in Illinois

We speak to Caitlyn Clark, director of the group Essential Workers for Democracy, which is working to institute democratic reforms within the United Food and Commercial Workers International Union (UFCW), a labor union that represents more than a million workers across the United States. These workers include the former Tyson plant meatpackers, who worked in “some of the most dangerous jobs in America” while making “billions of dollars in profit for Tyson.” Clark ascribes the U.S. cattle shortage to climate change-induced drought in the American West, rising prices for diesel and fertilizer amid the U.S. war on Iran and years of corporate consolidation that has driven down profits for cattle ranchers.

Since they were suddenly laid off, former Joslin plant workers have been protesting outside the shuttered plant, calling on Tyson “to extend their severance pay, and calling on Illinois Governor JB Pritzker to intervene to help keep the plant open,” Clark tells us. “We need to take on the bosses and be serious about the level of inequality and exploitation that working people are facing today,” she adds. “Working people need more, they deserve more, and they’re ready to fight for it. It’s time that our union leadership catch up.”

AMY GOODMAN: This is Democracy Now!, democracynow.org. I’m Amy Goodman, with Juan González.

As we head into this Labor Day weekend, we turn now to the abrupt closure of a Tyson Foods beef processing plant in Joslin, Illinois. Twenty-five hundred workers lost their jobs with almost no notice. Many had worked at the plant for decades. The plant closed August 14th. The United Food and Commercial Workers International Union, which represents more than 2,000 of the workers, said they received no advance notice from the company.

On Thursday, dozens of laid-off workers protested at the Illinois state Capitol in Springfield, urging Illinois Governor JB Pritzker to reopen the plant under state ownership and provide relief to all affected workers.

Tyson Foods also shuttered a facility in Utah, citing, quote, “historic cattle shortages” as the reason for the closures. The 75-year low of the U.S. cattle herd is due in part to drought, the rising cost of fertilizer and diesel due to the U.S. war in Iran, and years of industry consolidation.

Workers held a rally in Joslin soon after the mass layoffs. They demanded Tyson increase their severance pay to at least six months of full compensation with benefits.

Tyson Foods CEO and President Donnie King made over $34 million last year, nearly 800 times more than the median Tyson worker and double the average CEO-to-worker pay ratio among S&P, Standard & Poor’s, 500 companies.

This is a clip of laid-off Tyson worker Ahoro Asselissime, who spent 11 years at the plant. He sent the message yesterday.

AHORO ASSELISSIME: This closing make me a lot of stress. A lot of stress. Every day I have to think about it. I don’t sleep. Every day I have to apply the job. … It is difficult to get a job in Quad City here. It is very, very difficult. We lose our friends. Our income go down.

And I want to talk. Eleven years at Tyson is not easy. One year at Tyson is not easy. Two weeks at Tyson is not easy. I think about our grandma, our daddies, who spent 26 years, 30 years, 35 years at Tyson. They spent all their life over there. Where? What company can take them? … Our governor, please, we want our job back.

AMY GOODMAN: To talk more about this, we’re joined by Caitlyn Clark, director at Essential Workers for Democracy, her latest piece in Labor Notes headlined “'Treated Worse than Cattle': 2,500 Tyson Workers Laid Off, Plant Shutters Overnight.” She’s joining us here in New York.

Talk about what this means, 2,500 people laid off at once. Many worked at this plant for decades.

CAITLYN CLARK: Yeah, so, 2,500 workers were laid off virtually overnight. They got zero notice to the workers, to their union, their families or their community. Many of these workers, as you mentioned and as Ahoro mentioned, have worked at Tyson for 10, 20, some 30 years, doing some of the most dangerous jobs in America. Some have sustained cuts, chemical burns, repeated motion injuries. And through these years, they’ve made billions of dollars in profit for Tyson, which is one of the biggest meatpacking companies in the world. They’re part of what we call the Big Four meatpackers. They own 85% of all beef production in the United States.

And the Big Four packers have played a huge role in the cattle shortage that we’re seeing today. So, Tyson has credited the cattle shortage for the reason for the closure. But, you know, what we’re seeing is, while there are cattle shortages due to climate change-induced droughts and the increased price of diesel and fertilizer due to the war on Iran, we’ve also seen a yearslong corporate consolidation in the meatpacking industry that’s led to alleged collusion between the Big Four packers to repress the prices that they pay to cattle producers for their livestock, in addition to repressing the wages that they pay to workers for their work in the meatpacking plants, and inflating the prices that they charge to consumers at the grocery store.

So, after all this, you know, these workers are now looking for new employment. Many of them are losing their health insurance. And because a vast majority of them are immigrant workers, many of them, even though they are eligible to receive unemployment benefits, are afraid to apply for those benefits. And so, these workers have been organizing, protesting, organizing rallies to call on Tyson to extend their severance pay, and calling on Illinois Governor JB Pritzker to intervene to help keep the plant open.

JUAN GONZÁLEZ: And, Caitlyn, isn’t the — isn’t the shutdown illegal? There’s a WARN requirement for employers of a minimum number of days that they must notify workers before shutting down.

CAITLYN CLARK: Yes. So, that’s been under debate. The company has claimed that they will continue to pay workers severance pay until October, which would keep them in compliance with the WARN Act. But workers like Ahoro are receiving roughly half of their previous paychecks. So, you know, paychecks that used to be $900 or $1,000 are now much closer to $400 or $500 a week. So, that payment will continue for two months, until October, which the company is claiming will keep them in compliance with the WARN Act.

On top of that, they have made a plant closure agreement with the union that will give the workers about one to four weeks of additional severance, depending on how long they worked there. But for workers who worked more than 10 years at Tyson, they’re only receiving four weeks of severance on top of the federally mandated severance pay that they’re receiving due to the WARN Act.

JUAN GONZÁLEZ: You mentioned the union agreement. What has been the role of the union in this situation? And have they had meetings with the workers before signing such agreements?

CAITLYN CLARK: Well, unfortunately, what we’ve heard from many workers on the ground is that they are disappointed with their local union’s signing of an agreement with Tyson that basically said the union is not going to be fighting the closure of the plant, which has led many workers to independent worker action and organizing to hold their own rallies and their own protest actions to call on Tyson to do better for the workers and to increase their severance pay. You know, this is, unfortunately, a very common reality for many members of the United Food and Commercial Workers Union. It’s a 1.2 million-member union that represents hundreds of thousands of meatpacking workers and retail grocery workers across the country who believe that they deserve, you know, a fighting union where members are in charge.

That’s why we formed Essential Workers for Democracy. It’s a nationwide network of rank-and-file UFCW members who are fighting to build a stronger and a more democratic union from the bottom up, primarily through the right to vote for a top union leadership. We’ve seen what this looks like in other unions, like the United Auto Workers, that won the right to vote just a few years ago, elected new leadership, and then went on strike against the Big Three automakers in 2023. Part of those contract negotiations were fighting companies like Stellantis to reopen formerly closed plants. Now, that fight is still ongoing, but it demonstrates that rank-and-file union members and workers across the country are hungry to take on these companies for who they really are, that they don’t need, you know, partnerships with management, that we need to take on the bosses and be serious about the level of inequality and exploitation that working people are facing today.

AMY GOODMAN: And the message of the workers, in this last 30 seconds, who were rallying in Joslin, in Springfield, as we move into Labor Day?

CAITLYN CLARK: Mm-hmm. As we’re going into Labor Day, I think it’s an important reminder that working people in this country have never had their rights or their dignity handed to them by the bosses. It has to be organized for, it has to be fought for, and it can be won. When we look at labor history, we see that the eight-hour workday, the weekend, the 40-hour workweek, those are all due to the power of the labor movement, due to solidarity and strikes. And working people need more, they deserve more, and they’re ready to fight for it. It’s time that our union leadership catch up with them.

AMY GOODMAN: Caitlyn Clark, director at Essential Workers for Democracy. We’ll link to your article, “'Treated Worse than Cattle': 2,500 Tyson Workers Laid Off, Plant Shutters Overnight.”

## “Top of the World”: Remembering the Restaurant Workers Killed Atop World Trade Center in 9/11 Attack

We’re joined by Top of the World's co-directors, Julie Cohen and Betsy West, and by Sekou Siby, a former line cook at Windows on the World. Siby, who immigrated to the United States from the Ivory Coast in 1996, was originally scheduled to work on the morning of September 11, but a co-worker asked him to switch shifts. “He didn't make it,” says Siby, who later became an organizer and longtime leader at the Restaurant Opportunities Center (ROC), an advocacy group founded by Windows on the World workers to support each other in the aftermath of the attack.

“The extraordinary immigrant workforce of Windows [of the World] and what they collectively created is just a reminder of the contributions of immigrant New Yorkers to the restaurant world and to the culture of the city,” says Cohen. “We want people to remember not just the tragedy, but the real beauty and the specific stories of what was lost on that day.”
