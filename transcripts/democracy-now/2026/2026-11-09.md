## “A Crackdown on All Independent Voices”: Egypt Shutters News Outlets, Prosecutes Editor Lina Attalah

“Putting us in jail is not going to solve the country’s problems,” Attalah told Democracy Now! in an interview Friday. “We need to be operating as journalists in our newsrooms as opposed to being defendants in courtrooms.”

Attalah says she and Mada Masr will appeal the ruling to shut them down, hoping to “use this crisis” as a way to fight censorship in Egypt and expand the space for independent reporting.

AMY GOODMAN: This is Democracy Now!, democracynow.org, The War and Peace Report. I’m Amy Goodman.

We turn now to the escalating crackdown on press freedom in Egypt. Lina Attalah, the editor-in-chief of Egypt’s leading independent media outlet Mada Masr, faces a criminal trial Wednesday on charges of, quote-unquote, “spreading false information.” Depending on the outcome, she could be imprisoned for a year. The criminal trial comes just two weeks after an Egyptian court convicted Attalah of managing an unlicensed website. She was detained for several hours, fined $50,000, Mada Masr ordered closed.

Mada Masr was founded in June 2013, a year before a military coup led to President Abdel Fattah el-Sisi taking office. Mada publishes in Arabic and English, is internationally recognized for its investigative reporting and editorial independence. It’s continued working over the past 13 years despite repeated website blocking, office raids, staff arrests and judicial harassment.

The attack on Mada Masr follows a crackdown on another independent Egyptian media outlet, Matsadaash, where authorities arrested the entire newsroom, accused them of belonging to the banned Muslim Brotherhood, and forced them to record false confessions under torture.

Over a hundred groups, including Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch and the Committee to Protect Journalists and Reporters Without Borders, have condemned the crackdown on press freedom and called on Egyptian authorities to drop the charges against Lina Attalah, release the Matsadaash journalists and allow independent media to operate freely.

On Friday, Democracy Now!’s Anjali Kamat and I spoke to Lina Attalah. We asked her about the charges she faces and her upcoming trial Wednesday.

LINA ATTALAH: Two weeks ago, I was summoned by the prosecutor for interrogations on accusations of running a website without a license. It is not unusual to be faced with this allegation, even though we’ve been going through the bureaucratic motions of trying to apply for a license and have simply been not getting any response from the licensing authorities.

The new thing that happened two weeks ago is that our case was swiftly referred to trial, and last week we had the first and the very quick verdict already pronounced in this first session. So, Mada Masr was ordered closed. We were fined over $50,000. And I was briefly detained until the fine was collected, which is, again, unusual practice.

The entire trial saw a lot of violations that we’ve been talking about, including the judge refusing all our defense team’s demands. The trial ended with the judge even appointing a lawyer on my behalf. And upon my objection, he already proceeded to pronouncing the verdict that basically closes Mada and charges us a fine of over $50,000, all while I was pulled violently by the cops to a detention spot until the fine was collected.

Upon my release that day, after a very long day, after everybody showed up in solidarity to help us pay the fine, I learned that I’m facing another — a trial, where there could be a charge of one year in prison. So, it does feel like the most violent legal campaign against us in the last years, where we faced other forms of challenges, legal challenges to our work, but we’ve never really reached that level before.

ANJALI KAMAT: And, Lina, are you going to have to shut down Mada Masr? How are you fighting these charges against you and this publication that you co-founded in 2013?

LINA ATTALAH: We are doing everything possible to challenge this verdict. I do not think it’s fair for a media that has served this country for over 13 years, doing what has become very scarce in the country, reporting bravely and independently about everything that’s been happening, not only in Egypt, but also in the region. I do not believe it can be shut down through a court verdict that did not last over an hour, or a court session that did not last over an hour.

So, our plan is to appeal. Our plan is to also try to apply for a license again. And our plan is to use this crisis, as has been our habit, to open up the conversation about how licensing cannot be a tool for censorship and control. Licensing can be a regulating mechanism, but it can be — it cannot be used to shut down independent media in the country. And this should be resolved not only for us, but also for all other media outlets that are trying to work independently in the country.

AMY GOODMAN: Can you tell us about what’s happened with Matsadaash, with the journalists there? Where are they right now? The entire newsroom was arrested?

LINA ATTALAH: An entire newsroom was arrested right before our own ordeal began, which, you know, easily signals how this is a crackdown on all independent voices. And that’s why the sentiment right now is that we’re not just fighting for Mada, but we’re fighting for that very space in Egypt today.

Matsadaash is a fact-checking platform. The newsroom is made up for six brilliant young journalists. I’ve worked personally with four of them as they were starting in their careers. The youngest of them is 21 years old. They are all young, promising journalists who dared to do this work with bravery, courage and curiosity.

They have been arrested over about a month ago, and they remain in remand detention. They are facing terror charges for doing their work. We are still hoping that their ordeal ends, as ours, too. We are hoping for them to be released from detention. They are in detention facilities in Egypt, again, like I said, remand detention. So, we are hoping for this crackdown to stop here and for the authorities to wake up to the fact that the young men of Matsadaash are not their problems, Mada Masr is not their problems. Putting us in jail is not going to solve the country’s problems. And the wise thing to do is to drop the charges against us and against them, and to release them right away.

ANJALI KAMAT: And, Lina, can you talk about the timing of this escalating crackdown on the press? This is coming as, you know, in the middle of this crackdown, we saw a surprise announcement from the Egyptian President Abdel Fattah el-Sisi that he would be stepping down in 2030, at the end of his third and current term. Never in Egypt’s modern history has the head of state chosen to hand over power voluntarily. What does this mean for the future of Egypt and the space for dissent?

LINA ATTALAH: It is a very charged moment in the country, politically and economically. Politically, we’re not just talking about what’s happening on the local level; we’re also talking about regional challenges with surrounding conflicts, the Red Sea conflict, the war in Iran, and Egypt is not isolated from that. It is — it is a pressuring situation on the country and on the authorities here.

At the same time, the announcements that you mentioned point to a certain shift that might be happening, that, unfortunately, we couldn’t get a chance to report on properly because we’ve been having to face all these legal ordeals ever since the announcements were made. But we do have the sense that something in the mode of rule is shifting. And that’s why we need to be operating as journalists in our newsrooms, as opposed to being defendants in courtrooms, to understand what is happening.

And then, you know, the other context in which this crackdown is happening is a major economic challenge. You know, Egypt has been heavily integrated in the global economy, especially after 2016. So it’s been heavily affected with global shifts, market shifts, having become, having turned into so-called an emerging market and being quite dependent on hot money. We have major debt service to commit to. We have a gas bill to pay. So the country is also going through an important economic challenge that is getting more and more pronounced on a day-to-day level. So, I would say this is the political and economic context under which these decisions against us are being made, and it’s important to note them.
