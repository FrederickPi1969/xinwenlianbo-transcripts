## Purple Mississippi? Overcoming GOP Voter Suppression, Black Voters Power Dem Wins Across the South

“The entire country shifted left. But when you look at it regionally, the biggest shifts took place in the South — Texas, Mississippi, South Carolina, Louisiana,” says Cliff Albright, executive director of Black Voters Matter. That “wave of energy” was “largely led by Black voters who were most under attack with the mid-decade racial gerrymandering that was taking place. It clearly backfired, and all it did was to wake up a sleeping giant.”

“Without the extreme gerrymandering, without the extreme voter restrictions that have been seen throughout the South, [the] wave would have been even bigger,” adds Chris Kromm, executive director of the Institute for Southern Studies. “There’s a real opportunity for engaging more voters and really pushing the region in a more progressive direction.”

AMY GOODMAN: We begin today’s show in the Deep South, where Mississippi’s Senate race remains too close to call. Democrat Scott Colom is narrowly leading Republican incumbent Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by more than 1,800 votes as election officials process provisional ballots and late-arriving mail ballots. Just two years ago, Donald Trump carried Mississippi by 23 points. If elected, Scott Colom would be the first Democrat to win the state in 40 years and the first African American since 1875, during Reconstruction.

Colom made the issue of affordability central to his campaign, and in an ad that went viral, he blasted Senator Hyde-Smith for saying Mississippians should, quote, “pick another protein,” unquote, if they’re upset over high beef prices. The ad features Mississippians saying they’d rather pick another senator.

SEN. CINDY HYDE-SMITH: You know, you have so many proteins to choose from.

MISSISSIPPI VOTER 1: Pick another protein? [bleep], I’d rather pick another senator instead.

AMY GOODMAN: Scott Colom is a former district attorney for northeast Mississippi. In 2022, President Biden nominated him to the federal bench, a nomination blocked by Colom’s opponent in this race, incumbent Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith.

Colom has sharply criticized national Democrats for writing off Mississippi and failing to invest in his campaign. Early on, he told reporters, quote, “Mississippi was never a lost cause. We were a betrayed cause.” This is Colom speaking to NBC News.

SCOTT COLOM: The reality is our country does not do well when it ignores places like Mississippi. … You can’t lead with doubt. You have to believe. You have to lead with belief, and that’s where the power is. When you believe things, you can actually achieve it. And there’s some people in the party that they are just treading water. Right? “What’s the path to least resistance to get to 51? Oh my gosh, we did it.” That’s not the right way to think. In this particular moment, you have to think about how can we win everywhere, because we need to represent working people in a way where we actually deliver.

AMY GOODMAN: The potential electoral upset in Mississippi is part of a much broader political shift across the South, where Democrats have scored major victories in states long considered Republican strongholds.

For the latest on Mississippi and more on the sea change in the South, we’re joined by two guests. In Durham, North Carolina, we’re joined by Chris Kromm, the executive director of the Institute for Southern Studies, publisher of its online magazine Facing South. ## National Politics Will Never Be the Same: Juan González on How Latino Voters Remade the Political Map

“Texas has been suddenly transformed into a battleground state,” says Democracy Now! co-host Juan Gonzalez. “Texas has been the biggest and most important red state in the country for decades, and I’ve been predicting for years that it is the key link for achieving a realignment of national politics.”

AMY GOODMAN: This is Democracy Now!, democracynow.org. I’m Amy Goodman, with Juan González.

The midterms saw a landslide of Latino voters towards Democrats in state after state. In Texas, California, Kansas and Colorado, Latinos who had voted for Republicans just two years ago swung to Democrats in huge numbers, helping secure Democratic control of both the House and the Senate.

Latino lawmakers will make up about a fifth of the Democratic majority in the House. And in historic victories, the two most populous states in the country, California and Texas, will soon be led by Latinx Democratic governors. On Tuesday night, Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa defeated the incumbent Republican Governor Greg Abbott with a margin of over 700,000 votes. This is a clip from her speech.

GOV.-ELECT GINA HINOJOSA: I am a daughter of the Rio Grande Valley, where the people embody the work ethic and values of our great state, where no one would have imagined that one day a little girl from the RGV would stand before you here as the next governor of the greatest state, Texas.

AMY GOODMAN: That was Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa speaking in Austin, Texas, right after she was declared the winner.

And this is California Governor-elect Xavier Becerra addressing supporters Tuesday night in Sacramento after he easily defeated Republican challenger Steve Hilton.

GOV.-ELECT XAVIER BECERRA: Ladies and gentlemen, you have now entered the Becerra era. Tonight, I have a message for all those who have been made to feel afraid. There’s a new sheriff in town, and he’s got a law degree and a union card. If anyone wants to come after California or any one Californian, they better be ready for a fight.

AMY GOODMAN: For more on the significance of these victories, we turn to Juan González, yes, co-host of Democracy Now!, author of, among other books, Harvest of Empire: A History of Latinos in America.

Juan, can you talk about this massive Latino victory across the country? What was expected? What wasn’t expected?

JUAN GONZÁLEZ: Yeah, well, Amy, it’s been quite a week in U.S. politics, and I’ve been poring over the voting returns and the exit polls the past two days to glean a clearer sense of the trends and outcomes that maybe the major media may have missed, and not just about Latinos.

Let’s begin with the turnout, which I believe is always key. It tells you how motivated and mobilized people were. Well, nationally, the turnout was 63% of registered voters. Now, that’s high by historic standards for a midterm, because, remember, it’s always less voting in the midterms. But it’s still a lower rate than either the Biden midterm election of 2022 or the first Trump midterm elections of 2018. So, while some areas of the country may have had a historic turnout, across the country this was not the highest turnout of any midterm in recent years.

And the most notable shift occurred among white voters. They made up 73% of all ballots cast. That’s the same percentage that they made in 2022. But while they voted 58% for Republicans four years ago — and I’m comparing it midterm to midterm, not midterm to the presidential election — this time they split almost evenly between Republicans and Democrats. So you’re talking a huge shift among white voters away from the Republican Party and to the Democratic Party.

What about people of color, who represented 23% of the electorate? The turnout rate for Blacks and Latinos nationwide stayed about even, or even possibly dropped a little bit, compared to 2022. And both groups, though, shifted much more to Democrats than previously. Black voters nationwide went from about 86% to 90% this year, and Latinos moved from 60% Democratic to — it depends on who you look at. The national exit poll said it went to 64%. The New York Times voter trend says it went to 69%. But both groups shifted nationwide significantly toward Democrats.

But here’s the biggest surprise that no one has paid any attention to, is that what happened among Asian Americans. They not only doubled their share of the electorate — they went from 2% in 2022 to 4% in this year — but 71% of Asian Americans voted for Democrats. That’s a 13-point shift from what Asian Americans did in 2022. And I have to think that the wars in Gaza and Iran, the Trump administration’s banning of travel from many Asian and Middle Eastern countries, its constant belittling of that region’s countries and cultures, the deportation even of refugees from Afghanistan, all have dramatically transformed how Asian Americans view the two major political parties.

Now, there’s been a lot of attention to the Latino vote, and which I’ve been studying for decades, obviously, as you mentioned Harvest of Empire. But we have to begin with what I’ve always called the Brown Belt. That’s a vast area of six Western and Southwestern states where Latinos make up as much as 30% or more of the total population — talking about Texas, New Mexico, Arizona, Colorado, Nevada and California. And, of course, as you mentioned, in Texas and California, the first elected Latino governors in both of those states. But the most startling trends came at the congressional level.

Take northern Nevada, for example. Democrat and former social worker Teresa Benitez-Thompson defeated a Republican incumbent to become the first Democrat ever to win that state’s 2nd Congressional seat, a district that Trump won by 14 points in 2024. With her victory, all four of Nevada’s congressional districts are held — will be held by Democrats.

In southern Arizona, JoAnna Mendoza, a 20-year Navy and Marine Corps veteran, flipped another Republican seat. And meanwhile, an emergency room doctor, Dr. Amish Shah, a former state legislator, won the 1st Congressional District in another Republican seat with a large — a district with a large Latino population.

In Colorado, Democrat Manny Rutinel, a Latino attorney and former state legislator, defeated a Republican incumbent in a district in Denver’s northern suburbs with the highest percentage of Latino voters in the state.

Of course, Texas has been the biggest and most important red state in the country for decades, and I’ve been predicting for years that it is the key link for achieving a realignment of national politics. Remember those five [seats] that Trump ordered redrawn to elect more Republicans? Well, the voters didn’t cooperate. Democrats held on to four of those five seats. Henry Cuellar of Laredo and Vicente Gonzalez of McAllen, two moderate congressmen, prevailed in their reelections. Democrat Leticia Gutierrez rode a wave of anti-mass deportation and anti-ICE sentiment to defeat her Republican opponent in the redrawn 9th District outside of Houston. That’s the seat that Al Green, the longtime congressman, used to hold. And in the San Antonio area, former Deputy Sheriff Johnny Garcia won in the newly created 35th District. And at the same time, Republicans lost two other seats in Texas to Latino Democrats. In West Texas, former educator Katy Padilla Stout won a congressional district that had been in Republican hands for the past six elections. That’s followed the resignation of Representative Tony Gonzales in the wake of sexual misconduct and the ethics scandal. And in the lone Rio Grande Valley seat held by Republicans, the famed Grammy-winning Tejano singer Bobby Pulido, who describes himself as a conservative Democrat but favors immigration reform, he defeated Republican Monica De La Cruz, who was thought to be a rising Republican star.

Now, here’s another surprise that no one’s paid attention to, maybe the biggest Texas bright spot that has escaped notice. I’m talking about the remarkable performance by Greg Casar, the chairman of the House Progressive Caucus. He’s also a democratic socialist and someone we have interviewed on Democracy Now! numerous times. He ran for reelection, but to a seat formerly held by Congressman Lloyd Doggett, after Republicans moved Casar’s hometown of Austin out of his district and into this reconfigured district, and Doggett agreed to retire. Well, Casar won that race as expected, but he did so in a super landslide. He amassed over 254,000 votes. That’s 70,000 more votes than any other congressional candidate got in Texas this year — for a socialist in Texas. By my calculations, Casar received the fifth-highest vote in the U.S. out of all of the candidates for the 435 House seats.

The bottom line is that Texas has been suddenly transformed into a battleground state. Republicans not only lost the governor’s seat, a U.S. Senate seat, other statewide races, several state House and Senate seats, but instead of winning five new congressional seats, they ended up with a net loss of one. If Democrats are able to consolidate some of those Texas gains in the coming years, national politics will never be the same. That’s why progressive organizers and fundraisers should be looking at Texas, like the civil rights movement of the 1950s regarded Mississippi and Georgia, as the place to send resources and train new leaders for the upcoming 2028 election.

Meanwhile, over in California, all five Republican districts that Democrats redrew ended up flipping to Democrats. Two of those five went to Latinos. Randy Villegas, a progressive community college professor, won a seat in the Central Valley in the 6th District, and Linda Sánchez prevailed in Orange County’s redrawn district.

And it’s not just the Brown Belt where Latinos had an impact. In Pennsylvania, where Democrats flipped four Republican seats, 73% of the state’s Latinos voted for Democrats, according to The New York Times. That’s a 26-point shift from the 2024 election. And it almost certainly fueled Democratic victories in two of those districts, where Latinos make up 15 to 25% of the population. That’s the 8th District, around Scranton, where the city’s Democratic Mayor Paige Cognetti won, and the 7th District, around Allentown, Bethlehem, where retired firefighter Bob Brooks ousted the Republican incumbent.

So, Democrats controlling Congress, however, does not offer any certainty that a new era of progressive government is dawning in Washington. We shouldn’t forget, for example, that South Texas Democrats have historically been more conservative than those in other parts of the country. So it remains to be seen how those Texas victories will ultimately affect legislation in the new Congress.

In addition, the country’s billionaire class will doubtless ramp up its torrent of cash donations directed at the new Democratic majority, hoping to buy off enough of them or foment sufficient infighting among them to kill or stall any substantive efforts at truly progressive legislation.

So much damage has already been perpetrated on American society by the lawlessness and corruption of the Trump era that it will take who knows how long to repair. To do so, progressives will need to contend not only with their — with the billionaire class and the corporate Democrats, who never tire of conspiring to control things, with Trump for the next two years and his MAGA faithful; they must also contend with their own ultra-left activists, who will increasingly demand no compromise by the new Democratic majority, as if this week’s election was some kind of revolutionary sea change, and not just a much welcome shift in the seesaw world of late-stage neoliberal capitalism, which is what I think it is.

AMY GOODMAN: Juan González, thanks so much for that analysis. Juan, of course, co-host on Democracy Now! and author of a number of books, including Harvest of Empire: A History of Latinos in America.

Coming up, we’ll be joined by Hasan Piker, the popular political left commentator and live-streamer, who had one of the biggest streaming broadcasts on election night, number two just after NBC, beating out the networks. He campaigned heavily in the last months with many progressives and DSA candidates. Stay with us.

AMY GOODMAN: “Copper Kettle” by Stephanie Coleman and Nora Brown, performing at the Brooklyn Folk Festival. This year’s festival is this weekend, and Democracy Now! will be there.

## Hasan Piker: Democrats Won Midterms, But Now They Have to Deliver on Voter Demands for Change

“The Democratic Socialists of America had a phenomenal night, and this movement is only going to continue to grow,” says prominent leftist political commentator Hasan Piker. “Now, here’s the problem: Hakeem Jeffries and Chuck Schumer are two of the least popular politicians in the country right now, and there are demands to change the leadership in the Democratic Party to reflect the desires of this new coalition that was developed on November 3.”

AMY GOODMAN: This is Democracy Now!, democracynow.org. I’m Amy Goodman, with Juan González.

Tuesday night wasn’t just a blue tsunami, or “bluenami.” It was a historic night for progressive Democrats, in particular candidates backed by the Democratic Socialists of America, DSA. Six new DSA-backed candidates were elected to the House: Angela Gonzales-Torres in California, Claire Valdez and Darializa Avila Chevalier here in New York, Chris Rabb in Pennsylvania, Donavan McKinney in Michigan and Melat Kiros in Colorado, representing Denver. Currently, just two sitting lawmakers, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Rashida Tlaib, are DSA members. And in Los Angeles, democratic socialist City Councilmember Nithya Raman, an immigrant from India, defeated the incumbent Mayor Karen Bass, who conceded Thursday.

For more on the record victory by democratic socialists and progressives, like Michigan Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed, we’re joined now from Los Angeles by the popular left political commentator Hasan Piker, one of the most prominent online influencers, with millions of followers. His live stream on Twitch was the second most watched individual broadcast on election night, after only NBC News’ live stream. That’s according to Reuters. Hasan Piker joins us now from West Hollywood, California.

Hasan, welcome back to Democracy Now! Your views on what has taken place in this country? How would you characterize it? And what are you expecting from these candidates who have now won?

HASAN PIKER: I think what happened on Tuesday was the final chapter of MAGA closing, after a decade of death and destruction and destabilization. I’ve thought quite a bit about this. I feel like even 2020, given that Joe Biden’s margin of victory did not feel the same way, given that 2020 happened in the backdrop of COVID, did not completely destroy the Trump movement in the same way that November 3rd did. Very exciting.

I think there’s a lot of hunger. This is — a lot of hunger for change. This is something that I observed early on in the oligarchy — “Stop the Oligarchy” rallies that Bernie and AOC were conducting all around the country. It’s something that I saw at the ICE protests. And it’s also something that I saw at the No Kings rallies that were taking place. So, I would say the Democratic Party, and the anti-Trump coalition broadly, got sick and tired of the corruption. They got sick and tired of the impacts on the economy. And they came out, and they voted against the Republicans. They voted for Democrats.

But I also want to talk about what kind of Democrats they voted for. In many blue districts, very clearly, especially in these contentious primaries, a lot of Democrats, lifelong Democrats, came out and voted for figures that in the past would have been billed as radical by mainstream media, corporate media, and also certainly the party bosses. It seems to me like people wanted to place the interests of the working class over the interests of corporations for once. And the Democratic Socialists of America had a phenomenal night, and this movement is only going to continue to grow.
