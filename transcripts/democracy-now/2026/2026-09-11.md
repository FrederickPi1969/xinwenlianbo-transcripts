A block from the mountain of rubble that had once been the World Trade Center, Fire Chief Tom McCarthy stumbled toward a traffic divider and sat down to catch his breath.

The back of his uniform had been ripped away, his right arm was in a sling and his head was so covered with ash that his eyes seemed like tiny slits.

“Don’t move, Chief,” said a paramedic who rushed up to help. “We’ll get you to an ambulance and a hospital right away.”

“I’m okay,” McCarthy said, waving him off. “Take care of the guys in worse shape first.”

The medic started pouring water over McCarthy’s head and eyes to wash away the ash.

“Feels great,” the chief said, breaking into a smile. “I can see again!”

McCarthy and the men of Battalion 32 left their firehouse in Red Hook, Brooklyn, yesterday morning after the second plane crashed into the second tower at the World Trade Center.

“We were watching it on the TV,” he said, “then a few seconds later we felt the explosion all the way in Red Hook. That’s when I knew it was gonna be a long day.”

They arrived at the scene on West St. sometime after 9:30. McCarthy had just gotten instructions to deploy his men and was heading toward the burning towers when he heard yet another explosion about 20 stories high in 2 World Trade.

“The building started coming down, and everybody ran,” he said. “It was like being on the beach with this big wave coming at you.”

“A piece of the Trade Center got me,” he said. “But it’s my guys I’m worried about.”

A few blocks away, another lucky and exhausted fireman — from Ladder Co. 113 in Crown Heights, Brooklyn — was taking off his boots and saying a few quiet thanks.

“I thought it was all over for a while,” said the firefighter.

“A bunch of us went into the lobby of the Marriott Hotel to rescue another trapped fireman when the whole building collapsed on us. We all grabbed onto a pillar in the middle of the lobby, and that’s the only thing that saved us.”

By early afternoon, word was already spreading that the number of missing firefighters and cops might make this the darkest day our city’s uniformed services had ever seen.

For Don Domenico, a Port Authority engineer who worked on the 88th floor of 1 World Trade Center, the day had started like so many others.

Domenico had reported to his job at his 7:30 starting time. He was busy at his desk when a “humongous explosion shook the building from above.”

“The ceilings started collapsing, file cabinets tumbled over,” Domenico said.

They raced down smoke-filled staircases, passing injured and dazed people as they went.

“Just when I got outside and was crossing the plaza,” Domenico said, “I heard another explosion and saw glass and steel all over the place.”

He fell on a car hood and suddenly there was so much smoke and debris that he couldn’t see a few feet in front of him and could barely breathe.

He felt his way along a row of parked cars until he found a door that was unlocked, and he sought refuge inside the car. He rifled through the glove compartment of the car, found some tissues and started spitting out the thick black muck that had filled his lungs.

Raheem Turane, a customer service representative for Empire Blue Cross and Blue Shield, was on the 20th floor of 1 World Trade Center talking on the telephone to a client when the first plane hit.

He looked out the window and saw “flames shooting down and people falling.”

He and the other employees scrambled for the exits and made it out safely. As the fire raged out of control, he headed toward the Brooklyn Bridge along with thousands of others, a river of people fleeing the chaos and terror of downtown Manhattan.

Turane was a third of the way across the bridge when he heard another explosion and turned back to look. Stunned, he watched one of the towers, this marvelous jewel, symbol of the world’s greatest city, collapse like a house of cards.

“As long as I live, I’ll never forget what I saw today,” he said.

In a few short hours, our nation had aged in ways impossible to measure. And all over town last night, thousands of grown men and women who had once thought themselves such hardened New Yorkers that nothing could shake them, managed to find their way home.

The lucky ones, those who survived without serious injury, held their children and loved ones tight in their arms. They took long, warm showers to wash the ashes from their bodies. And when they were alone in their beds, many saw the wave coming at them again, and they cried like frightened children at the horror they had witnessed.

And I think that is what so many people who went through that day —

JUAN GONZÁLEZ: And, Jameel, I’m wondering if you can talk especially about the policy that developed of extraordinary rendition, where, effectively, the government, in collusion with other governments around the world, kidnapped and disappeared people into black hole sites, and where that stands in terms of the legality as far as the Supreme Court is concerned.

JAMEEL JAFFER: Yeah, I mean, I think that that policy, the extraordinary rendition policy, is sort of emblematic about our collective decision to surrender the values that we thought we were supposed to defend. And, you know, one of the really — you know, when we talk about the departure from those important norms and principles after 9/11, we often focus on the Bush administration, the decision of the Bush administration to, you know, depart from those norms, the decision of the Obama administration to normalize some of those departures. But I actually think that the biggest failure was not in the executive branch. The biggest failure was in the courts, because, Juan, you were asking about, you know, what’s the status of the — what’s the legal status of these policies. JAMEEL JAFFER: Yeah, I mean, I think that we are seeing this in a lot of ways. JUAN GONZÁLEZ: And, Azmat, I’m wondering if you could talk about, you know, the shifting legal landscape after 9/11, the secrecy, the expanded surveillance and persecution under the Espionage Act and beyond, how that affected the ability of journalists, especially in the mainstream or commercial media, to properly report on wars and federal policies, and what the impact has been of independent media and whistleblowers going to organizations like WikiLeaks to bring out stuff that was not previously being — had not previously been revealed.

AZMAT KHAN: If you go back to that climate after 9/11, Americans were shocked and devastated and heartbroken. And journalists were asking, “How did this happen? What intelligence did we miss? How did the U.S. government fail?” And their efforts were largely concentrated on answering these very important and fundamental questions.

But we also saw newsrooms institute shifts, so they started making beats for covering national security. There was a real reworking of what kinds of things legacy media was covering, and, you know, in many ways, that’s incredibly important. But it also meant that more and more journalists were dependent on the federal government for intelligence assessments, what they deemed incredibly important to understanding and answering those questions that felt so urgent at the time.

And what you wound up having is, you know, a broad understanding that legacy news media in that time period was incredibly dependent on anonymous sources, sometimes unvetted intelligence, leaks to journalists that oftentimes came from an administration that was trying to lead the public to war. And later on, you would see these sort of mea culpas in major news organizations about what they had gotten wrong and why. I think the most famous is the lead-up to the Iraq War. I think many people are familiar with the failures, the unvetted intelligence that made its way onto the very front pages of major newspapers.

But I think it was also something deeper. I think we saw a fundamental shift take place in the presumption of guilt or innocence. I think that the label of terrorism or the designation that an individual was associated with a group, just the mere allegation itself would be so damning. And so, we went from what would ordinarily be a presumption of innocence until somebody is proven guilty, to what I found in my reporting was often a presumption of guilt unless you can prove yourself innocent. And the bar for proving yourself innocent was so much higher than the bar for intelligence used to lethally target and kill someone in the first place, and that gap was enormous.

You know, you’ve had some of the guests from my own reporting on this show, a man named Basim Razzo, for example, whose homes were targeted in the middle of the night, of his and his brother. And Basim woke up one night to see the stars over Mosul, to find out that his wife, daughter, brother and nephew were killed. You know, the roof of his house was gone. He’s looking up. He learns his loved ones have been killed. He sets out on a quest for justice.

And he has one of the most rigorous cases I’ve ever seen.
