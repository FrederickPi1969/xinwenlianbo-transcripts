## Yemen Plunges Back into “Full-Scale Civil War,” Houthis Trade Strikes with Saudi Arabia

This latest escalation comes after Ansar Allah captured key areas along Yemen’s Red Sea coastline in recent weeks, driving back the Saudi-backed, internationally recognized government and taking control of the strategic Bab al-Mandab Strait.

“Yes, the Houthis are attacking Saudi Arabia with airstrikes, but there is now, actively, a real ground war going on within Yemen, and that is a far more relevant and important issue for the Yemenis on a day-to-day basis because they are now back into a full-scale civil war,” says Middle East scholar Helen Lackner.

AMY GOODMAN: Houthi fighters from Yemen have targeted the Saudi capital of Riyadh for the third time in recent days. On Saturday, a Houthi missile strike on a Riyadh airport killed 13 people and injured over 300 in the deadliest single attack on Saudi soil in over a decade. The Houthis have hit the airport at least four times. All flights out of the airport are currently suspended.

Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman has not been seen publicly in a week, is reportedly shuttling between palaces with heavy security and bunkers. Riyadh is 500 or so miles from Houthi-controlled areas in Yemen.

This is Houthi military spokesperson Yahya Sarea speaking Monday.

The first operation targeted King Khalid Airport in Riyadh in three phases, starting the evening before yesterday, resulting in the disruption of air navigation at the airport and its complete closure.

AMY GOODMAN: In Yemen, a leader of the Houthis, also known as the Ansar Allah movement, warned the U.S. it would face a swift and painful response if it joined Saudi Arabia’s attacks on Houthi-controlled territory. The warning came after President Trump said he’s going to look at joining the Saudi-led counteroffensive aimed at ousting Houthi fighters from Yemen’s Red Sea coastline and the strategic Bab al-Mandab Strait.

In September, Reuters reported Houthi leaders met secretly with U.S. officials in Oman and assured them U.S. ships in the Red Sea would not be targeted, even as a blockade was underway on Saudi-linked ships.

Yemen’s Saudi-backed, internationally recognized government says Houthi strikes over the past 24 hours within Yemen killed at least five civilians and left over a dozen wounded. That includes an attack on a hospital in Yemen’s third-largest city of Taiz, which is encircled by Houthi fighters. Residents there are reportedly rationing food and essential goods amidst growing warnings of a growing humanitarian disaster.

Meanwhile, the Yemen Data Project reported more than 250 Saudi air raids on Yemen last month, the highest number since 2022, killing at least 66 civilians and injuring 90.

For more, we’re joined by Helen Lackner, author of several books on Yemen, including Yemen in Crisis: The Road to War and Yemen: Poverty and Conflict. Welcome to Democracy Now!, Helen Lackner. Thanks so much for being with us. Do you see what’s going on here? Saudi Arabia has been at war with Yemen for over a decade. HELEN LACKNER: Well, thank you for inviting me. Do you want me to start with the Houthis or with the government?

HELEN LACKNER: Right. Well, the internationally recognized government is the descendant of what was the government of Yemen until 2014, when the Houthis took over the capital Sana’a. And since then, the government has been based mainly, in principle, in Aden, which is the major city on the southern coast. And also, some of its main leaders, and usually the presidents, have been based in Saudi Arabia, in Riyadh, and various other people have been all over the place.

The basic issues to do with the internationally recognized government is its failure to operate as a functioning, operational government, and in particular of having a united and effective military. Its military are composed of different groups, which are largely rival to each other and have different commands, and they have been unable to operate as a single unit. And that is really one of the main reasons why the Houthi offensive last month, that took over Mokha and the Bab al-Mandab, succeeded so quickly, basically because the people opposing them were far too disorganized. And it’s also true that the Houthis are, and were, still the stronger military element throughout Yemen.

So, very briefly, to get into who the Houthis are, they are a group that was initially created in the 1990s to oppose the rise of Salafi Muslims in the far north of Yemen. Their heartland is the absolute far north of Yemen, a city called Saada. And they were created, and they are a group of Zaydis. Zaydis are a branch of Shia Muslim, but they are not particularly different from Sunni Muslims in terms of theology. But the Houthis are very much a fundamentalist group, a very extremist group when it comes to their way of ruling and their ambitions. And many people tend to compare them with the Afghani Taliban. And I think that comparison does have some validity. And for people who don’t know Yemen well, it will give them an idea of what kind of group they are. They are extremely repressive. They are also very ideologically motivated. They’ve now, for 15 years or longer, been educating and forcing their ideology onto children throughout their school and education system, as well as adults, but certainly we now have a generation of young adults who have known nothing else but Houthi ideology, and at least many of them must be expected to believe them.

And I think it’s worth pointing out that Houthis now control approximately two-thirds of Yemen’s population, if not more, so at least 20 million people are living under their authority. The party, the Socialist Party, the Yemeni Socialist Party, really got very much weakened, but partly by mainly a civil war that happened in Yemen in 1994, but also very much because it was unable to maintain the support it had had, because of the differences between the people who wanted to return to the pre-1990 borders, and who basically wanted to separate Yemen back into two states, and those who believed in Yemen as a single state.

And basically, what people are interested in, what people are missing very much, are the basic social welfare and the basic social democratic aspects of that regime, which was the fact that it provided, you know, good health systems, good education systems, basic infrastructure and basic salaries that people could live on — not in luxury, but they could live on them.

And these things basically vanished, largely, very rapidly after 1994 and under the Saleh regime, which was — you know, Saleh had been president of the Yemen Arab Republic from 1978 onwards, then he became and stayed president of the Republic of Yemen until he was ousted after the 2011 uprisings. And that led — that also gradually led to, basically, the civil war that started in 2014. I mean, I don’t know if you want me to spend time discussing the 2011-to-2014 period or if you want to focus more on the current — on the current situation.

HELEN LACKNER: Yeah, I think it’s important to remember that, you know, yes, the Houthis are attacking Saudi Arabia with airstrikes, but there is now actively a real ground war going on within Yemen, and that is a far more relevant and important issue for the Yemenis on a day-to-day basis because they are now back into a full-scale civil war. It’s a war between Yemenis, between the Houthis and the government, and basically what I would call the non-Houthis or the anti-Houthis. So, the Saudi element is really — for Yemenis, it’s part of the war, but it’s not the war. And, you know, the airstrikes at the moment by most Yemenis are considered airstrikes that are supporting them against the Houthis.

So, the fact that the — you know, that the world attention is focusing on a few airstrikes on Saudi Arabia is not something that is the top priority for Yemen and Yemenis. The top priority for Yemenis is dealing with, you know, the Houthi-anti-Houthi war and the fact that if the — you know, after the defeats they suffered in early September, the anti-Houthis have pulled themselves together, to some extent, and now there is a lot of confrontations going on on a daily basis around Taiz, around the Bab al-Mandab, around various other fronts, even in the far north and around Marib and Jawf. And this is — you know, this is an ongoing war — i.e. the war that went on from 2015 to ’22 and was interrupted for the last four years has now basically resumed.

AMY GOODMAN: Where, Helen Lackner, do you see this going? We have these reports that last month Houthis secretly negotiated with Washington in Oman, saying they wouldn’t target U.S. or Israeli ships in the Red Sea, only Saudi ships. The reason they’ve said this, I’m sure, is because, you know, there was a 52-day air — series of airstrikes last year, in 2025, from March ’til May, from the U.S. against Houthi areas, and although this ended with this apparent agreement between them to to cease fire, it certainly did cause quite a lot of damage to the Houthis.

I don’t — and I’m very sad to say this, and I hope I’m wrong, but my belief, and that of most other people I’ve heard and spoken to, is that this restarting of the war, renewal of the war, is going to last until one side or the other gets close to winning. And the Saudis are currently supporting the government, and they were — they are really in a very bad dilemma, because they know from previous experience — they did, you know, aerial bombing for seven years, and it got nowhere. Trump did aerial bombing for 52 days, and it didn’t really go anywhere, either. So, it’s quite clear that pure — and it’s been demonstrated elsewhere in the world, that, you know, airstrikes on their own do not win a war.

HELEN LACKNER: And so, the situation, the future for the Saudis at this point is they have to either decide to make a deal with the Houthis, which I think is increasingly difficult, even though they almost had such a deal in 2023. But I think the situation between then and now has changed, and particularly with the recent attacks. It would be extremely difficult for them to make a deal with the Houthis or to continue with the war. I mean, you know, I haven’t seen anyone who has found a solution for this current situation, other than a continuation of, you know, very considerable disasters at the humanitarian level. I’m really glad you mentioned earlier, you know, the displaced. And you also remember that the humanitarian situation for Yemenis, in general, is absolutely disastrous. ## Headlines for October 13, 2026

Elsewhere, the Saudi-led coalition in Yemen said it had intercepted missiles fired by Houthis toward southwestern Saudi Arabia and the capital, Riyadh, where Houthi attacks on King Khalid International Airport in recent days killed 18 people and injured over 300. The continuing attacks came as the U.S. Embassy in Riyadh said U.S. citizens had been arrested for taking photos of recent events in Saudi Arabia — though it did not specify how many people were detained.

The U.N. reports at least 139 people are dead and 84 others remain missing after an overcrowded boat capsized while sailing from Djibouti to Yemen. The International Organization for Migration said in a statement, “Despite the violence in Yemen, migrants are still making the perilous journeys hoping ​to make it to the Gulf countries.” This is U.N. spokesperson Stéphane Dujarric.

Stéphane Dujarric: “This tragedy is yet another stark reminder of the dangers people face in search of safety, in search of work, and just in search of a better life for themselves and their families. Since the fighting escalated on Yemen’s west coast, more than 217,000 people have been displaced from their homes.”

In the Persian Gulf, the U.K.'s Maritime Trade Operations Center reports an oil tanker was struck by a projectile while exiting the Strait of Hormuz Monday. All crew members are reportedly safe and accounted for. Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps took credit for the attack, saying it was the third ship it had struck in two days. Since the start of October, there have been at least 18 attacks on ships in or around the Strait of Hormuz.

In Ukraine, a Russian strike on a residential high-rise in a Kyiv suburb killed at least two people Monday evening, while Russian attacks on other parts of Ukraine killed another six people. The deadly strikes came as Ukrainian officials said Russia continues to bomb energy sites despite President Trump’s insistence on Saturday that Moscow and Kyiv had agreed to cease such attacks. Both sides denied Trump’s claim, though Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said he was willing to halt attacks on energy infrastructure if Russia reciprocated.

Meanwhile, 11 sailors went missing in the Black Sea today after an apparent drone attack on two cargo ships off the coast of Bulgaria, one of which sank. It was the second deadly attack of its kind in the past week and prompted Bulgaria to summon Russia’s ambassador. This is Bulgarian Prime Minister Rumen Radev.

Prime Minister Rumen Radev: “We do not possess solid, clear evidence to level an accusation against any specific party. However, logic suggests that the outcome works to the advantage of the Russian Federation. For this reason, I am summoning the Russian ambassador for a meeting today.”

A bipartisan group of senators is calling on President Trump to reverse plans to purchase diesel fuel from Russia as part of an effort to lower fuel prices ahead of the midterm elections. In a statement, the senators wrote that Trump is violating a 2022 law that explicitly banned Russian energy imports, adding, “The United States should not provide Putin with a financial lifeline while Russia continues to kill Ukrainian civilians. The Administration must immediately reverse course.” Four Republican senators signed the statement: Susan Collins of Maine, Alaska’s Lisa Murkowski, Thom Tillis of North Carolina and John Curtis of Utah. On Monday, President Trump defended the Treasury Department’s waiver allowing Russian fuel imports through next April, writing on his Truth Social website, “I made the right choice for the American People — ALWAYS, AMERICA FIRST!”

Federal agents from the Department of Homeland Security raided the Washington, D.C., home of Democratic Congressmember Ilhan Omar of Minnesota on Monday. The raid came less than a month before the midterm elections. The Justice Department has traditionally tried not to take public investigative actions involving a candidate in the 60 days before a primary or general election.
