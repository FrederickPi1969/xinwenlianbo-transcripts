The United States war in Afghanistan lasted for nearly 20 years, the longest-ever active conflict in U.S. history. Now as the Taliban government — overthrown by the U.S. in 2001 and later reestablished following the end of U.S. military occupation in 2021 — tightens its patriarchal restrictions on women in Afghanistan, Sultan warns international actors not to “repeat the [mistakes of the] 1990s, of turning our backs on the country. We need to engage with the Afghan people, support Afghan women and support civil society.”

NERMEEN SHAIKH: Tomorrow, Friday, is the 25th anniversary of the September 11th, 2001, attacks on the World Trade Center in New York. Less than a month later, the United States began bombing Afghanistan.

One of our most moving shows from our archives was from January 2002, when we brought together two women whose lives were deeply impacted by 9/11 and its aftermath: Rita Lasar lost her brother Abe Zelmanowitz at the World Trade Center, and Afghan American New Yorker Masuda Sultan, who had family in Afghanistan. This is Masuda speaking in our studio in January 2002.

MASUDA SULTAN: When September 11th happened, and I watched those buildings collapse, and I thought about all those people in there, like Rita’s brother, I was angry. I was angry, just as any New Yorker. And my immediate feeling was, “Who are the jerks that did this, and what are we going to do about it?” And I went down there that first week and actually got through some police barricades and watched what was going on and volunteered for a few hours. But my immediate reaction was anger and shock.

But that same day, I sensed that something like this was not going to go unpunished. I mean, I would want to figure out who did it, too. I realized that Osama was probably in some way responsible and that the United States was probably, you know, going to do something about that. And guess what: Osama’s in Afghanistan. And that day, I started making phone calls, on September 11th, to family here in New York to tell them to also call some family back in Afghanistan to sort of alert people to the fact that this may happen. And some of them didn’t think that it would go that far, and they said, “Oh, you’re being — you know, you’re being too cautious.” And as time and the events sort of unfolded, that’s exactly what happened.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: That’s Afghan American New Yorker Masuda Sultan talking to us about her experience of 9/11, just a few months after the attacks. In December of 2001, Masuda Sultan traveled to Afghanistan and Pakistan to visit her family. She was accompanied by Jon Alpert and Tami Alpert of Downtown Community Television, who made a film about her journey called Afghanistan: From Ground Zero to Ground Zero. Here’s an excerpt.

MASUDA SULTAN: We’re on our way to my cousins’ house. They moved to Quetta after being hit by a U.S. bomb. I don’t know what we’ll find there.

They were living near government buildings in Kandahar with Taliban close by, and they figured that the U.S. would target those buildings, and they didn’t want to get hurt by those, so they moved outside of the city and went to their farm just on the outskirts. They were there for two weeks before they were hit. There was a low-flying plane, and they came out of their houses, and many of them were shot at.

This couple, they had four kids. There’s only one mother-in-law left out of the whole family.

Yes, all of these children were there, running from the bullets. And it’s her leg, as well. That’s another one of the bullets.

This is the daughter of one of my cousins. Her mom was killed, and that’s her other sister over there. It was 19 people in total.

His daughter died, his daughter-in-law, his brother, his nephew, one of his cousins, another cousin. He says he lost three grandchildren and a nephew. When the plane started shooting at them, they lost track of where everyone was, and it was chaotic.

He says that — he says that when — when they realized what had happened, one of his sons yelled out that there are many sheep lying out here. When they went to find out, they saw that it was humans. It was their family, shot on the floor.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: An excerpt from Jon and Tami Alpert’s Afghanistan: From Ground Zero to Ground Zero, a 2002 film about Afghan American Masuda Sultan’s journey to Afghanistan in December 2001.

Right after Masuda met with her family, she also called in to Democracy Now! to describe what she had seen. We played a clip of that phone call when she and Rita Lasar met for the first time in our studio in January 2002.

MASUDA SULTAN: These people were not Taliban supporters. They weren’t al-Qaeda fighters. They were simple Afghans trying to stay safe in their own country. The events of September 11th really made me angry, but seeing these people and what they went through makes me angry, as well. You know, they say that in war — they say that you have to break a couple of eggs in order to make an omelet, but when those eggs are your family, what can you do?

AMY GOODMAN: Rita Lasar broke down listening to Masuda Sultan speaking, and then she herself, Rita, talked about her brother, Abe Zelmanowitz. He was on the 27th floor of the World Trade Center on 9/11 and did not survive. This is Rita talking about her brother Abe.

RITA LASAR: President Bush mentioned him in the National Cathedral speech and cited him as being a hero. And I realized that my government was going to use my brother as justification for killing other people, and that had a tremendous impact on me. I didn’t want that to happen, not in my brother’s name. And so I wrote a letter to the Times, which they printed, asking our government to please be cautious and not do something they couldn’t take back.

And then I was asked to speak at a peace rally, and I did it. And just before I went on, I was told they had started bombing Afghanistan, and I realized something I had never realized before. I had heard the term “collateral damage” all my life. It was always used about people far away from us. And I realized now what it meant, because my brother was collateral damage, in a war that he didn’t want and Masuda’s people didn’t want. And I knew I had to do something, but I didn’t know what.

And then I got a call from this wonderful woman from this marvelous organization called Global Exchange, and she said, “Would you like to go to Afghanistan and meet people like you who have lost their families?” And I thought, “That’s perfect,” because Masuda and I are the same. There’s no difference between us. My family member died, I’m grieving, and her family — God, I don’t know how — how you survived, just hearing about yours. But we’re the same people.

AMY GOODMAN: That’s Rita Lasar speaking on Democracy Now! in January of 2002, across the table from Masuda Sultan. Rita was on her way to Afghanistan with Global Exchange and would then go on to co-found September 11th Families for Peaceful Tomorrows. She died at the age of 85 in 2017. September 11th Families for Peaceful Tomorrows will be holding a vigil today in Union Square at 5:00. Joining them will be our guest right now, Masuda Sultan, the Afghan American women’s rights activist who is now the co-founder of ABAAD: Afghan Women Forward.

I only wish that you and Rita could be sitting across from the table now, Masuda. This is 25 years later. As you talked then about being an Afghan New Yorker and, like all New Yorkers, horrified on September 11th, but then going back to your family in Afghanistan, 19 members of them killed in a house that was bombed by the U.S. military, your thoughts today?

MASUDA SULTAN: It’s really difficult to process that day without getting very emotional. I’m going to try not to get emotional.

And, you know, as a New Yorker, the shock and the horror and all of that, and the thoughts about what would happen to us, knowing that it probably was Osama bin Laden — I had that feeling right at the beginning. You know, as Muslim New Yorkers, we were grieving the tragedy of 9/11, and then, at the same time, we were having to be questioned about whether we belonged. And, you know, that’s what happens when a whole population is folded into one act. And then, at the same time, thinking about Afghanistan and what would happen if it was indeed Osama bin Laden, what would happen in Afghanistan. It was really just a very confusing time.

And, of course, then going to Afghanistan, with just the intention of connecting again with the family I had so recently met. I had left Afghanistan as a child due to the Soviet war. I was barely 5. And I had gone back the summer of ’01 to connect with my family, and it had been this incredible trip, where I really got to understand where we came from and what the love of family meant and what it was to belong in a place where your parents were from and they spoke the same language, and the love that I received. And so, I was worried about them, and that was why I wanted to go back, just to see what was happening. I had no idea that this had happened when I went on the trip with Jon, and then having that unfold of hearing that whole tragedy that happened to our family, and then coming back, not knowing —

AMY GOODMAN: And just to say, they had left Kabul, because they were afraid of being bombed, and went to the family house in the country, and that’s where they were bombed. And you went to a U.S. military base there —

AMY GOODMAN: — Jon and Tami Alpert following you with a camera, and you asked them why they killed your family?

MASUDA SULTAN: Yeah, I mean, it’s — it’s incredible that I actually was able to, with the help of Jon, get onto the base in Kandahar.

So, they had been living in Kandahar city. Some buildings around them were bombed, and they had been Taliban-occupied, or they thought they were, and they decided to go to their farmhouse about 60 miles north of Kandahar just to get away from it all. And that’s where it happened. So, yes, after we discovered that, we discovered them in Pakistan, because they had left Afghanistan after that, and then we went to the site of the bombing and discovered that. And then we — you know, just like big gaping holes in the ground and a man cleaning up from the village, you know, just a very distraught, scary scene.

And then we went to the base in Kandahar. And I didn’t want to go to the base in Kandahar after seeing all of that, but Jon Alpert said, you know, “This is your chance to ask questions.” And, of course, I wanted to know what happened and how this happened. So I did. And when I went to the base, you know, I asked a question from a guy who was like a guy I went to college with, who was standing there saying, you know, like, “Oh yeah, I don’t know what happened to your family. I don’t have any information. Oh, but here’s an AC-130 gunship, which was probably the type of gunship that attacked your family.”

And, you know, and I looked at him, and I thought, “He’s here because he’s, you know, wanting to protect his country. He’s a good guy. I went to school with guys like this. Like, how is — who do I blame? Like, how did this happen?” And that question of how that happened really was eating at me, because I thought, “Why would they kill innocent people?” Like, I know — I’m an American. Like, I love Americans so much. I grew up in New York City. Like, everyone’s kind, compassionate. I mean, yeah, you have some crazies out there, but, generally speaking, Americans are kind, compassionate, helpful people. And that was what I could not reconcile. I was sure it had to have been some terrible mistake, and I thought, as soon as this was understood, that there would be some kind of resolution or help or apology coming to our family, because our family did not support al-Qaeda. They did not support the Taliban. They were happy that the Taliban were gone. And that apology never came. And I guess that that, you know, just was too much to expect.

But, of course, through that process, I met the incredible human beings, like, thanks to you and your show, Rita Lasar, who — that was the first time that I met her. I had no idea what to expect on your show. I had just come back, was in shock. And I thought she might attack me. Like, I was — I thought she might say to me something along the lines of what some reporters had been saying to me, like, “How do we know what your family was up to? And how can you” — you know, so, I was not prepared for the kind of response that she gave me. And —

AMY GOODMAN: Because she lost her brother, Abe Zelmanowitz, that day.

MASUDA SULTAN: Yes, and he was a hero that stayed there. And she was very, very thoughtful about the fact that innocent people were in Afghanistan, should not suffer. And she was an incredible human being.

But then, the other incredible human beings I met, like Medea Benjamin, Colleen Kelly, Kelly Campbell, like, these were people that, even though they had their own grief to deal with, they said, “Wait a minute. We don’t want other people to go through this.” And they thought that it would be a good idea to try to help these people, which was, you know, wonderful. But how are we going to help them?

So, that was when we lobbied the U.S. Congress to create the victims’ fund for innocent Afghan civilians. And Senator Leahy actually passed a bill to assist victims, which, now that I think about it, given all the polarization and grief, it’s incredible that we were able to actually get that through. But that was because people like Medea Benjamin and the 9/11 Families for Peaceful Tomorrows were, you know, knocking on doors at Congress, at State Department and elsewhere, saying, “We need to do something about this.” And it’s really like a very American story in that way, like civil society really engaging and saying, like — when we would go to some of these congresspeople’s offices, they would say, “We’ll get those terrorists. We’re going to get them.” And then, you know, people like Rita would say, “Yes, but we need to be careful not to kill innocent people.”

NERMEEN SHAIKH: And finally, Masuda, if you could talk about your assessment of where things stand now, your response, first of all, to the 2021 withdrawal of the U.S. and NATO troops, and the situation today under the Taliban?

MASUDA SULTAN: Well, I wanted the war to end for a long time. I think we definitely stayed way too long. We empowered the wrong types of people. They were abusive and corrupt. We did not listen to the Afghan people’s grievances.

But I think one of the things we need to keep in mind, based on all that we went through in Afghanistan, is not to repeat the 1990s of turning our backs on the country. We need to engage with the Afghan people, support Afghan women and support civil society.

The fact that Afghan girls are being denied secondary education and beyond is unprecedented in the whole world. Everybody knows that it’s completely wrong. And it’s their right within Islam. And actually, all the Taliban we talk to agree with us, but the emir — because we went to Afghanistan, a group of us women, including Kelly Campbell and Medea Benjamin, went back after the Taliban takeover and wanted to connect with the women there. We talked to the Education Ministry of the Taliban. We talked to many other officials and said, “Why are you doing this?” And they said to us, both privately and publicly, that “We want the girls to study, and we want women to work, but the emir is not allowing it. He stopped it the day the girls were supposed to go to school.” And that got them in trouble. And subsequently, all of them who say this get in trouble, and the emir is enforcing, you know, that people shouldn’t talk about this. So, we’re in a pickle with that.

AMY GOODMAN: So, you founded a new organization. I want to thank you, Masuda, for joining us. In a moment, we’re going to go directly to Kabul. Masuda Sultan, Afghan American women’s rights activist, co-founder of ABAAD: Afghan Women Forward.

Despite the outsized role it played in destabilizing the country, there has been “zero funding” this year from the U.S. to humanitarian organizations operating in Afghanistan. “Don’t forget your promises when you went for the door abruptly five years ago,” Egeland says, addressing the United States and other, largely European, countries that made up the U.S.-led NATO coalition in Afghanistan.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: We go now to Afghanistan, where a deepening humanitarian crisis is being largely overlooked by the international community following the withdrawal of NATO troops in 2021 and the return of the Taliban. The crisis is most severe for women and girls, whose basic rights to education, freedom of movement and access to services have been severely restricted over the last five years. Afghan women are banned from outdoor parks, among other spaces, and books written by women have been removed from libraries. Women venturing beyond the home must be accompanied by a male guardian, and so-called morality laws curtail their rights to speak, sing or recite out loud in public.

AMY GOODMAN: Nearly 6 million Afghan refugees who had been living in neighboring countries, including Pakistan and Iran, have returned to Afghanistan following mass deportations and have been met with very little support following the withdrawal of U.S. troops. International aid dropped from $3.3 billion in 2022 to $1 billion last year. The U.S. [sic] this year has given half a billion dollars.

For more, we go to Kabul, where we’re joined by Jan Egeland, secretary general of the Norwegian Refugee Council. This is his fifth visit to Afghanistan since 2021.

Jan Egeland, what have you found on the ground in Afghanistan right now, as we speak to you from blocks from ground zero 25 years after the 9/11 attacks?

JAN EGELAND: Well, I’ve been these last days together with the poorest people on Earth, really, and people who have especially then come back from Pakistan to Kandahar in the south of Afghanistan, also here to Kabul and to elsewhere. These are people deported, as you rightly said, 6 million in three years. It’s one of the biggest deportation processes on Earth in this generation.

And the world is not here. The international community is not really here to help them. There are some nongovernmental organizations and U.N. partners on the ground, but we’re so overstretched and underfunded.

So, these women with children who have been 30 years as refugees in Pakistan come empty-handed back. They get 48 hours to leave. They’re dumped at the border. And then they are in desperate search of shelter, of water, of food, of some protection. We’re able to help some, but we would like to help many more.

And that’s why we’re calling on all of those countries who promised to stand with the Afghan civilian population, girls, women, families in particular: Don’t forget your promises when you went for the door abruptly five years ago.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: Jan, could you talk about, specifically, the situation in rural areas, as well as whether — given these severe, harsh and actually quite unfathomable restrictions on women, whether women are still allowed to do humanitarian work, provide social and health services to Afghan women and children?

JAN EGELAND: Yeah, I mean, the poverty, the deprivation is, of course, particularly big in the countryside. I was in areas outside of Kandahar, and people are living under plastic sheeting and were longing for the home that we were now building to them. One woman I met, a mother, several other children, cried when she told that she sold her 12-year-old daughter as a child bride for $600. I mean, she loved this girl, and she had to sell her to get food for the rest of the family.

The rights of Afghan women have been — have been destroyed, really. There is no doubt about it. There is 100 edicts from the supreme leader, the emir, curtailing right to work, right to education and freedom of movement. Of those, the right to education is possibly the worst, because after sixth grade, primary school, girls cannot get further education, which means we will not have female — new female teachers, doctors, nurses, midwives in some time from now.

That’s why I went to Kandahar and met the Taliban leadership. That’s why I met the Taliban leadership here in Kabul, to confront them with this factor, saying to them, “You promised us there would be education. You said it would just take time for you to organize it in the Islamic and Afghan fashion. It’s five years now. And you cannot have an economy which only involves half of the population.”

Where are the other diplomats? Where are the other countries that should come here and fight with us for the women of Afghanistan? We’re too alone on the ground here in Afghanistan now.

AMY GOODMAN: I want to correct something I said, Jan Egeland. I said that the U.S. had given half a billion dollars to the Taliban this year.

AMY GOODMAN: But in your news release, you said $500 million U.S. have been provided for the humanitarian response. That’s not necessarily from the United States, right?

JAN EGELAND: Yeah, there is zero funding from the United States of America to humanitarian work, that I know of, here in Afghanistan this year at all. And, you know, I remember what Barbara Bush promised when she was the first lady, when what one politician after the other from both parties promised. They said, “We will stand with the women and girls of Afghanistan.” Well, I don’t see that here, because the reason we have to lay off female aid workers, female teachers, teaching primary school girls, is because of lack of funding. It’s not because of the Taliban. We have been able to negotiate a return of all of our female colleagues, aid workers. They work as Afghan women to Afghan women in all of the provinces where we operate.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: Jan, you mentioned this extraordinary mass deportation campaign from Pakistan and Iran, 6 million refugees in total. If you could say a little bit more? Where are they? And how are they being assimilated back into Afghan society, many of whom were, in both Iran and [Pakistan], with refugee status —

NERMEEN SHAIKH: — some of whom had asylum status, the others who had gained, effectively, citizenship?

JAN EGELAND: Yeah, they had all sorts of rights, which is natural, because they had been there for decades, both in Iran and in Pakistan. And I met these refugee communities. Of course, again, the rest of the world did not help Iran and Pakistan that much in hosting all of these refugees. There is a lot of talk in the United States and in Europe — perhaps even more in Europe — that, “Oh, we have too many Afghans. We have to get them back,” and so on. There’s a very small number of Afghans in North America and in Europe compared to Pakistan and Iran.

So, in the last three years, Pakistan and Iran said, “We will not anymore shoulder this on our own. We will send people back.” So, they give one day, two days’, three days’ notice, and then they come and, by force, take people. And in many cases, it’s only the women and children they find, the grandparents. The father of the household may be out searching for work or being in the market or being traveling. So, then they are dumped at the border by Pakistan, by Iran, on the other side.

And there, we are, some humanitarian groups, trying to help them. And there is then some transport to the places where they want to go. And then they are often helped to distant relatives that are too poor to really be able to absorb them. So, they would be next to a relative’s house, but with no real shelter. And there’s where I found many of the families, because we were already giving them some cash assistance, and we will now give them shelter and water and sanitation.

By the way, none of these girls that — women that I met, who had been in Pakistan 30, 40 years, had ever had education, ever. And their children had not had education, neither boys nor girls. So, it’s a crisis for human rights, really. It’s a crisis for humanity, this.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: Jan, very quickly, I just want to make one clarification: Neither Iran nor Pakistan granted citizenship to Afghan refugees or the generations who were born in either of the two countries. Amy?

AMY GOODMAN: I want to thank you so much for being with us, secretary general of the Norwegian Refugee Council, joining us from Kabul, Afghanistan.

This is President Bush speaking before a joint session of Congress on September 20th, 2001.

PRESIDENT GEORGE W. BUSH: Americans should not expect one battle, but a lengthy campaign, unlike any other we have ever seen. It may include dramatic strikes, visible on TV, and covert operations, secret even in success. AMY GOODMAN: President Bush declaring the start of the so-called war on terror, September 20th, 2001.

We go now to Spencer Ackerman, Pulitzer Prize and National Magazine Award-winning journalist and author of the book Reign of Terror: How the 9/11 Era Destabilized America and Produced Trump and the Forever Wars newsletter. His latest article the War on Terror went global and backfired on the United States for Zeteo is headlined “How the 'War on Terror' Went Global — and Backfired on the United States.”

SPENCER ACKERMAN: Thank you, Amy. Good morning. Good morning, Nermeen. Really appreciate you having me on the show.

The “war on terror” was always a construct to be waged, never quite to be ended, that involved a tremendous violent exertion of American power as a demonstration effect to — on the theory that it would ward off the next 9/11. A whole lot of regimes around the world, rising powers looking for their own advantage, saw what the United States was constructing and realized they wanted that for themselves.

Eventually, that would be Vladimir Putin rejecting U.S. unipolar hegemony and deciding that much as the United States in Iraq wanted the ability to attack its adversaries before they had a chance to, you know, allegedly attack them, so, too, Russia would reserve that right for itself. We see that most recently in Ukraine.

In China, they looked at the Muslims of their Xinjiang province and decided they wanted to construct the same sort of panopticon and repressive apparatus of surveillance and reeducation. In their sense, we didn’t have that part here, but certainly the surveillance and the law enforcement repression of Muslims inside the United States.

And in Israel, they looked at the United States as going on an open-ended war against what, certainly subtextually, to very many, was on Islam, and said to themselves, “Finally, the United States is going to act the way we seek to act against the Palestinians.” And the long legacy of that is on display in the genocide of the Palestinian people.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: And, Spencer, you write in that same piece about going yourself to Israel in December 2001. If you could talk about what happened during that visit and what you were told by the people you spoke to?
