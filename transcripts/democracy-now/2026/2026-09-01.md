## Amid Severe Water Crisis & Drought, Puerto Rico Marks 10 Years of “La Junta” Oversight

Puerto Rico’s water woes come 10 years after the United States passed the bankruptcy law PROMESA to restructure the U.S. territory’s debt, installing an unelected board colloquially known as “La Junta” that continues to hold sweeping authority to determine fiscal and public policy. “This water crisis taking place right now in Puerto Rico, also La Junta bears some responsibility on it,” says Puerto Rican investigative reporter Luis Valentín. “It did not reduce the debt, and [so] pretty much crowds out resources that could be used for infrastructure work.”

AMY GOODMAN: This is Democracy Now!, democracynow.org. I’m Amy Goodman, with Juan González.

In Puerto Rico, the month of July was the driest on record in more than 120 years, accompanied by record-breaking heat. Reservoirs are so low, the governor has declared a state of emergency for more than two dozen cities and towns. Agricultural areas, as well as cities, are impacted. The Aqueduct and Sewer Authority implemented a water-rationing schedule in early August, where 180,000 households and businesses in San Juan and surrounding areas receive public water only every other 48 hours, complicating daily life for more than a half a million Puerto Ricans, who are U.S. citizens.

But the current water crisis in Puerto Rico is not an isolated event. Juan Carlos Dávila filed this report from Puerto Rico.

JUAN CARLOS DÁVILA: Puerto Rico’s water crisis is worsening, and this summer’s record drought isn’t the only reason why. In early August, the Water and Sewage Authority began rationing water services for residents of San Juan and surrounding areas due to the drought. But even before the drought was declared in July, communities in Loíza and San Juan were having water problems, and people depend on water trucks to meet their needs.

This is Melody Arévalo, who runs a local restaurant in Loíza.

MELODY ARÉVALO: [translated] Well, the problems began three months ago. A day like today has been difficult, because we had water needs since yesterday. And because of the chaos that’s been happening around the country, our cistern was not filled yesterday. Today, we had very little water, and we had to dispatch the clients we already had here.

JUAN CARLOS DÁVILA: In some places, like Residencial Luis Lloréns Torres, Puerto Rico’s largest public housing project, the government has installed water cisterns to mitigate the crisis. This is Antonio “Puruco” Látimer, community leader and former Puerto Rican basketball player.

ANTONIO ”PURUCO” LÁTIMER: [translated] Our country should not be living this way. AIDA LUZ PEÑA: [translated] Not even a drop. Every time I open it, I grab it and say, “Oh my goodness.” Not even a little sound comes through.

JUAN CARLOS DÁVILA: Aida Luz Peña lives in a third-floor apartment at Lloréns Torres. But the water rarely reaches her on the third floor. And she needs to buy water bottles so she can cook, which strains her limited budget.

AIDA LUZ PEÑA: [translated] They’ve left us without water, yet we still get the bill. Charge us for what we actually use when the water is on.
