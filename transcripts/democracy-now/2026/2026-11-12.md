## Scott Colom Wins Stunning Victory, Becomes Mississippi’s First Black Senator Since Reconstruction

Colom is set to become the state’s first Democratic senator in 44 years and Mississippi’s first Black senator since post-Civil War Reconstruction. Colom’s election after three years — comes three years after Cindy Hyde-Smith blocked Colom’s nomination to a federal judgeship. In 2024, President Trump won Mississippi by nearly 23 points.

AMY GOODMAN: On Wednesday, Scott Colom gave his victory speech at the historically Black Tougaloo College. He spoke just miles from where civil rights activist Medgar Evers was murdered in 1963 by a member of the Ku Klux Klan.

SEN.-ELECT SCOTT COLOM: God bless you. For me, this journey started with a prayer, y’all. And in the Mississippi, our prayers have often changed the course of history.

For people that don’t know, the first African American to be a United States senator was from the great state of Mississippi. His name was Hiram Revels. And the way he got to be a United States senator was he was a minister in Natchez, Mississippi. He was elected to be in the state House, and when they were trying to figure out who was going to do the opening prayer, someone suggested Hiram Revels. He gave a prayer that was so powerful that people at that convention decided that he had a voice to be United States senator. That’s an example of the power of prayer.

And throughout our history, there are so many people that lifted this state up through the power of prayer in very dark times. But we all know that prayer is only a part of our Mississippi faith tradition. You need faith.

And I’ll tell you, throughout this campaign, there were many times where I had to have faith. There were times that I couldn’t get donors to answer the phone; they did not believe a moment like this was possible. But I kept the faith. There were times when I did my first town hall in Jackson, Mississippi. Frank Figgers helped me organize it. Less than 10 people showed up. But I kept the faith. I kept the faith. There were times when I was tired, and I felt like I couldn’t keep going. But I kept the faith.

And that is another tradition of our state. There’s so many people that had faith in Mississippi at times where it seemed impossible, people like Medgar Evers. I can’t help but think about Medgar Evers on Veterans Day, a moment like this, where he served his country and came home to Mississippi, when they wouldn’t let him vote. But he had faith that Mississippi could make progress. He had faith that one day everybody would have the right to vote. He had faith that a day like this was possible. God bless you, Medgar. God bless you, Medgar. God bless you.

When you talk about faith, you can’t forget Fannie Lou Hamer, who had faith that her being sick and tired could move a nation to understand that places like Mississippi have to matter, who had faith that even after she became a national celebrity and she could have gone anywhere in the world, she came right back to Mississippi. She had faith, y’all. And right now we have several veterans of the civil rights movement who are a part of her tradition of faith. Let’s please give them a round of applause, y’all.

AMY GOODMAN: That was Scott Colom, Mississippi’s newly elected Democratic senator.

## From Ranked-Choice Voting to Dumping Electoral College: Rob Richie on Election Reform Movement

Rob Richie, president and founder of Expand Democracy and a senior adviser with FairVote, explains how ranked-choice voting has shaped the Alaska race and how it is “a system that promotes [candidates] reaching out, engaging, and, for voters, a lot of freedom.”

Richie adds that Peltola is “heavily favored to win at this point.”

AMY GOODMAN: This is Democracy Now!. I’m Amy Goodman, with Nermeen Shaikh.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: The Alaska Senate race is now the only Senate race yet to be called. Democratic challenger Mary Peltola is narrowly leading Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan with about 86% of the votes counted. The close race has drawn controversy because of a second candidate also named Dan Sullivan, retired teacher Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., who’s also on the ballot.

Alaska uses ranked-choice voting, a system where voters choose not only their top candidate, but can also indicate an order of preference for remaining candidates on the ballot. If no candidate wins a majority as the top choice, then second-choice votes are added to the totals and so on until there is a winner.

AMY GOODMAN: Alaska and Maine use ranked-choice voting for statewide elections, and it’s also used in 36 cities and three counties nationwide. And it’s popular outside of politics. The Oscar for Best Picture is decided by ranked-choice voting. So, how might ranked-choice voting impact the results in Alaska?

Our next guest is an expert on voting reforms, including ranked-choice voting. Rob Richie is with us, president and founder of Expand Democracy, a senior adviser with FairVote.

Let’s start in this first few minutes explaining how Alaska votes. Explain ranked-choice voting. And does it have anything to do with the fact that we don’t know who’s won the Alaska Senate race?

ROB RICHIE: Thank you, Amy. Great to be here, and fun to talk about ranked-choice voting in such a prominent way. It is the last Senate race to be decided.

The delay is entirely due to the fact that it’s a very rural state. Mail takes a while to get to where it needs to go, and they create space for ballots to come late. And so, that’s what we’re waiting for. It’s just ballots to be processed. All the ballots that have been processed, those 86%, have actually been — the data has been provided, so we can see what ranked-choice voting does with those ballots.
