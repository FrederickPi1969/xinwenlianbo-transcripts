So, we get new generations of AIs very quickly, without necessarily having the corresponding tech to understand what they’re doing and control what they’re doing.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: So, your response to Jacob Coxon’s story and what his position is?

JACOB SILVERMAN: Well, I think we’ve seen a lot of this in recent years of researchers quitting AI companies, sometimes going to another one right afterwards. We need to have responsible outside figures, and especially technically competent people, who can kind of audit these claims. And then we can figure out what to do from there. NERMEEN SHAIKH: Yeah. So, let’s go back to 2014, when the world-renowned, groundbreaking physicist Stephen Hawking issued a warning about the development of artificial intelligence. STEPHEN HAWKING: The primitive forms of artificial intelligence we already have have proved very useful, but I think the development of full artificial intelligence could spell the end of the human race. NERMEEN SHAIKH: So, that was Stephen Hawking, world-renowned physicist, speaking through a computer. He had Lou Gehrig’s disease. So, these warnings, then, about the risks of artificial intelligence are minimally 11 years old. Your final words?

JACOB SILVERMAN: I think, actually, they’re decades old, because a lot of the stuff goes back to the science fiction that people like Elon Musk or other people involved in this debate grew up reading.

RHIANNON GIDDENS: I don’t know. I mean, like, I’ve got DNA, whatever. But in terms of like paperwork, like, it kind of stops, you know, in the early 1800s, late 1700s. RHIANNON GIDDENS: Yeah, I — again, talking about collaborations, you know, everything that’s about the album artwork is a collaboration, from the original photograph by Ebru Yildiz. And what I love about that is that that’s American music. When you kind of get really close, you can identify this thread and this thread and this thread, and then when you just step back and listen to it, you hear this, like, cohesive, beautiful thing that is — the sum is greater than the — the whole is greater than the sum of its parts, right? It is — it becomes another thing, but it is made up of all these other — these threads, you know? So, it’s a nice metaphor. And also, I love artist to artist. Like, you know, there’s three artists involved in that, which I love.

AMY GOODMAN: What’s the Biscuits & Banjos Foundation that you’ve launched?

RHIANNON GIDDENS: Biscuits & Banjos is a festival that I started a couple years ago and is the idea — and we’ve turned it into a foundation so we can keep the work going. It’s the idea that there’s this cultural excavation that’s going on in music, but it’s also going on in foodways, you know? Like, where do we find — where we’ve been erased, you know, where do we find narratives that have been told that aren’t quite right, and how do we — how do we fix that?

And so, I’ve seen — you know, I’m very into food. During the pandemic, I had to learn how to make biscuits, because I was, like, locked in Ireland, and I was like, “I can’t get a biscuit here. I got to learn how to make them myself.” And then I got obsessed with the whole process and the history of biscuits and how they’re different from scones and all that kind of stuff. And so, I started thinking about that and reading these cookbooks. And I was like, “This is kind of something I feel like we’re all doing. So, instead of just, you know, a food festival or music festival, let’s combine them, and we can draw strength from each other and, like, see the shapes of the stories are the same.” You know? Because what do we bring with us when we move from one place to another? Music, food. You know? And so, you can see the history of people in their food and in their music.

And so, it just was an opportunity to put those two together, and it was just really a beautiful festival. Hopefully, we can do it again. But until we can, this foundation is a place where if people want to be involved in the building of that community, if they want to donate money, if they want to just be a part of this, that’s the place that they can go.

RHIANNON GIDDENS: Well, how long do you have? But it’s — I’ll say, very shortly, it’s in the amount — the ratio of ingredients. So, there’s usually less butter. And it’s also in the technique. So, biscuits, you fold. Scones, you just kind of pat and cut.

AMY GOODMAN: So, as we talk about narratives that are not often told, if you — you know, Dolly Parton just died. And if you talk about country music in America, I think people mainly think, or some people mainly think, white, a white world. But you’re bringing the diaspora into this. You’re redefining it all. Talk about that.

RHIANNON GIDDENS: Well, me and a lot of people, right? Like, I draw from a lot of academic folks’ work who are digging in the archives and doing these things. But I think the thing to remember is that whiteness is a construct as much as Blackness is, right? Whiteness was in response to the creation of Blackness to make a permanent underclass. So, what whiteness does is it flattens the differences between all the different European immigrants who came over. And, like, not all of them were seen in the same light, right? I mean, obviously, we know about the Irish. People don’t even know. Like, it was really bad, you know, in terms of where they sat in the hierarchy. But they were always still going to be eventually above the lowest, right? But in the beginning, they weren’t. So, it’s the idea of what you trade to step into whiteness.

And so, like, for me, the history of country music is very interesting, because it is a very diverse history. But what happens is that the recording industry flattens a lot of things, as well, and it supports sort of a white supremacist narrative, all of these things happening all at the same time. But before recording industry, it is like really an interesting story of what goes into it and how much commercialism actually has shaped our perspective and what we get of country music. Commercialism is wrapped up in there. And there are all of these things that come out of folk music and come out of working-class music, and then, you know, white supremacy and commercialism turn things and then create this false narrative.

But when you get into the weeds, and you start looking at the music, and you’re like — you know, because, like, if you’re talking about Irish people, are we talking about the Scots who were planted in the top of Ireland and then moved over, but then they have a mixture of both cultures? Are you talking about Irish Catholics, who were — you know what I mean? — like, who were coming over from the famine? Are you talking about — you know, there’s so many different people that are engaged in this. And then there’s the whole Black string bands, Black fiddlers, Black musicians, who were all over the South and all over the North, as well. So, it’s a very complicated history.

AMY GOODMAN: Can you talk about The Carolina Chocolate Drops —

RHIANNON GIDDENS: Yeah, Carolina Chocolate Drops was a kind of an attempt to remind people that the Black string band was a really big deal. It’s like it’s a part of all of the history up until the recording industry, you know, when we’re talking about American cultural history, going back to the Caribbean in the 1600s. Like, this is a long history of Black people playing fiddles and banjos, but, like, really being expected to be the community musicians, whether they were enslaved or whether they were not. But, like, you know, it’s not to say that non-Black people weren’t playing the fiddle and stuff — they were — but the the position they held in different communities, particularly, say, North Carolina, the Black string bands were playing all of the functions. You know, they played the plantation balls. They played the political rallies. They played the dances that went all night. They played all of these things. So, there’s a particular kind of cultural power that they held. And so, that was just very much sort of erased, you know, which is kind of incredible when you look at the history. They were everywhere.

So, The Carolina Chocolate Drops was a Black string band that I started with Dom Flemons and Justin Robinson back in 2006 as an attempt to, number one, take Joe Thompson’s music. He was like the last Black fiddler of the old tradition that we know of. You know, by the time we came along, he was it, and he was holding down the fort, last person in his family to do this music. And so, we learned his — you know, we kind of received his lineage, which is kind of incredible. So we took his music and also the history of Black string bands kind of out into the world for as long as we could.

AMY GOODMAN: — Ryan Coogler’s film. You also have worked with Ken Burns on Country Music and also The American Revolution. Can you talk about your experience with these projects? We interviewed Ryan so far back, when he was doing his first film —

RHIANNON GIDDENS: Yeah, I mean, I just — I just drop what I have. Like, I just go, “This is what I know.” And people do what they will with it, you know. And I understand that people are — you know, particularly when you’re talking about, like, mainstream movies, like, there’s a lot of moving parts. So, I just try to do what I’m hired to do, which is just to say, “Here’s all I know: blah.” You know? And then they take what works for them.

You know, working with Ken Burns on The Revolutionary — and Johnny Gandelsman did the music for that, you know, was one of the organizers of the music for that — was kind of a dream come true, because, you know, we were talking late 1700s, and it’s like, “OK, who can give me a early banjo tune from the late 1700s?” I’m like, “Pick me!” You know, like, I’ve been waiting my whole career for this moment, you know. So, it was kind of amazing to get to, like, offer up some really historically, like, connected things that I could then play, because I’m — this is the work that I do. You know, it’s not a recreation; it’s like a living tune, you know?

He’s folding. And I’m just like —

RHIANNON GIDDENS: Right. And so, that’s the pressure that’s on Ed Sheeran. And, like, I sympathize with him. He’s got all these people who have their jobs depending on these stadium shows.

RHIANNON GIDDENS: Right. And I understood that that was a difficult decision. But for me, it wasn’t a difficult decision. You know what I mean? Like, you’re a multimillionaire.

But anyway, look, the second thing is, is just what you said. I’m like, OK, Ed Sheeran, whatever, but if it doesn’t direct the attention to Robert Kraft and if it doesn’t direct the attention to the people who actually have the power who are doing these things, that’s — then it’s for naught. You know, so, like, we have to — we have to be informed. We have to inform ourselves and stop expecting celebrities to do it for us. Like, we have to go, “What do we stand for?” What are we putting our money towards? What are we putting our time towards?” The entertainment complex is massive. It’s too big. There’s too many people out there. Like, you know what I’m saying? Like, put me out of a job. Pick up a banjo, play some music, write your own song, not to perform for other people, but to just like process the emotions that are going on right now. People need art to do that, but they need to do it themselves. And, like, be inspired by somebody who’s a really good songwriter or whatever — I’m not saying that. There’s room for both.

AMY GOODMAN: In 2024, you led the Silkroad Ensemble in releasing Palestinian Lullabies.

RHIANNON GIDDENS: Mm-hmm, yeah. It’s like, you know, one of our members is Lebanese. Like, you know, being in a group that includes somebody from the Congo — right? — that includes somebody from the Armenian diaspora — right? — that includes somebody from India, like, being in a group where people are bringing the histories with them and telling personal stories, telling what this means — you know what I mean? — puts it all in a different light. And you’re just like, “If I can’t do something like this, you know, what am I doing? What am I doing?” Like, we now do “Wein a Ramallah” in our show, you know, and I’ve interpolated — inter — whatever. I’ve put in — I’ve written verses about Tulsa, Oklahoma, and Wilmington, 1898, into.

And it’s like, if we don’t pull our struggles together, if we don’t connect the struggles together and the overall larger patterns of extraction, the larger patterns of colonialism, the larger patterns of empire, the larger patterns of massive moneymaking — if we don’t connect those dots between the struggles, we’re never going to get anywhere. So, for me, that’s what music is for. That’s what art is for. And that’s why we have to make music with people from different backgrounds, because then we have that conversation, and then it turns into a song, and then it turns into a performance. I mean, you know.

AMY GOODMAN: And then there’s AI. There’s artificial intelligence.

RHIANNON GIDDENS: Yeah, it’s hard. It’s hard being an artist these days. And I’m like privileged beyond belief. Like, I’m not complaining, 'cause, like, I'm not getting a bomb dropped on my head. So, like, I’m OK. But, like, if we want to look at what we’re doing to our society with AI, like, look, we all use AI, you know, translating your thing or whatever. Like, it doesn’t — it’s not to say it can’t be a tool that we can use for good. But generative AI used in the place of making art, that’s not making art, right? It’s like the process should be the point. And with generative AI, the process is not the point. And I think that’s — you know, that’s the wrong path to go down.

AMY GOODMAN: So, where can people hear this album that you’re releasing now, Hope Is the Thing with Feathers?

RHIANNON GIDDENS: You can find them everywhere you find music. It’s a Nonesuch record. If you don’t want to buy the physical copy, you can go to Bandcamp and purchase a digital. It’s out there, vinyl, CDs.

RHIANNON GIDDENS: Yeah, there’s a tour in February 2027, and I’m doing a European tour shortly. And I’m always out there performing. But yeah.

AMY GOODMAN: And as you talk about what’s happening today, the final question is about President Trump saying he may well rip down the JFK Center for the Performing Arts, which many take as a metaphor for the performing arts, for the role of arts in our culture.

AMY GOODMAN: Unless he gets his name inscribed in the marble in the building.

RHIANNON GIDDENS: The new normal is insane. You know what I mean? Like, just consider what we’re talking about, you know, the tantrums of a person who has unbelievable amounts of power. I don’t really know — it’s really hard to articulate, but I will say it’s also tied to generative AI, because generative AI is controllable, and artists are not. Just look at the Macklemore thing, right? Why people want generative AI to make the music is because they can make it do whatever they want. And this is tied to taking the Kennedy Center down, because, like, artists, our job — and this is — you know, there is no apolitical stances, right? Because artists’ job — now, I’m not talking about — you know, I think part of the problem is that we have a commercial music industry where music is a commodified product, you know, and artists survive in that framework, but in spite of, not because of. Right? So, you sell what you gotta sell in order to make the statement that you’re trying to make, right?

But I think that artists’ job is to push on, you know, these fissures in society, is to make people uncomfortable, is to make people think about what they’re doing, is to make people think, “Who are we morally?” Right? So, people who are in power only want the things that are going to keep them in power and that are going to not challenge them. So, artists are going to challenge them, because that’s what we do.

So, yeah, let’s take it down. Take down the Kennedy Center. Take down, you know — what, 40% of new music on Spotify is AI? Like, you see, it’s happening. It’s happening. People don’t even know that they’re listing to AI music, you know? Like, the Grammys have — like, they’re not making a stand on how much of music is AI. We have to label — it should be labeled, it should be regulated, it should be — you know, so people can choose whether they opt in to AI or not. But this idea, this myth of inevitability — “Well, it’s going to happen anyway, so we’ve got to get in front of it” — no! Nobody agreed on this. Just the people with the money and the power agreed on this. You know what I mean? So, we deserve the opportunity to say, “No.”

AMY GOODMAN: Rhiannon Giddens, Pulitzer Prize- and Grammy-winning artist. Her new album is out today, Hope Is the Thing with Feathers. This is Democracy Now!, democracynow.org. I’m Amy Goodman.
