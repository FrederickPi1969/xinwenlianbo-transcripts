## “Toxic Nightmare”: 25 Years After 9/11, Juan González on His Predictions of Worker Illness & Death

Democracy Now! co-host Juan González reads from his book, Fallout, in which he extensively reported on the toxic nightmare around ground zero. “No one could guarantee the air around Ground Zero was safe because no one had ever confronted a disaster of such proportions,” González wrote in Fallout.

AMY GOODMAN: Despite official assurances after 9/11 that the air near the collapsed twin towers was safe, newly released records show health officials were still detecting asbestos up to half a mile from the site 10 months later. Over 170,000 pages of air quality reports, contamination data and internal city correspondence sat in 68 boxes in a city office for more than 20 years. City officials claim they were only found last year. New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani has ordered the material to be posted to a public online portal in time to commemorate this week’s 25th anniversary of the attacks on the World Trade Center. Mamdani’s office budgeted some $34 million as the administration in the coming months plans to review, redact and publish more previously unreleased documents linked to the environmental and health hazards in the aftermath of 9/11.

In the weeks and months that followed the attacks, former New York City Mayors Rudy Giuliani and Michael Bloomberg, as well as other city officials, reassured residents and workers it was safe to return to the area surrounding ground zero, concealing the dangers of lingering toxins in the air after the collapse of the twin towers. Today, more people are believed to have died from cancer, respiratory and other illnesses due to exposure to the toxins than the nearly 3,000 people killed on September 11th, 2001.

The historic release of the documents comes as part of litigation brought by the advocacy group 9/11 Health Watch, which sued New York City to force the publication of the records.

And, Brenda, we’re going to begin with you. Talk about what you were doing 25 years ago, what you understood to be the case in safety for the firefighters, and for you, as a firefighting woman, what that has meant. Who has been left out from being cared for today, 25 years later?

BRENDA BERKMAN: Well, good morning, Amy and Juan, and thank you very much for having us on here today. Excuse me.

Yeah, so, on 9/11, I was off duty, but I responded off duty to the World Trade Center, and I had a group of my own off-duty firefighters with me. It’s a long story. We got there right as the second tower, the north tower, collapsed. So, the air was completely full of dust and debris. And because we were off duty, we came down there without any protective equipment, no respiratory protection, no radios to communicate with, really no real tools. And we were — you know, we had to do what firefighters do — namely, look for anyone who might still be alive in the pile of rubble, and stretch hoses to put out the the fires, get water out of the Hudson because the water mains in Lower Manhattan had been disrupted when the towers fell down. It was really complete — a disaster beyond what anyone could possibly had imagined. But in those early days, we did what we had to do despite the lack of protective equipment, because we believed people to still be alive, and we wanted to get to them.

But as time went on and we realized that no one could still be alive, we went into a recovery effort, and that meant we were looking for remains. And people may not recall, but we were digging through the pile and using rakes to spread out all the debris to look for anyone’s remains. We were working alongside, you know, really heavy equipment, as time went on, that picked up big loads of debris and dumped it, you know, creating a cloud. This went on for 10 months.

And at first, there was no proper decontamination at all. We were walking around with this dust really all over the city, back to our homes. And for instance, in the case of my fire truck, which we were able to start using again after we got some of the dust out of the engine, was never properly decontaminated for 18 months. So, that meant that every time we turned on the AC or the heat system or changed a headlight, poof, dust blew out on us. So, this went on.

But the point I want to really make, and the other panel members are really going to be able to expand on this, is that because there was a relatively small number of women first responders — so, in the New York City Fire Department, only about 25 women were in the fire department, in the firehouses, out of 11,500 firefighters at the time of 9/11. And half of us were down there on the day of 9/11 and got exposed to all of those toxins that day, but also many, many days thereafter for the next 10 months. And in the police, it was slightly higher. In EMS, it was higher.

But the point being that people don’t understand that not every medical condition is covered by the medical monitoring program. And indeed, for the civilian people who are just now finding out that they should be part of the medical monitoring program, civilian people have to show a condition before they can start to be monitored, which means they could already have come down with cancer in an advanced stage before they exhibit symptoms or whatever. So, women’s cancers have been left out of the equation for being covered under the medical monitoring program, because the government was arguing that they didn’t have the proper statistical comparisons. And Lila has talked about this — I’m sure Marianne has also talked about this, and Bob knows — that, you know, they weren’t — there’s certain women’s conditions that have never been properly studied, so they don’t even know what the — you know, how many women come down with this stuff in the general population, so they don’t have anything to compare it with.

I mean, it’s really — I commend the mayor for releasing the documents that he has released. I hope that the city continues to do this going forward, that they release all of the documents, that we have full transparency, so we can know. We have no idea how many people, how many women especially, have died from 9/11-related conditions.

JUAN GONZÁLEZ: Yeah, I’d like to bring Lila Nordstrom into the conversation. Lila, you were a student at Stuyvesant High School at the time. LILA NORDSTROM: Yeah, absolutely. I think, you know, so often when people want to hear the story, they want to hear the story of the day itself, which was certainly traumatic. Marilena Christodoulou had already been engaged in a long conversation, as had the rest of the Parents’ Association, with the Department of Education about the safety of the building, and numerous false assurances were made about the condition that we would find the building in. And that included that, you know, they claimed that they had done a very deep cleaning, that there had been HEPA filters installed, and, you know, that students would find that the building was sort of decontaminated.

And when we got back, it was clear that that was not the case. We went through five police checkpoints on our way into the building on the first day. So, you know, we were essentially being paraded in front of television cameras as part of the messaging efforts to show that downtown was safe. But as soon as we got into the building, not only did we find that there were no HEPA filters and the building had never really been adequately cleaned, but also the school itself was located next to the barge that they were bringing all of the debris from the site to. So, that meant that the building was being recontaminated on a daily basis every day for months and months, not to mention the fact that fires burned at the site until, I think, the end of January. Other schools in the area pushed back their returns based on the experience that we were having at Stuyvesant. And they didn’t do a full cleaning of the building until the next summer.

AMY GOODMAN: And you’ve ended up writing a book, what happened to the Stuy students, to the Stuyvesant students. Again, Stuyvesant High School was on the Hudson River.

LILA NORDSTROM: Yeah, and one of the reasons I wrote the book was because I wanted not only there to be an accounting of the story, because it felt like the national narrative had really excluded survivors from the conversation, but I also wanted there to be a story of the advocacy efforts and the fact that we were involved in the subsequent advocacy efforts that secured the Zadroga Act on three different occasions. I think there’s a perception that, you know, that was a story that only involved first responders and that the community really didn’t play a role in that process. But the community, which includes 300,000 people that were downtown, played a role in that process, has access to those programs in a different way than responders, but was an important part of that advocacy effort, as well.

JUAN GONZÁLEZ: And could you talk about the students, and even faculty, that became sick? I think one teacher at Stuyvesant immediately requested a transfer as a result of his illnesses immediately after getting back into the school.

LILA NORDSTROM: Yeah, he was actually my math teacher, and I — it led to, well, really the end of my experience as a math student, because things fell apart from there. But there were a lot of reports early on of, you know, respiratory conditions, nosebleeds, headaches and that sort of thing. The New York Times reported that the lines for the nurse’s office were stretching around the school in the immediate months after 9/11. But we were told, “Oh, those will only be temporary. There will be no long-term health consequences.” And I think, you know, as we all know, that didn’t turn out to be the case.

I started StuyHealth in 2006 as a lifelong asthmatic who had just been diagnosed with GERD. I was finding it difficult to afford treatment for just those simple chronic conditions. And then a classmate of mine came forward and said, “Well, I just got diagnosed with lymphoma. Could that be related?” And we started to hear from other classmates that some of those earlier cancers that have shorter latency periods were starting to appear in the student population at Stuyvesant. Obviously, now we’re seeing cancers that have slightly longer latency periods. And so, in the aftermath, we were helping flight attendants. We lost 20% of aviation workers overnight, furloughed, gone, those jobs gone. We went through a series of bankruptcies. So, that is really, to me — this has become, obviously, my life’s work. But 9/11 is alive and well with me every single day, because “never forget” means also understanding that we have to define those problems, that we have to fix them, and that we have to stand together, just like those passengers and the crew did on Flight 93, with urgency, to fight the problems that we have today.

## 25 Years After 9/11: Cover-Up of Air Quality Risk Proved to Be “Death Sentence” for Thousands

JUAN GONZÁLEZ: Yeah, I’d like to bring Bob Hennelly into the conversation. Bob, you were there. You have been reporting on this issue now for 25 years. I’m wondering if you could talk about your own experience, and also if you could deal with this issue of Mayor Giuliani developing a national reputation in the aftermath of 9/11 as “America’s mayor,” when some of the major decisions that caused so many lives to be lost were made by him, including the radios that the firefighters were using that didn’t work and the command center that Giuliani put in the World Trade Center and, therefore, was destroyed and was not available to keep firefighters and police in communication with each other.

BOB HENNELLY: Well, Juan, first of all, I want to thank you for all you did, and just thank you for keeping on and getting it right, right from the very beginning, so we can build this legacy forward.

I will tell you that anyone who really knows the New York City Civil Service, and I spent many years at The Chief-Leader, which has been around since 1897, knows that Mayor Giuliani’s presidential bid was ended, to a large degree, by a caravan of buses that were filled with widows of FDNY firefighters and people closest to the people that gave 343 lives on that day. And subsequently, 600 have perished, both retiree and active.
