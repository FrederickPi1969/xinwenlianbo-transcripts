TERRY STRADA: Thank you, I appreciate that. And thank you for having me.

AMY GOODMAN: Why don’t we start off by talking about your call on Friday—and it was quite unusual for—while people were naming those who died 25 years ago—demanding that—calling out Trump and Vice President Vance and demanding that the Trump administration recognize Saudi Arabia’s role in the 9/11 attacks. TERRY STRADA: I want the truth to be recognized. We have already taken an abundance of evidence into a courtroom. A federal judge has ruled in our favor that the evidence is credible and that we can go to trial with the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. I really wasn’t calling out President Trump; I was calling on him to help us. They are the ones that began the decades-long cover-up of the role that the kingdom played and they are the ones that have harmed the families all these years.

AMY GOODMAN: Can you talk about the lawsuit, who brought it and what it is calling for?

TERRY STRADA: The families brought the lawsuit on in 2002. That is when we filed a lawsuit against Saudi officials, Saudi state-run banks, state-run charities. Over time, through discovery, the evidence all pointed back to the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia—the _kingdom_—for the role that they played, that their agents were directly involved with providing the support network that was needed here in the United States for Al Qaeda to carry out the treacherous attacks of September 11. So we are holding the kingdom accountable for attacking America. And because that evidence has now been in a courtroom, it is the president—current President Trump’s—responsibility to tell the kingdom that the evidence points directly at them and that they can no longer hide and that they need to confront the evidence and work things out with the families. Enough is enough.

BOB GRAHAM: The FBI, although they were asked to provide the congressional inquiry all the information they had about 9/11, did not tell us about these extensive contacts in San Diego. AMY GOODMAN: Can you talk, Senator Graham, about the 9/11 Commission and the suppressed pages of that commission, what you believe is in them, and if it links to the Saudi royal family?

BOB GRAHAM: The suppressed pages were in the congressional joint inquiry. We worked diligently throughout 2002 to gather as much of the information as we could and to make recommendations. We had an 800-plus-page report, one chapter of which—which related primarily to the role of the Saudis in 9/11—was totally censored. Every word of that chapter has been denied to the American people. One of the reasons that I wrote a novel Keys to the Kingdom was because I felt that that was a means of beginning to tell the American people some of the things which they have not been able to be told because of the degree of cover-up that has surrounded the Saudi activities in the United States prior to 9/11.

TERRY STRADA: Right. I worked alongside former Senator Bob Graham to ensure that those 28 pages were declassified. And what he held back was to protect the Saudis. This has been the pattern of every administration. But before we get to what the 28 pages revealed—under the Bush administration, Director Mueller, FBI, withheld the most damning evidence that the agents of the kingdom were directly involved in the September 11 attacks. He withheld it from the San Diego field office that was investigating Omar al-Bayoumi and the connection to the Saudis. They also withheld it from the 9/11 Commission. So the 9/11 Commission had a report from the joint inquiry with an entire chapter excised, and then they had the 9/11 Commission and the FBI did not give them the evidence.

That evidence now has been uncovered. We had to go to the Metropolitan Police services in London because that is where all of this evidence was confiscated 10 days after September 11. And sadly and unfortunately and really cruelly, they withheld it for 20 years. It wasn’t until our lawsuit pried it open through discovery. So that’s the problem that we had with the Bush administration.

The Obama administration gave us the 16 pages—or, gave us the 28 pages—still redacted. But the most egregious thing under that administration was when Secretary Clinton, Hillary Clinton, she worked with the Supreme—or, I’m sorry—she worked and denied our lawsuit being heard by the Supreme Court. She made every effort she could to block that and she was successful. That’s another harmful thing to the community. Then President Obama vetoed the Justice Against Sponsors of Terrorism Act. We worked on that bill for seven years. The purpose of that bill was so that the victims could walk into a courtroom and show our evidence. We’ve always wanted to be heard on the merits. President Obama didn’t think that was a good thing, however Congress came back and overwhelmingly overrode his veto.

So we have just had these blocks, these hurdles, these damaging decisions made by those administrations. That’s why I said what I said on Friday morning. Because they were all there. It was the perfect opportunity to look at all of them and say, “What you did was wrong, but we have managed to get it into a courtroom anyway, and now we need President Trump to act on it.”

AMY GOODMAN: So this is clearly a bipartisan affair. I want you to stay on with us as we talk to the ProPublica reporter Tim Golden, but I wanted to finally ask you, why do you think the U.S. has protected Saudi Arabia all these years?
