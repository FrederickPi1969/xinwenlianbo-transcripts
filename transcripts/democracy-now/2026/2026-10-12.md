The demonstrations “represent an outpouring of anger, of frustration at what many Indians feel is the fact that their democracy has been taken away,” says Shoaib Daniyal, political editor of Scroll, who was arrested on Saturday along with 7,000 other protesters. He points out that “the deletions have affected Muslims far more than they have affected other Indians.”

On Monday, in one of largest recent protests in India’s capital of New Delhi, India’s main opposition leader, Rahul Gandhi of the Congress Party, led a march to the Supreme Court of India. “This march has become a people’s march, with all of them heading towards this constitutional authority, which they feel should safeguard the rights of Indian people, especially this very basic right to vote,” says Jahnavi Sen, managing editor of The Wire.

ANJALI KAMAT: In India, the right-wing government of Narendra Modi is facing its biggest challenge yet in 12 years in power, as tens of thousands of people have taken to the streets to protest what they call vote theft. At issue is the deletion of 130 million names from the voter rolls, and the vast majority of those deletions are believed to be opposed to Prime Minister Modi’s ruling BJP party.

The protests are being led by students and the youth Cockroach movement and joined by leaders from the opposition parties. They’re demanding the resignation of the chief election commissioner, Gyanesh Kumar, and an overhaul of the electoral system. The Cockroach movement started after the chief justice of India’s Supreme Court compared unemployed youth to cockroaches earlier this year.

Today, in one of largest recent protests in India’s capital of New Delhi, India’s main opposition leader, Rahul Gandhi of the Congress Party, is leading a march to the Supreme Court of India. Speaking to reporters earlier today, Gandhi reiterated the call for the election commissioner to resign.

RAHUL GANDHI: Narendra Modi is blocking the whole town, is putting 30,000-40,000 police people outside. For what? Because the opposition is asking one question: Prove to us that you have not cheated in the election. Prove to us that you’ve not cheated in the election.

## Indigenous Peoples’ Day: Leonard Peltier on Resistance, New Film & Fighting for Freedom

Peltier is a longtime political prisoner who spent nearly half a century behind bars for a crime he says he did not commit — the 1975 fatal shooting of two FBI agents. In the final hours of his administration last year, President Biden commuted Peltier’s sentence, but his conviction has not been overturned, and he remains under home confinement on the Turtle Mountain Band of Chippewa Reservation in Belcourt, North Dakota.

“It was one crime against the justice system after another in order to frame Leonard for the assassination of these two federal agents,” says David France, co-director of the new documentary Free Leonard Peltier. “And although he and his team were able to prove over and over that every bit of evidence used against him in his conviction was brought into question, he was never able to get a retrial.”

So, everything they — when they put up evidence that’s false, that’s not a hearing. And that’s been proven over and over, that the grand jury hearing was fake. They're — Trump and these organizations, they’re talking about doing away with our reservations and our treaties and everything else. That’s it. That’s it for us. If we lose those, we’re done. Indigenous Day is another day for us — excuse me — to express these feelings, to let the world know that we’re still here, and we’re going to stay here. DAVID FRANCE: The making, I guess, really, for me, goes back almost 50 years, because, as you said, I was a witness to the news reports around the events of 1975, and then the efforts to find Leonard, which was and remains, I think, the largest and longest FBI manhunt in history. It’s a story that galvanized my understanding of justice, social justice.

And when I realized that Leonard was approaching his 50th anniversary, I visited him in Coleman in central Florida and asked him for permission to tell his story. And it was a conversation that took some time before he agreed that we could do it. And one of those conditions was that we put together an Indigenous team of filmmakers to lead in the making of the film.

AMY GOODMAN: And when you talk about the longest manhunt in FBI history, Leonard had escaped to Canada. Meanwhile, Bob Robideau and Dino Butler were put on trial.

DAVID FRANCE: They were found not guilty by reason of self-defense.

AMY GOODMAN: And so, the search by the state was for another jury, another court.

DAVID FRANCE: What’s really clear from the record is that after they lost that case against those two men, those co-defendants, they were — they were prepared to do anything to convict Leonard Peltier. And it was one crime against the justice system after another in order to frame Leonard for the assassination of these two federal agents. And although he and his team were able to prove over and over that every bit of evidence used against him in his conviction was brought into question, he was never able to get a retrial. He was never able to get a hearing before the Supreme Court. And it had become for him, and for anybody watching it, one of the most Kafkaesque experiences in American history,

AMY GOODMAN: Leonard Peltier, there was a moment when President Biden — this is before he commuted your sentence — when President Biden apologized for the residential schools that people like you, Indigenous children, were put in. Can you talk about growing up as a Native American in this country and what happened to you as a child?

LEONARD PELTIER: I was just — I grew up in an era when it was — we were just coming out of the times when this was actually a concentration camp. We couldn’t leave the reservation without a pass, and that’s even to go to shopping for groceries on the border towns of our nation. We’re a nation. We’re not reservations. We’re nations. And we had to pay for that pass. I never got to go to town 'til I was about 6 years old, I think, somewhere there, because we had to pay for it. We didn't have money to pay for stuff like that.

So, the racism was just so out. It was so out, and, I mean, it was just so obvious. It was not even trying to — they didn’t even try to hide it. Of course, to us younger people, they started — they would — they wouldn’t be so vocal about it, because they’d get their goddamn [bleep] kicked, and because we’d start fighting back. My generation started fighting back real strong.

AMY GOODMAN: Do you remember the first day you were taken to a residential school and what these residential schools were, where you were taken, where you were living?

LEONARD PELTIER: My grandfather died in 1952, who was my hero. He was a very cultural person. He was very, very popular. And he passed. And my grandmother went up to the agency to see if they can get — my grandfather and grandmother was raising myself, my sister Betty and my cousin Pauline. And she went up to the agency to try to get some help, because she had no way to support us and feed us and everything. And so, in retaliation, they took us to the boarding school in Wahpeton, North Dakota. I mean, I tried to escape there, from there, at 10 years old. I fell in the Red River and almost drowned. So, that was the type of conditions we had.

When we got there, they took us to the barber room, the barber shop, and they buzz cut all our hair off. And then they took — they stripped us of all of our clothes, took our — what clothes we had, and poured DDT over us. Some people said that they had — in their generation, they poured kerosene over them. But DDT is poison. And then we went to the shower and took a shower after that. But the brutality was — it was so open, for the least violation of any kind of rule. And that’s what we — and all we were being taught was that we were saved by the white man who came to our shores, because we were a bunch of savages here and we didn’t know nothing and we didn’t — I mean, it was just outright racism.

DAVID FRANCE: It’s absolutely true. It was the first and only time that the FBI has staged a demonstration ever in public. And it was threatening, and it was certainly very effective.

AMY GOODMAN: I thought it was very interesting, the part of the film that deals with James Reynolds. And I wanted to ask, Leonard, you about this. AMY GOODMAN: — based on FBI, judicial and prosecutorial misconduct.

LEONARD PELTIER: Well, he wasn’t the only one. The regional director of the FBI, Don Edwards, he was the third in line to be the director of the FBI. He was a committed supporter. They asked him how come he was supporting me, and he said, “Well, I work there. Reynolds is just one of the latest ones that in the last 10 years came forward and mentioned about the illegal things. Judge Heaney, he was the main judge in the 8th Circuit Court of Appeals on the murder weapon. You have to ask yourself what possible national security issue could be involved in this FBI campaign against Leonard and campaign against the American Indian Movement. And I can think of none, except what we discovered, that the United States declared that it was in the national interest, strategic national interest, to claim access to the minerals at Pine Ridge, including the minerals necessary for making atomic bombs. And that might be their explanation. It certainly wouldn’t hold up today, but — and yet, they still maintain that they should not be released to the public.

AMY GOODMAN: When I did the question-and-answer after the film here in New York, talking to Holly Cook Macarro, who was a lobbyist with the NDN Collective fighting for your freedom, she talked about the importance of Deb Haaland, who’s now running for governor of New Mexico, and Xavier Becerra. LEONARD PELTIER: Well, it was kind of, I would say, soothing to know that we did have some victories. We are having some victories. They just passed a law here six months ago, where — maybe a little bit longer — where if a Native woman is killed, they have to investigate immediately. Just think of that, too. I mean, you know, they didn’t have to investigate when an Indian woman was killed. And this is 2025. They just tried to pass a law where we can’t buy back our own land. I mean, we beat the law; we defeated it. But nevertheless, this is what we’re still facing today — today, Indigenous Day, the day that’s supposed to be honoring us. And, you know, at least — at least leave us alone. Let us lead our own lives. Let us do our own thing. We’re not — we’re not here to overthrow the United States government. That’s not what we’re here for. We just want to be left alone. Let us live our own lives. Let us have our own society we’ve had for thousands of years. What the hell? I mean, we don’t want to be part of a government that’s killing women, children, babies, and bombing every damn thing that they can, and talking about annihilating a race of people.

LEONARD PELTIER: That’s right. Right. Who wants to be part of that? I don’t. We don’t. We don’t want to be part of that [bleep]. That’s what happened to us. So, you know, this is — we’re supposed to celebrate Indigenous Day when we see this stuff still going on and still happening to us?

AMY GOODMAN: Leonard, I was hoping you’d be sitting here at the table. I was hoping that when the film came out in New York City, that you’d be there addressing the audience. AMY GOODMAN: And, David France, what are you hoping to do with this film, Free Leonard Peltier?

DAVID FRANCE: Bringing Leonard’s story to the public again, because I think many years ago his story was very well known, to remind people that the history, the horrible history, genocidal history, of the United States government against Indigenous people is not a thing of the distant past. AMY GOODMAN: That’s David France, co-director, with Jesse Short Bull, of the new Netflix documentary Free Leonard Peltier, and longtime Indigenous activist Leonard Peltier himself, his sentence commuted last year in the final hours of the Biden administration after almost 50 years in prison. ## Headlines for October 12, 2026

The Pentagon has once again raised its count of U.S. troops killed and wounded in operations tied to the U.S. war on Iran. The Pentagon quietly updated the figures without a public announcement on Friday. The Defense Casualty Analysis System website now lists 21 service members killed, up from 18, and 865 wounded, 98 more than in August.

This comes as 74 Democrats in Congress are pushing the Pentagon for answers about U.S. airstrikes in Iran that killed civilians. They’re focusing on a September strike on a wedding in Iran that killed five people, three adults and two children, and injured about 70 others. In a letter to Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, the Democrats wrote, “We are deeply alarmed by the continued pattern of civilian casualties resulting from U.S. operations in the war in Iran, and the apparent lack of action and accountability from the Department. Acknowledging such incidents, accepting responsibility, and learning from them makes our forces stronger, more effective, and an example to the world. When we fail to do so, it has the exact opposite effect.”

In Gaza, two Palestinians were killed today as an Israeli drone struck vehicles in the southern city of Rafah. This follows an Israeli airstrike on Sunday that killed three Palestinians in a tent in the al-Mawasi area of Khan Younis, which Israel previously designated as a so-called safe zone. On Saturday, separate Israeli strikes killed at least five Palestinians in Gaza. Those attacks came on the first anniversary of the U.S.-brokered so-called ceasefire agreement. Palestinian officials say Israel violated the deal more than 5,100 times in attacks that killed at least 1,471 Palestinians and injured nearly 5,200 others. This is Nael al-Buhaisi, whose home was damaged in an Israeli strike.

Nael Al-Buhaisi: “Where would they go? They will stay in the streets. Where? There’s no shelter or anything. All empty areas are filled. All is filled with tents in the north, the south and in the middle, where we are living. We started living this tragedy, as well. There is no choice left for humans. There is no desire to live with this situation that we are in.”

Russian attacks on Ukraine killed at least six ​civilians on Sunday, including a strike ​on a grocery ​store in Kharkiv that left three people dead and 20 injured. That followed Russian missile strikes on southeastern Ukraine on Saturday that killed 20 people, including three children. Elsewhere, Russia bombed a power plant in the city of Kharkiv on Sunday, setting it on fire. Speaking to ABC News, President Zelensky said he would organize elections and stop attacking Russian energy sites if President Vladimir Putin agreed to a 60-day ceasefire.

President Volodymyr Zelensky: “Tell Putin: Give us two months, 60 days, of total ceasefire, 60 days, and we will organize elections. Of course, we will be ready for any choice of our people. Exactly. … We are ready for ceasefire. We are ready to begin from any kind of steps of deescalation. We are ready for energy ceasefire, not to attack energy, both sides.”

In India, protests against the right-wing government of Prime Minister Narendra Modi rocked Delhi over the weekend. Police detained thousands of people on Saturday, including the three leaders of the youth-led Cockroach Janta Party, Abhijeet Dipke, Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka. Authorities had intercepted their flight from Mumbai to New Delhi to prevent a large demonstration. A magistrate judge ordered them released Sunday on the condition that they not “breach peace” for one year. After their release, the leaders vowed to continue protesting, even as they accused Modi’s government of waging war on its own citizens and creating an atmosphere of fear. Today, Rahul Gandhi, the leader of the opposition Congress Party, is leading a march toward the Supreme Court.

Neha Bora: “So, I think there is hope, and that’s the only reason why people have the courage to come down onto the streets now, because there is hope that not only can you fight against these people who rule, who claim to rule with an iron fist — and they did rule with an iron fist — not only can you fight them, you can also defeat them in some way or form. And this hope is infectious, and that is why it keeps spreading in this entire country. Fear is infectious, of course, but hope is also infectious.”

The Justice Department is looking into whether the five major TV networks that make up the White House TV press pool broke federal antitrust law last month. In September, news departments at ABC, CBS, CNN, NBC and Fox News briefly halted their shared rotation of White House coverage after CNN was pulled from a scheduled assignment. The networks reportedly found out about the probe on Friday, when the Justice Department’s Antitrust Division sent letters demanding records of internal communications. Last month, CNN, MS NOW and Politico sued over their ban from the White House. A Trump-appointed federal judge issued a temporary order requiring the White House to restore their access, with a ruling on a potential longer-lasting injunction expected on Tuesday.

A federal judge has ordered the Trump administration to stop running government-funded TV ads that promote the president, finding they are likely unlawful. In her ruling, U.S. District Judge Jennifer Rochon of New York blocked the Trump administration from spending public money to distribute the ads. She also said it must cancel “any slot, placement, reservation, or arrangement for the airing or dissemination of the challenged advertisements.” The ruling took effect immediately, covering any ads that were set to air later on Sunday night. But the Justice Department in a weekend filing said the ad buy was slated to end on Sunday. The ads began running on national TV in September using millions of dollars from the Department of Homeland Security.
