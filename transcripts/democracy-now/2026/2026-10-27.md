## “Anti-Fascist Economics”: Isabella Weber on How Inequality Fuels Extremism & Erodes Democracy

Economist Isabella Weber’s new book, Anti-Fascist Economics: In Defense of Affordability, Dignity, and Democracy, warns economic insecurity can fuel the rise of authoritarian movements, and argues protecting democracy requires making life affordable for everybody.

Anti-fascist economics is “not about getting rid of the market,” says Weber. “It’s about having public players that behave differently, and by behaving differently, changing the dynamic of the market as a whole and literally creating an alternative so that people are not dependent on often very concentrated and private providers as the only choice,” she explains, providing current examples of governments enacting such policies from New York City to Mexico City to Rome.

AMY GOODMAN: This is Democracy Now!, democracynow.org. I’m Amy Goodman at Colorado State University, Rocky Mountain Student Media, CTV in Fort Collins, with Nermeen Shaikh in New York.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: With the U.S. midterm elections just a week away, the issue of affordability has emerged as a major campaign issue, as voters express frustration over the rising cost of groceries, housing, gasoline and other basic necessities. Data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics show workers’ wages have failed to keep pace with inflation over the past year.

President Trump has blamed his predecessors, denied the current affordability crisis and defended his record. Here is Trump speaking earlier this month in Denton, Texas.

PRESIDENT DONALD TRUMP: They’re the ones that caused the inflation. I’m the one that’s bringing it down. And we have to be able to get that word out somehow, because they’re using the word “affordability.”

AMY GOODMAN: President Trump campaigned on a promise to bring down prices, but his tariffs, the war in Iran and rising energy costs have intensified concerns about affordability. Across the country, families are struggling to make ends meet, while oil and gas companies have reported record profits during recent periods of price shocks. This is Trump speaking in Perry, Georgia, this weekend.

PRESIDENT DONALD TRUMP: And while oil prices are temporarily high and will be falling dramatically in a short period of time, as soon as that war is over, because there’s so much oil — all in the Hormuz Strait, there’s ships waiting to come. We get them out so fast. The United States oil and gas business is making hundreds of billions of dollars. …

No, I could just see the fake headlines: “President Trump is talking about all the money they’re making while people are suffering at the pump.” That’ll be the headline. But I said, “I don’t give a damn. I’m going to say it: We’re making a freaking fortune.” Our country is getting stronger and stronger all the time. And those pump prices are going to come down.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: Our next guest warns economic insecurity can fuel the rise of authoritarian movements, and says protecting democracy requires making life affordable for everybody. We’re joined now by the acclaimed economist Isabella Weber, author of the new book, Anti-Fascist Economics: In Defense of Affordability, Dignity, and Democracy. She is the founding director of Essential Economics and associate professor of economics at the University of Massachusetts Amherst.

Welcome, Professor Weber, to Democracy Now! Why don’t we just begin with you outlining the argument you make in the book, in the context of what Trump said in this introduction that we played and Trump’s overall position?

So, each of these shocks that we are going through is kind of a choice point. Now, when it comes to the Iran war, it’s actually an engineered shock that has been literally started by the United States government, right? And what we see, that in the wake of this shock, oil prices have been shooting up, as you have just shown in the trailer. So, this has meant that for ordinary Americans, the price at the pump has been up 45%. But at the same time, if we look at what’s happened to profits in the petroleum and coal industry, it’s actually been up 415%. So we have record oil and gas profits that are even above what happened in 2022 in the wake of the Russian attack on Ukraine. Then, the question is, of course, if Trump says, you know, we as a nation are winning, he’s pretending as if the nation was united, but the reality is that 50% of those profits go to the top 1%. The bottom 50% only get around 1%. So, for each of these shocks, you know, on the one hand, you have a deepening of the affordability crisis, but, on the other hand, you have massive increases in profits that go to the richest of the rich. So, it’s a combination of an economic insecurity crisis and an acceleration of an inequality crisis with concentrations of private economic power that is no longer compatible with any meaningful notion of democracy.

So, that’s the starting point of the book. And if we apply a neoliberal economics playbook, then we say, you know, in moments of shocks, let the market do its job, which delivers the kind of outcomes that we have just looked at. What I’m arguing is that to defend democracy in this world of shocks and crises, we need democratic states that stand up and protect people’s basic livelihoods. And that also means limiting windfall profits, means limiting this acceleration in private economic power and actually stepping up against that. So, it’s about a floor under people’s livelihoods and a ceiling on extreme forms of wealth concentration.

AMY GOODMAN: And how exactly would you carry out this anti-fascist economics, Professor Weber? What is President Trump doing when he says — for example, he dismisses affordability concerns, says he doesn’t give a damn about rising prices, but then at the same time claims that he is bringing prices down? What should he be doing?

ISABELLA WEBER: I mean, what Trump is doing is treating the global economy and people’s livelihoods as collateral damage in his war — right? — and his political project. And that, to me, is the authoritarian kernel of what he’s up to.

What he should be doing, or anyone in power should be doing, I mean, not start illegal wars, to begin with, but then, you know, put a price ceiling on essentials and a price floor concretely for the fossil fuel industry. So, you could be defending such a price ceiling and a price floor with the strategic reserves that we already have, with the releases from stockpiles through the International Energy Agency that we already have. So, you could be actually having a multilateral effort to defend, you know, a price ceiling so that prices do not explode in the ways in which they have. This is a short-term intervention, a short-term stabilization.

Of course, in the medium run, we need to get out of fossil fuels, because fossil fuels is what makes our economies volatile, which is at the core of the climate crisis. So, the time that we buy with price interventions, like the one that I’ve just mapped out, needs to be used for large-scale investments in renewables, and the buildout of renewables has to be done in a way that we do not replicate the power structures that we currently have in the energy system.

In the book, I actually go back to the debates of the 1940s, where the question of an economics of “never again” was really, you know, a central question across schools of thought. And one of the main lessons was that the essentials are too important to be controlled by private companies, because whoever is in charge of our water, our energy, our food system, our housing infrastructure, and so on, is actually in command of the fate of society and economy. So, therefore, for democracy to be resilient to authoritarian threats, we actually need public control over those essentials.

And energy is the obvious — is one of the obvious points to start, but it is about the full range of essentials, where what you do for every essential depends on the sector. So, for example, for food, I think that what Mexico did with the so-called basket model is a very good approach. They defined a basket of food essentials, then drew up an agreement between the government and the largest grocery companies in the country, and the companies had to commit to keeping the price of this basket as a whole stable. And the president herself would actually, in press conferences, report on how companies have been doing in keeping that price at that agreed level. Now, what this is doing is, you know, saying that it’s the job of the head of the state to make sure that people can afford the basics, starting from food, and it’s her job to hold companies accountable to actually, you know, hold up this agreement.

Now, this is the exact opposite logic of what we have been seeing under neoliberalism, where people have been told that, you know, democracy is the greatest system that has ever existed — which is, of course, right — and then, in the second sentence, unfortunately, even when it comes to, you know, your most basic needs, like being able to afford food, there’s nothing that we can do for you.

Anti-fascist economics takes the opposite stance and says, no, it’s the job of democratic states to make sure that everybody can afford the basics, and policies, such as this basket policy, is something that can be done in the here and now, and then has to be backed up with structural policies that change the ways how essentials are being provisioned to tackle the power concentration and tackle the climate crisis, because, actually, the essential sectors account for large parts of emissions. So, decarbonization is also a question of transforming essential sectors.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: Well, let’s go to what our very own New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani, what steps he’s taken with respect to affordability. And that was, of course, one of the central tenets of his campaign. Mamdani announced that the city’s first municipally owned grocery store will open in the Hunts Point section of the South Bronx next year. The plan is part of a broader $70 million initiative to open one city-owned store in each of the five boroughs. Under the proposal, the stores would pay no rent or property taxes, keeping overhead costs low. The plan still requires city council approval. This is Mayor Mamdani.

MAYOR ZOHRAN MAMDANI: Standing here this morning, I cannot help but think of the words of our 40th president, Ronald Reagan. He famously said, “The nine most terrifying words in the English language are ’I’m from the government, and I’m here to help.’ It’s a good quote, but I disagree. I think nine more terrifying words are actually 'I worked all day and can't feed my family.’ We are going to use the power of government to lower prices and make it easier for New Yorkers to put food on the table.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: So, that is Mayor Mamdani, as we said earlier, who campaigned on affordability. So, one of the questions that arises as I was reading your book is that, on the one hand, you have states — you take what you call anti-fascist economics, which is to say that there are economic policies in place, some of which you were just talking about, that create the conditions for the rise of fascist movements; on the other hand, in an exactly — in a more local sense, where those same economic policies are in place, we can have a movement like the movement that elected Mamdani. And it’s not just in New York City. We have democratic socialist mayors elsewhere in the country, including Seattle, and then also in Paris, Barcelona, etc. So, how do you explain this difference?

ISABELLA WEBER: Yeah, I mean, from where I stand, anti-fascist economics is a very flexible, very nimble strategy. It basically tries to demonstrate that an alternative to the neoliberal playbook is possible in the here and now under the concrete conditions that we face, whatever the level of governance we are looking at. So, if we take the example of New York City and the question of food, then the creation of municipal grocery stores is one of those steps in the direction of demonstrating that the state can deliver for people’s affordability, right? Now, creating a grocery store is, of course, a project that will take several years, even with the greatest level of determination and, you know, the most skillful project management. So, in the meantime, you also have to do things that deliver in the here and now. That’s Zohran’s focus on potholes, right? That’s the sidewalks that are no longer icy because he organized for people to clean them up, right? So, you have to do these steps that are effective in the here and now, while building out the infrastructures for greater public control, in the name of an economy that serves life and affordability and, ultimately, dignity. So, in that sense, you know, wherever you can operate, you take a step in the right direction, and the municipal level is a very important one.

And thank you for bringing up that it’s not just New York City, right? I was at the Urban 20 that Zohran Mamdani hosted, and I was actually really impressed by seeing how, across the world, cities are taking action. In Mexico City, there are the urban UTOPÍAS. There are projects to actually integrate rural small producers with urban consumers through public provisioning of wholesale and retail infrastructure. In Istanbul, there are people’s kitchens. In Rome, they, you know, reintroduced public provisioning of sports facilities for youth to actually compete with the private providers, and thereby drive down prices. And that’s an important point, because both for the grocery stores as well as for the public provisioning of youth programs, it’s not necessarily about, you know, getting rid of public — sorry, private providers. It’s not about getting rid of the market. It’s about having public players that behave differently, and by behaving differently, changing the dynamic of the market as a whole and literally creating an alternative so that people are not dependent on often very concentrated private providers as the only choice they face.

Now, for something like healthcare, I think the situation is different. I mean, healthcare is something where we literally do not have a choice, right? Like, what kind of youth program you join, you have some choice. Where you go for grocery shopping, you have some choice. But if you have a heart attack, you don’t have a choice where you’re going to go. You’re just going to do whatever keeps you alive, right? Which is why for healthcare, I would not be arguing for a public option, but I would be arguing for Medicare for All as a, you know, all-in public effort. So, the idea really is to say, for each sector, we have to understand how these markets work, how the need side work, how the supply structures are, and then find sectorally targeted strategies.

AMY GOODMAN: And can you also talk about, Professor Weber, the enormous concentration of wealth? When you look at President Trump’s second inauguration, the men standing behind him, as they are called, the billionaire tech bros, what does this mean? You say affordability is not just an electoral issue; it’s a democracy issue. And how do you deal with this disparity as people are struggling to make ends meet?

ISABELLA WEBER: Yeah, I mean, the concentration of wealth is, of course, staggering. And in fact, in the ’30s, economists warned that this level of wealth concentration, power concentration that they had at the time was going to be a threat to democracy. And it was a threat to democracy. But the levels of corporate concentration that we face today are even much more staggering.

And we have all seen the images of the inauguration and the tech oligarchs having a front-row seat. But I think it’s also important to realize that this is the tip of the iceberg, right? We have seen a business community that has been cheering on Trump’s deregulation agenda, that has been cheering on Trump’s tax cut agenda. We just saw how the market reaction was to Lula’s reelection, right? I mean, the real had the worst, I mean, decline in value since the 1990s, since, you know, like, total economic turmoil in Brazil. We have seen the market reactions in France to the mass affordability protests. So, it’s very clear where the markets stand, right?

So, of course it’s about power concentration. It’s about these individuals that have outrageous amounts of power. But it’s also structurally about where capital lands amidst this crossroads for democracy. And I think it’s pretty clear that the richest of the rich have chosen their side, that markets are, you know, reacting in the ways in which they do to a Lula winning against a Bolsonaro with a proto-fascist agenda, with an agenda that would tip the balance in Latin America very clearly against democracy. So, what we are up against is the concentration of wealth, but it’s also the power of markets over our governments.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: And if you could say, Isabella, what — are there particular demographics you feel are particularly vulnerable to what you call a fascist economics? And what the broader — because, obviously, as you say in the book, too, the economy and the way that the economy is structured is essential, but there is also a wider political and social context in which — and this is not to say that all of these sectors are impermeable, but that they do signal, they refer to separate things.

ISABELLA WEBER: Yeah, I mean, studies suggest that it’s those who still have something to lose that become most vulnerable, most perceptive to fascist rhetoric. So often it’s those who, you know, who are already unemployed, who are already at the bottom, who are already poor, are not necessarily the prime constituency here. It’s those who get squeezed.

And these shocks squeeze people very directly, right? The bottom 50% in the United States spend around 70% on essentials, from, you know, energy to healthcare to food to housing to transportation, the stuff that they can’t do without. Now, when these prices shoot up, this other 30% gets squeezed, right? If we then react by hiking interest rates, which is currently the standard playbook in response to inflation, then the prospect of being able to buy a house — which, of course, for Americans, is a very important, you know, step towards a certain economic security, a certain lifestyle, a certain sense of self-worth, in fact — that that is pushed out even further out of reach.

So, this means that, you know, we have these shocks in the first place. Then we have a policy response that makes housing less affordable, that aims to push people out of work. That’s a declared goal of hiking interest rates, right? And all of this becomes this toxic cocktail of economic insecurity, where people have been told over decades that their worth is however they perform on the market, right?

Now, with these structural shocks, they have no chance to perform well on the market, right? They go to work every day, they do their job, and yet their lives are becoming more precarious. And then, when the far right comes in, they basically, you know, appeal to this experience of depreciation by saying, “No, you are actually worth something because you’re white, you’re a man. You’re better than these other folks.” So, they appeal to this natural hierarchy, and they suggest that, you know, you are not performing on the market because the hierarchy has been distorted by women not staying at home, by transgender kids being in your sports club, by disabled children being allowed to be in the classroom, and so on. And then, the promise goes: If we remove all these, you know, distortions, then the market will be pure again, and you will be able to perform and show how strong and capable you are. And that’s actually why —

ISABELLA WEBER: — you know, even very neoliberal policies are often key to the far right, because the promise goes that, you know, it’s about these impurities, and once they are removed, then the market can do its job, and you will be doing fine.

AMY GOODMAN: Professor Weber, we just have one minute, and I just wanted to touch on AI. You write in your book, “Artificial Intelligence is the new arms race between China and the United States.” You say, “What AI is for the future of defense, steel, coal and cannons were for Nazi rearmament.” This is quite a statement. Thirty seconds.

ISABELLA WEBER: Yeah, I mean, what we have seen with AI is that the AI leaders, for more than 10 years by now, have been saying that this could lead to human extinction, but that in the process, great business will have been made. So, this contradiction between, you know, business interests, profit fundamentalism and, literally, the life of humanity is becoming very clear when it comes to the AI question. And the AI is an emergent essential where the need for democratic control is as urgent as it gets.

AMY GOODMAN: I want to thank you so much for being with us. There’s so much more to talk about, but people will just have to read your book.
