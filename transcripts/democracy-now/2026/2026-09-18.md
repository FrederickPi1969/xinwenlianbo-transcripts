We are joined now by Amitabh Behar, Executive Director of Oxfam International. You have just come into New York for the U.N. General Assembly. It’s also Climate Week. Your focus is the deepening inequality in the world. Lay out what your message is, Amitabh.

AMITABH BEHAR: Absolutely. Let me start on a personal note. I grew up in India and the U.N. was all about hope—hope of a just and peaceful future—but at the moment, what we are seeing is that the world is torn apart by the inequality emergency, the climate crisis, the wars all around. Just look at the obscenity of inequality. At the moment, 3,000 billionaires have a combined wealth of $18.3 trillion. That’s almost the GDP of China. And this is at the time when a quarter of the global population actually lives with food insecurity. Almost 780 million sleep hungry. They don’t get three square meals a day.

But I think what is really important is that we need to reclaim our agency. We need to ensure that there are solutions. And there are solutions. There are positive steps being taken at this moment. That’s really the message to the U.N.—that U.N. cannot continue to live with this fragility. It needs decisive action. It needs to give more leadership. It needs to ensure accountability, ensure impunity is not tolerated. So there is multiple expectations from the U.N. and that’s the message from us.

JUAN GONZÁLEZ: But Amitabh Behar, what precisely would you expect the United Nations to do, especially given the reality that in the current U.S. government, there is more of a continued move toward unilateralism, to ignoring the U.N. and ignoring international agreements?

AMITABH BEHAR: Let me start by talking of, say, inequality, because it’s about rebuilding our future. And on inequality, I actually see more than glimmers of hope. One of those big ideas that emerged from the G20 in South Africa is the idea of the International Panel on Inequality. That’s being backed now by several member states—Brazil, South Africa, Spain, Norway. The entire Africa Union has agreed to back this. So there are different initiatives where specific countries are coming together to fight inequality and also for a better future. And this is really the moment where we do need to look at major reforms. So I am glad that the U.N. is talking about U.N. reforms, U.N. at 80.

The reforms need to look at some of those changed geopolitics of the world. We live in a very different world from 1945 when we saw the U.N. come in. You cannot continue with the veto power, particularly the penholder power. We need to really, really change that. The realities of the world in terms of where—you have a far stronger Global South; That needs clearer representation. And I would say one of the most important things is we must go back to the opening lines of the U.N. Charter, which is about “We, the people.” The people get often missing in these conversations. So, it’s bringing the people’s voice. At the moment, you are seeing a shrinking of civic space. More than 90% of global population actually lives in closed contexts. So there are different challenges, but as I started, there are real possibilities of change.

And let me just add that while I talked of the member states and them coming together, I also take huge inspiration for me personally—I am hugely inspired by the movements across the world. Amy, you were talking about it. I come from India, so I have seen movements in Sri Lanka, in Bangladesh, in Nepal changing regimes. In India, it’s the first time the Cockroach Janta Party, a students’ movement, was able to get a union minister dismissed. So let’s not underestimate people’s power. People’s power is here and they will certainly move towards a more just and equal society.

JUAN GONZÁLEZ: A key change that the U.N. will make very soon is the selection of a new Secretary-General as António Guterres is scheduled to step down. What is Oxfam calling for in terms of a decision on who will lead the world body?

AMITABH BEHAR: It is really odd that in all these 80 years of the U.N., we have not had a woman Secretary-General. We must ensure that we have a woman Secretary-General. But let me also say, it is not just having a woman Secretary-General; what we are talking of is feminist politics. It is a feminist worldview leading the U.N. And that is a fundamental shift that we need to make. The new Secretary-General is going to have an extremely full plate, starting with tackling the emergency of inequality, the climate crisis and the wars growing everywhere. So this is a moment, pretty much, of a fundamental reboot for the world, and we need that decisive, moral, normative leadership coming from the Secretary-General.

AMY GOODMAN: Oxfam is warning that artificial intelligence, AI, can accelerate global inequality. Talk about what you’re demanding there.

AMITABH BEHAR: I think it’s very clear—and now you are seeing in the last few days the AI companies themselves are saying that they need regulation—it is really important that there is some multilateral initiatives to govern the AI. We have seen over the years, technology when it comes in, we often say that, oh, it is going to be a huge boon for people. But often we see how it ends up actually accentuating inequalities. We saw it during COVID. It was really heartbreaking to see that you actually had vaccine apartheid. Even for something of the scale of the COVID pandemic, there were pharmaceutical companies who wanted to make profits out of that pandemic. So it is really important that we take a decisive multilateral action in terms of the AI.

But the story goes bigger. This is also about who owns these AI companies. It is those tech billionaires who are pretty much now—a report earlier this year at Davos talked of now the super-rich are not just happy being wealthy; this is about buying political power. So we are shifting from this extreme inequality to oligarchies. The same people who own AI are the people who are buying political power. These are oligarchies. They are the ones who also control media. They’re the ones who have control of narratives. And then you have essentially state capture by these oligarchs. So it is AI, it is about our future, it is about the future of democracy, and this needs decisive action.

JUAN GONZÁLEZ: I also wanted to ask you about the issue of Palestine, and Israel’s genocidal war against the Palestinians. You recently released a statement titled “Palestinians should lead the rebuilding of Gaza.” How would you compare how Oxfam views what needs to be done versus President Trump’s so-called Board of Peace?

AMITABH BEHAR: The Board of Peace came into existence in November 2025. It has a lot of legislative powers, executive powers, but what do you see? The continued killing of civilians over the last one year. So, even after the Peace Board has been created, you’re at the moment seeing in Gaza 90% of the people being water insecure. 66% of the people do not even get the basic minimum standard of six liters of drinking water.
This is not what we expected from any kind of a new executive party. What we really need is the Palestinian people building, rebuilding and leading their own future.

AMY GOODMAN: Finally in this last minute, climate. We are moving into Climate Week this week in New York, and then of course the U.N. Climate Summit which will be taking place in Turkey. What are the immediate demands? You spend much of your time in Nairobi, in India. We see the devastating effect of the breaking away of the glacier, the melting of the glacier in Nepal, and they have thousands.

Look at the question of climate financing. Even according to the estimates made by G77 and China—what we actually have, it’s not even 10% of their expectations. So we need to decisive action. Let me just give you another interesting data point. The 0.1% of the top in terms of the wealth emit carbon in one day which is more than 50% of the bottom. In one day, they are able to emit that carbon which is more than anybody living in the bottom 50%. This is about the inequality crisis. We need to understand that the inequality crisis, the climate crisis, the crisis of democracy, they are interlinked. RHIANNON GIDDENS: Oh, yeah. It’s really hard to know what to do. I think part of the job of folk music—folk music idiom—is to connect those things emotionally to each other, because that’s really the only way forward is to recognize ourselves in that other person. So that’s just thinking about, if I was in that situation, what would I be thinking? I’d be missing my family. I’d be missing my friends. I’d be missing—you know, like, will I ever see you again? AMY GOODMAN: Talk about collaborating with your sister with the song “Walk with Me.”

RHIANNON GIDDENS: My sister is my first—kind of my first everything. She has been there for me since I was born. She’s seven years older. It’s also sibling harmony, which is very special. It’s also a tradition in North Carolina of sibling gospel harmony that we are also pulling from.

RHIANNON GIDDENS: Just in trying to get rights for her people. Coming from such a hard background and just being just completely laser focused on what she was trying to do, while her body is taking all of this abuse. The weight that that life—particularly when she was alive, the weight and the tear that that does on Black bodies. It’s really hard to talk about the Civil Rights Movement and to think about where we are right now, I’m going to be honest with you. Because—what they went through! What Fannie Lou Hamer went through. She was beat, and her life—just so that we could vote, you know?

RHIANNON GIDDENS: Yeah, absolutely. And was trying to do something for her people, and the selflessness of that. And like, here we are. Like, where are—? It’s just—I can’t even really be articulate about it because it is just so distressing to think about what folks went through, and here we are with our voting rights being challenged and the whole system being destabilized kind of day by day by day. It’s just like, what was it for? If we don’t figure out a way to stop what is happening—I don’t know. I don’t know. We just have to figure out how to draw from that and find that again in this fractured landscape.

YOTAM MAROM: It’s hard to track the impact that movements have when they’re happening. and Occupy is a complicated one. I remember actually in the immediate aftermath wondering, what did we accomplish? Did we win anything? And feeling a lot of loss and despair. But it’s really clear to see now, 15 years back, how much of an impact it has made. I think obvious things like thousands of people get politicized in these big movement moments and that definitely happened with Occupy Wall Street. And then training of lots of leaders and building of lots of organizational infrastructure that movements really didn’t have at that time.

But I think the biggest impact, I think the biggest contribution, is the 99% and the 1%, this sort of intervention in the language of the public. I think this is what movements often do really well when they’re successful is they change the public’s common sense about something. Occupy Wall Street changed the public’s common sense about class and class struggle and inequality in this country. I think it’s hard to see how you get a Bernie Sanders 2016 run without the public shifting its relationship to that story. Bernie has been saying the same thing for decades—and bless him—but suddenly, he was popular. That’s because movements came around and made it a thing, and effective organizations and good politicians can support that and turn it into something durable. That’s how you get Bernie and AOC and The Squad and Zohran. Yeah, I think that’s a huge impact.

JUAN GONZÁLEZ: Can you talk about the international inspirations to Occupy? Because clearly, about a year earlier, the Arab Spring had broken out in May of 2011. The Indignados in Spain.
To what degree were the activists in New York and across the United States aware and inspired by those movements?

YOTAM MAROM: I think a lot of the people in the sort of heart of Occupy Wall Street were hugely inspired by those movements, by the Arab Spring, the Indignados in Spain and movements kind of all across, and really saw ourselves as part of a lineage of mass movements in the streets, taking life into their own hands. I think even the form, this sort of decentralized taking over public squares, we were deeply inspired by those movements.

AMY GOODMAN: Yotam, on September 19, 2001 [sic], two days after the start of Occupy, Democracy Now! interviewed the late activist and anthropologist David Graeber, who has been credited with helping to coin Occupy’s defining rallying call, “We are the 99%.” He talked about who showed up on the first day of the protest.

DAVID GRAEBER: It was mostly young people and most of them were people who had gone through the educational system, who were deeply in debt, and who found it completely impossible to get jobs. These people really feel very strongly that they did the right thing, they did exactly what they were supposed to, the system has completely failed them, and they’re not going to be saved by the people in charge. If there is going to be any kind of society worth living in, we are going to have to create it ourselves.

AMY GOODMAN: That’s the late David Graeber. Of course he was talking about—that was two days after September 19, 2011. Then Occupy, that mass protest in October in Times Square, talk about the scene and looking up and seeing the ticker.

YOTAM MAROM: Yeah. This is a story in my book, too, that I helped—I played a bunch of roles at Occupy Wall Street and one of them was helping to pull off this October 15th action which was our biggest day of action. We were sort of tearing through the streets of New York and shutting down banks and blocking traffic. It was also the day that I think 100, 150 occupations started in other cities around the country. Not just big cities but also like, you know, a parking lot in Idaho, and the state lawn in wherever, and really became a significant political force.

That day ended in Times Square with like 80,000 people. And I remember this moment standing on a trashcan in Times Square. And, you know, Times Square has these massive buildings. And the ABC building has a ticker, a rolling ticker, that pumps out the news. We’re standing there and the ticker goes “Occupy Wall Street goes worldwide”—while we’re there. And it’s this like time-stops kind of moment.

I look back at that actually as a pretty transformative moment for me. And I think, in a way, Occupy was very transformative for a whole generation of us. For me, what that moment was, was a first feeling that we could build movements that were powerful, that were popular. I think I had grown up in movements that had lost and were used to losing. And I think we over time sort of accepted the idea that we were going to be on the margins, we were going to be the righteous few, but we weren’t going to win. Occupy Wall Street was this first glimmer of, oh, we could be—we could be powerful. We could be something that the public is paying attention to. We could potentially win. Of course that’s not exactly how Occupy played out, but that is an important prerequisite to movements is the sense that we could be powerful. I think movements that don’t believe that they can win tend to take on hard dynamics internally.

JUAN GONZÁLEZ: Yotam, you mentioned your book For Louder Days: Reaching Beyond a Politics of Powerlessness. But clearly, as you also mentioned, a lot of what the protesters were hoping for did not occur. You also deal with the conflicts and divisions that developed within the movement. I’m wondering if you could talk about that a little bit.

YOTAM MAROM: Yeah, it’s a good question. Look, I think Occupy Wall Street brought us a lot, and I think there’s a lot to be grateful for, but I also think in some ways it was an embodiment of this thing that I call “the politics of powerlessness” which is when movements and organizations internalize the sense that they belong on the margins and that they’re always going to be on the margins—whether out of bad habit or having lost for a very long time or feeling despair in the face of the climate crisis or this towering opponent—when movements don’t believe that they can win, we tend to turn away from our opponent, we turn away from the public we’re supposed to be organizing, and we turn towards each other—and often against each other. Because that’s what we can control.

We don’t like to feel powerless in the face of this big wide world, so we sort of enact power on each other. That often looks like tearing down leaders. It looks like forming these sort of tight clubs that are really hard for everyday people to join. It looks like battles over identity and things like that. And a kind of conflict avoidance that makes all that possible. So the book is really about—Occupy was, in a way, a case in that, where I learned a lot of that. And then the book is stories from my work as a movement facilitator and strategist for social movement organizations, supporting them to work through that thing and watching them break through those patterns over the course of the last 10 to 15 years. That’s what I mean by the politics of powerlessness. I will stop there.

JUAN GONZÁLEZ: To what degree do you think the decentralized, autonomous, anti-hierarchal approach of the Occupy protesters made it more difficult for them to actually, once the protest ended, build national organizations that could actually exercise power? Have you thought about that?

YOTAM MAROM: Yeah, totally. It’s another good question. I think all the things that made Occupy magical were also its sort of self-destruct mechanism. The sort of leaderlessness was a thing that invited people to join and take agency and be creative, and movements often are decentralized and that’s how they go to scale. But it also made it impossible to craft a strategy, to build a long-term organization, to really absorb all these thousands of people who were being politicized. In some ways, that is just part of what happens in movement moments, and it’s our responsibility as movements to be building organizations and parties and things that can capture that kind of momentum. But I do think that, yeah, we got it wrong on a lot of things. We had a ton of internal conflict that really I think jeopardized the project.

AMY GOODMAN: You write all about it in For Louder Days: Reaching Beyond a Politics of Powerlessness, as we talk about Yotam Marom’s book out on the 15th anniversary of Occupy Wall Street.
