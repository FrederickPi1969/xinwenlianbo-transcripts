## “She Was a Fighter”: Dolores Huerta, Legendary Farmworker Organizer & Activist, Dies at 96

In the years that followed, Huerta became heavily involved in the women’s liberation and immigrant rights movements and became a major supporter of the Democratic Party. In March, The New York Times published an investigation into Cesar Chavez’s history of sexual abuse of women and girls in the farmworkers’ movement, prompting Huerta to reveal that she, too, had been raped by Chavez. Huerta said the assaults led to the births of two of her children.

The United Farm Workers grew out of a merger between the National Farm Workers Association and the Filipino-led Agricultural Workers Organizing Committee.

AMY GOODMAN: So, let’s hear Dolores Huerta in her own words. In 2017, she came into our studio to talk about her life and the documentary that had just been named about her, Dolores. She talked about her childhood.

DOLORES HUERTA: Well, I was born in the state of New Mexico and moved to Stockton when I was 6 years old. My parents divorced, and my mother took us to Stockton. And that’s where I was raised, and that’s where I started my initial organizing, in that area.

AMY GOODMAN: And how did you, as a teenager, decide that organizing was going to be the path of your life, and specifically working with farmworkers?

DOLORES HUERTA: Well, that was kind of accidental, as where I grew up, Stockton, California, is an agricultural community. And as a youngster, I was a Girl Scout for 10 years of my life, and I was in a lot of different social clubs. But when I met Mr. Ross and he showed us what we could actually do by coming together and organizing and taking direct nonviolent action, then I was really — I thought, “Oh my goodness! This is what I want to do with my life.” And so, I became a schoolteacher, then I left teaching to go to Delano to start the union there.

AMY GOODMAN: And farmworkers themselves, the grape boycott that you helped to lead, the worker strikes, talk about how you organized them. What was the strategy? And then talk about your response to the impact that it had.

DOLORES HUERTA: Well, the thing is that when people think about the strike and the — it’s very dramatic, colorful. But what they don’t see is like the three years before the strike, where we organize workers in house meetings. And this is the method that Fred Ross taught us, by meeting in people’s homes, you know, one family at a time, and then bringing them all together. And this is the same kind of a method that we use today with my foundation, the Dolores Huerta Foundation, where we’re working on ending the school-to-prison pipeline, working on environmental issues, on economic issues and, you know, inspiring people that they — and motivating them that they have power, but that they can make the changes, but that they have to make the changes. No one can do it for them. And this is the kind of work that we do, using that same organizing method, one family at a time, one family at a time. And it’s very tedious, and it’s very time-consuming, but then the results are really wonderful.

We recently settled a lawsuit with our current high school district in California, in Bakersfield, for the expulsion and suspension of African American and Latino students. And now they have to change their policies, and they have to change their procedures, to keep the students in school. And so, we’ll go out there, and we organize the parents, and then the parents are the ones that take on the — they become the leaders of making sure that some of these policies are changed.

DOLORES HUERTA: It was in 1968. We started the boycott in 1968.

AMY GOODMAN: And explain what it was. What was the strategy you took?

DOLORES HUERTA: Well, the strategy was that we had farmworkers come out from California, and again using those same methods of meeting people in their homes and organizing groups throughout the United States and asking people to come and support, number one, to get people to picket the stores and ask people not to buy grapes. And at the end of the day, we had like 17 million people that were not eating grapes. And this is what brought the growers to the bargaining table.

AMY GOODMAN: What about Robert Kennedy’s role? How significant was it? And how did you and Cesar Chavez reach out to him?

DOLORES HUERTA: Well, Senator Kennedy had been a supporter early on. He came to California. He had a hearing right in the middle of the strike. And there’s a very dramatic moment in the movie, Dolores, where Robert Kennedy tells the sheriff of Kern County and the district attorney to read the Constitution of the United States, because they were continuously arresting us, and so that we wouldn’t be — you know, we wouldn’t be able to picket. And so, he was a great supporter. And then, of course, we helped him very much in his campaign, when he was running for the presidency, by going out there and getting people out to vote.

PRESIDENT BARACK OBAMA: On a personal note, Dolores was very gracious when I told her I had stolen her slogan, ”¡Sí se puede! Yes, we can!” Knowing her, I’m pleased that she let me off easy, because Dolores does not play.

DOLORES HUERTA: In Arizona, actually, in 1972, when Cesar was fasting, was doing a 25-day water-only fast, kind of to take the hatred out of the hearts of the growers. That’s the way that he put it. ## Meet AI Engineer Who Resigned from DeepMind over “Google’s Complicity in the Gaza Genocide”

“I felt I could not, in good faith, send this message of building AI to benefit humanity while ignoring that our company was complicit in a genocide,” Liguori tells Democracy Now! “The problem is that when the focus is on the model capabilities and on the potential for agents 'going rogue,' we are shifting the attention from a specific human choice … which is this unrestrained, unregulated integration of the systems without red lines.”

AMY GOODMAN: This is Democracy Now!, democracynow.org. I’m Amy Goodman.

Several tech workers at Google’s AI unit DeepMind have resigned in protest over Google’s work with the U.S. and Israeli militaries. Last week, DeepMind research engineer Sofia Liguori announced her resignation. In a letter to her colleagues titled “Stay Human,” she denounced what she called, quote, “Google’s complicity in the Gaza genocide and the increasing integration of Google’s technology into military and surveillance systems.”

Democracy Now!’s Anjali Kamat and I spoke with Sofia Liguori yesterday. She joined us from London. I started by asking her to explain why she resigned and the process she went through in her decision to leave Google.

SOFIA LIGUORI: Sure. Thank you for having me, first of all. I’m very honored.

Yeah. When I discovered about Google complicity in Gaza genocide, I knew I couldn’t stay there and continue with business as usual. I actually discovered about the Nimbus contract, which is the deal that Google has with the Israeli government and the IDF, a deal that includes cloud and AI services for — used by the military for war. I discovered about this deal when Google fired employees in the U.S. protesting over the deal in 2024. And at the beginning, in London, there was no internal movement. No Tech for Apartheid was at the time mainly a U.S. movement. And so, I felt really powerless. I just bought a Palestine hoodie. I went to the office with it, but I really didn’t know what to do. I felt isolated. I felt powerless.

Then I went on maternity leave. And during maternity leave, I was watching the news every day, watching a live-streamed genocide, and I knew that my company was complicit. So, my first thought was resigning straightaway, not even coming back from maternity leave. But what changed my mind was that as the time to come back was coming, I met a colleague. They were on their way out from the company exactly for ethical reasons. And they put me in contact with UTAW, a union movement that had in the meantime developed inside Google DeepMind. So I decided to stay and try to break the silence around Nimbus, around the increasing number of military and surveillance involvements, and try to organize and push back against the company direction.

AMY GOODMAN: Can you — for people who are not familiar with what Google does, can you explain what Nimbus is in plain, lay language, and then also what DeepMind does?

SOFIA LIGUORI: Sure. This is very important to understand. So, Nimbus is a $1.2 billion contract signed by Google and Amazon together in 2021, actually — it’s a pretty old contract — to provide cloud services, which include the cloud infrastructure to run every possible workload and to store data, and also provides AI services. And it’s a contract that is directed to the Israeli government, but also to the army, to the IDF.

Nothing about this contract was ever disclosed publicly. And actually, all the public statement that Google did around this contract has been disproved, as it was basically a lie. What we know about this contract is mostly from leaks and investigative reporting, especially from The Intercept. And what we know is that it provides the full suite of AI tools, including Gemini. Gemini is the large language model specifically developed by Google DeepMind. And we also know for sure that it has been used in war. We don’t have an exact list of all the applications it contributed to, but Israeli officials themselves recognize the importance of cloud capabilities and of the Nimbus project for the war in Gaza.

And we also know that Google had been advised by human internal lawyers and also an external consultancy firm about the potential for human rights violations when signing the contract, and they were advised not to include AI services and not to — and to incorporate AI principles into the contract. What Google instead did is signing a contract which has no restrictions about terms of usage and leaves the company with very little visibility into how the products are used. This means that we are giving away this very powerful infrastructure with these very powerful AI models, and at the same times we are giving away any possibility to restrict usage, monitor usage, limit usage in any way. And this is a very convenient contract designed for executives to escape accountability.

AMY GOODMAN: You know, I think it’s interesting that among those who investigated all of this was Yuval Abraham. He did it at the time for +972 Magazine, Local Call and The Guardian, and found that as part of the $1.2 billion deal, Google and Amazon are prohibited from restricting how Israel uses their products, and are obligated to secretly notify Israel if a foreign court orders them to hand over data stored on the company’s cloud platforms. Of course, Yuval Abraham is the co-director of the new film out, NAZA, about the use of AI, the programs Lavender and “Where’s Daddy?” and the killing of thousands of Palestinians in Gaza. SOFIA LIGUORI: So, my role in the company was actually I was working in a “good” area of the company. I was working on sustainability-related projects. My specific project was on AI application to optimal power flow. But I increasingly felt that even if my project was good, we were doing ethic washing for the company. I felt I could not, in good faith, send this message even of building AI to benefit humanity while ignoring that our company was complicit in a genocide.

And I sent letters to colleagues to ask to talk about these topics. I messaged on internal channels to raise awareness around these contracts and so on. And I have to say, like, most of the times, I found solidarity from colleagues, but whispered. Like, people are very scared to show their dissent publicly. They are very scared of retaliation. And the reason is that it’s very hard to talk about these topics. You have to face intimidation if you do so. Every time you mention these topics, you end up having a meeting with HR, a meeting with your manager. Your posts get removed from internal channels. They use internal policies to find excuses to silence dissent, basically.

ANJALI KAMAT: And, Sofia, you write in your resignation letter, quote, “I no longer believe that our technology will have a net positive impact on society. I think there’s a fantasy world where AI can help decarbonise grids, cut waste, protect ecosystems, and benefit humanity. But it’s not this world, and it’s not this type of general-purpose AI we are racing to develop. Racing to deploy agentic AI in this society and in this historical moment is a terrible idea, and will definitely be a net evil.” Sofia, we’ve seen a wave of resignations from employees concerned that AI might someday turn against humanity, but you resigned over the current usage of AI in war.
