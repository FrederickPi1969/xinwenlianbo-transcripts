And they could recruit informants by paying them or by bribing them and saying, “We’re going to speed up your immigration status or application,” or they would use a stick approach and say, “If you don’t help us, we might make things more difficult for you.”

Eventually, the FBI amassed a base of about 15,000 informants, which is absolutely wild. But what’s so critical is that Muslims became scared of going to the mosque, of going to bookstores, of going to cafes, because these undercover informants were everywhere. They were infiltrating practically every restaurant and cafes, certainly in the Tri-State area, but also around the country.

You know, we have used informants in the past. The FBI certainly has. But in those cases, usually an informant has a tie to a mafia member or a gang member. After 9/11, the only tie that informants had to another Muslim was that they were Muslim.

ANJALI KAMAT: Fahd, you’ve worked with Matin Siraj’s family. What was the impact of his arrest and his being sentenced to 30 years in prison on the community? And, I mean, 25 years after 9/11, what’s changed for the communities you’re working with, working-class South Asian and Muslim communities?

FAHD AHMED: Yeah. We worked with the Siraj family for more than 20 years, and first and foremost, this has absolutely devastated the family. Every day that Matin has spent in prison, his mother and his family have been in prison with him. Every single day. The family, it becomes hard to find work. Your own family becomes afraid: Do I stay in contact with them, or do I not? If I stay — if I support them, will I get targeted? And that same dynamic plays out in the community. People that were your neighbors now are like, “I’m not sure who — should I talk to this family? Who should I talk to?”

And what we saw was the complete devastation of the social fabric in Muslim communities for many, many years, for almost two decades, to where people were afraid to have political conversations. And, you know, if you know anything about Muslim communities and masjids, like, it’s — that’s pretty much all people do, is you do the ritual, like, religious things, and then you sit around and talk about what’s happening back home, what’s happening here, what do we think about the war, what do we think about this. And there were large number of places where people said, “No political conversations allowed.” You know, there was like Muslim student associations that started putting up signs: “Political conversations here are not allowed.”

And all of this was happening at a time that several Muslim countries were being targeted by U.S., U.S. wars, while civil rights of Muslim communities were being violated. And so, our communities were in this difficult situation that at the very moment where people needed to defend themselves, there was a high level of uncertainty on: Can and should we talk, or should we not talk? Should we engage in political activism, or should we not?

AMY GOODMAN: And this, of course, all didn’t begin 9/11. Rozina, you write about Abdeen Jabara, co-founder of the American-Arab Anti-Discrimination Committee and longtime leader of the National Lawyers Guild. In the 1970s, he exposed the Nixon administration’s surveillance program against Arab Americans and forced the NSA to admit it had been spying on him, Abdeen, since 1967. We spoke to him in 2013 about his groundbreaking case.

ABDEEN JABARA: I had been very, very active in Palestinian support work. And one day I read in Newsweek magazine, in the Periscope section, that 26 Arabs in the United States had been targeted for surveillance, electronic surveillance. So, I thought, surely, some of those had been clients of mine or had talked to me on the phone about issues and so forth. And that’s when I brought the lawsuit. …

I was surprised, after all the revelations about the Snowden-NSA brouhaha, that nobody had looked back at what had occurred back in the — in the ’70s to show that at that time it came out in the press that over 1,600 Americans had been surveilled by the NSA.

AMY GOODMAN: So, that’s Abdeen Jabara, former head, founder of American-Arab Anti-Discrimination Committee, and at the time, we had a lower third that said “friend of Alex Odeh.” Rozina, explain.

ROZINA ALI: So, Alex Odeh was one of the vice presidents of the ADC for the West Coast chapter. And in 1985, there was a bomb in that office that killed him, unfortunately. And his murder was never solved, although at the time the FBI did suspect the Jewish Defense League, and there was actually a lot of conversation about the rise of terrorism in the U.S. against Arab Americans coming, emanating from the JDL. Unfortunately, what has happened in these intervening years since then is that terrorism has become more and more aligned with Islam, and we forget that terrorists can be from any group or any ethnicity or any religion. But really, what this long history shows us, that how we think about terrorism and Arabs and Muslims has been a problem much longer than 9/11.

ANJALI KAMAT: And where do you see — I mean, your book is called Seasons of Fury, and you trace the kind of changing Islamophobia both through the institutions and practices and bigotry of people, but also different administrations. Where are we today, 25 years later? I mean, this is also a time of the rise of so many Muslim politicians, outspoken Muslim politicians, including our own mayor, Mayor Mamdani, who is once again facing Islamophobic attacks.

ROZINA ALI: I would say we’re probably at a really — a worse place than we have ever been, because it is so accepted, and policymakers are peddling anti-Muslim rhetoric. But just even take the example of Giuliani. It’s funny to me that he apologized to Talat Hamdani, because, years later, he’s essentially doing the same thing over again towards Mayor Mamdani by saying Muslims cannot be victims of 9/11, right? Muslims can only be the perpetrators. And this is a narrative that has been repeated over and over again, and certainly around election season.

AMY GOODMAN: And to be clear, this is about Giuliani and former Governor Pataki saying that Mamdani, the mayor of New York, should not be allowed at the ceremonies for the 25th anniversary of the 9/11 attacks.

ROZINA ALI: Exactly, correct, yes. And one wonders: How much longer do Muslims have to apologize for 9/11?

ANJALI KAMAT: And, Fahd, as you look back on these 25 years, and, you know, your organization, DRUM Beats, was one of the first to endorse Zohran Mamdani, you know, how do you see the community having changed, I mean, from that time of fear and putting their heads down to now having a political voice?

FAHD AHMED: Yeah, I think the — there’s divergent consequences. On one hand, we are seeing a resurgence of Islamophobia at levels that is unprecedented, and at the same time, it’s not as effective as it used to be. And I think part of what we see, you know, the emergence of Mayor Mamdani and similar politicians, a lot of it is because of a lot of the organizing efforts that built the foundations in the aftermath of 9/11, that built the communities, and also just the humiliation and the resentment that the communities had for being targeted for so long, that part of the ways of, like, being able to resist back is, like, “All right, it’s — we’ve been through it enough. We’re going to organize ourselves, and we’re going to support people that are going to be much more aligned with the experiences and the perspectives of our communities.”

And so, part of what we’re seeing in the rise of more Muslim politicians — and, you know, I don’t subscribe to identity politics in terms of just identity is enough — but there is a confidence around, like, “I’m not going to hide it anymore.” And I think that deeply resonates with our communities, who felt for two decades that they had to hide it. And I think now we’re sort of seeing the response to that.

AMY GOODMAN: We want to thank you both for being with us. Thank you to Rozina Ali, author of Seasons of Fury: Four Families and the Rise of Islamophobia in America, and Fahd Ahmed, executive director of DRUM, Desis Rising Up and Moving.

That does it for our show. On September 17th, I’ll be going home to Bay Shore, New York, on Long Island for the screening of Steal This Story, Please! — check our website at democracynow.org — and then September 22nd at the IFC. I’m Amy Goodman, with Anjali Kamat.
