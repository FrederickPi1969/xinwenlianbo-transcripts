When you want to understand why 660 million pounds of some of the least expensive frozen beef that’s going to go into fast food hamburgers, and why is that coming to the United States? Just remarkable unchecked corporate power.

AMY GOODMAN: Explain what “mystery meat” is, and how did this come into being?

ERIC SCHLOSSER: “Mystery meat” is a colloquial phrase for lean beef trimmings. These are little pieces of frozen beef, the beef that’s most likely to be contaminated, taken from the outside of the animal. It’s sort of generic industrial meat that will then be ground into hamburger meat, mainly for fast food restaurants.

President Trump allowed—made a proclamation saying that 660 million pounds could be coming into the country. It’s quite remarkable that we have this president who talks about “America First” and is so supported by rural America, but with a stroke of his pen is basically screwing over American ranchers. This beef is most likely coming from Brazil.

In my New York Times article, it’s this remarkable coincidence that the day before Trump issued this proclamation, he met with the head of the biggest beef company in the world, JBS. His name is Joesley Batista. He’s one of the co-owners. He met with Joesley Batista at the White House—who, you know, Maggie Haberman at the New York Times said is a friend of Melania’s. I don’t know what that connection is. But he met with Batista and the next day issued this proclamation bringing in hundreds of millions of pounds of beef, most likely from Brazil.

And another really weird coincidence is that the European Union has suspended all imports of beef from Brazil starting the first week of September. And Trump, in allowing these imports into the United States, it just so happens the timing is the first week of September. So it smells like a pretty corrupt deal. The European Union has suspended the import of Brazilian beef for concerns about antibiotic misuse. And this meat is some of the meat most likely to be contaminated.

This company, JBS, I mean, it’s quite remarkable that Joesley Batista was meeting with the president of the United States to help determine our agricultural policy when he has confessed to spending over $186 million bribing almost 2,000 legislators in Brazil, including three presidents of Brazil. I have never met Mr. Batista but he is clearly a very bright man to be able to bribe three presidents of Brazil and yet be meeting with the president of the United States and helping to determine our agricultural policy. I mean, he may have a great career in American politics.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: As you also point out, what makes it even more remarkable is that he was long considered persona non grata in the U.S. And then not long after his meeting with Trump in November at the White House, JBS got approval from the Securities and Exchange Commission to be listed on the New York Stock Exchange.

ERIC SCHLOSSER: The timing is a little different. The largest single donor to Trump’s second inauguration was a subsidiary of JBS. ERIC SCHLOSSER: The subsidiary of JBS that made the donation was Pilgrim’s Pride. But it’s all the same company and it’s owned, controlled by these two brothers. But the real issue here isn’t JBS. It isn’t President Trump. It’s how every sector of the American economy has been taken over by a handful of corporations. Sadly, 25 years ago, I wrote in Fast Food Nation that the great issue—this is a paraphrase—the great issue of the 20th century is basically the challenge to totalitarian systems of power, and the great issue of the 21st century is going to be resistance to unchecked corporate power. So much attention goes to Trump, and what we really need to be looking at are the corporations who are benefiting profoundly from all this deregulation. Whether it’s the oil companies because of the Department of the Interior, the chemical companies because of who runs the EPA, and sadly, the food system now is controlled as never before by a handful of very, very powerful companies. Anyway, I—

NERMEEN SHAIKH: Let’s talk about that, and beef in particular. Four large meatpacking companies control about 85% of the market for beef, whereas in the 1970s, the top four beef companies had only 21%.

ERIC SCHLOSSER: Right. And if you go back to the 1970s—and there were so many terrible things going on in American society, so I’m not being nostalgic for it—but in the 1970s, we had a competitive market for beef, Americans ate more beef then than than they do today, and we had twice as many American ranchers. So the change that has occurred has helped a handful of corporations.

Fast Food Nation paid homage in many ways to Upton Sinclair. Because Upton Sinclair wrote The Jungle in 1906 and he wrote about the meatpacking industry because he thought it was a perfect symbol of the monopoly capitalism of his day. In my book, I paid homage to him by doing the same thing. The whole book is structured to take you to the slaughterhouse. When Upton Sinclair wrote The Jungle, there was something called “the beef trust” and it controlled maybe 55% to 60% of the market for beef. So right now these companies are more powerful than the companies when Upton Sinclair wrote The Jungle.

But Woodrow Wilson broke up the beef trust, and when you talk about the competitive market in the 1970s—things can change for the better. Again, I’m so sorry to be here 25 years later and saying so many of the same things I said 25 years ago. But the reason I do it—things don’t have to be this way. There are certain kinds of problems we’re really going to have difficulty changing, but strict antitrust enforcement, labor unions, food safety laws—these can really have an impact on our food system and on what we eat.

AMY GOODMAN: You note that during the past year, China rejected two shipping containers of beef from Uruguay after finding residue of an anti-parasitic drug in the meat and rejected beef from Argentina reportedly because it tested positive for a band antibiotic that can cause serious blood disorders. One of the things that JBS got in trouble for was bribing the food inspectors in Brazil so that contaminated meat could be sold.

ERIC SCHLOSSER: Contaminated meat is spoiled meat that’s riddled with bacteria. That’s how you get sick. They were using ascorbic acid and other chemicals—this is straight out of Upton Sinclair. So, I’m critical of the American meat system, but if you’re going to eat beef, get American beef and get it ground at your local butcher shop. Back to the—so the FDA is responsible for 80% of the food consumed in the United States. The FDA reports to Bobby Kennedy Jr. That’s all you need to know about is your food safe. I mean, I could spend hours talking about the disgrace of Bobby Kennedy Jr., the unethical qualities of Bobby Kennedy Jr., the routine lies. In the afterword of my book, I write about the infant formula scandal and about the shutdown of one infant formula plant that led to huge shortages of formula a few years ago. You want to talk about monopoly power? There are two companies that control 80% of the market for infant formula.

ERIC SCHLOSSER: In the United States. And the federal government buys more than half of the infant formula. So if we wanted to change the infant formula industry, we could do it in weeks, because the taxpayers are paying for it. Anyway, the acting head of the FDA right now has no background in public safety, in public health, has no medical training, has no scientific training. He is an attorney who represented the largest infant formula manufacturer in a case involving potentially contaminated formula.

ERIC SCHLOSSER: Yeah. That’s who this administration has put in charge of food safety. The USDA is in charge of the safety of our meat and about a quarter of the USDA employees have been fired.

ERIC SCHLOSSER: The head of the FDA. Now, look, I want to make one thing clear. These food companies don’t want people to get sick. I’m not saying they’re evil. But they also don’t want to be held liable when they do get sick. And if you’re not testing, you can’t trace; you can’t recall contaminated products. So when you dismantle the food safety system, as is happening right now in the United States, you’re helping these companies avoid legal liability.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: You also just mentioned earlier that despite all the problems with the food industry in the U.S., you would much prefer to have American beef. But can you tell what is American beef and what is not? Because country-of-origin labeling on beef used to be mandatory but it no longer is, and it has been many years since it has not been.

ERIC SCHLOSSER: And it’s amusing, because there’s all this conversation about the free market but if you read Adam Smith, a market only works efficiently if there’s total information. If consumers have adequate information, then the invisible hand is going to lead them to select the right product. When you get rid of that information, the power leads to the corporations. All meat had to be labeled from where it came from until 2015. Then the Republican-controlled Congress got rid of country-of-origin labeling for beef and pork. What’s remarkable is a little more than a year after that, the former speaker of the house, John Bonner, who shepherded this legislation, joined an JBS International board.

When you don’t have country-of-origin labeling, a consumer has no idea where this meat is coming from. And two of the major beef companies are Brazilian-owned now. I’m not being anti-Brazil; If Brazil wants to sell its meat in the United States, put a label on it. Make the best beef there is. I’m actually very libertarian in some ways. If these meatpacking companies want to sell meat full of E. coli and salmonella, put it on the label. Consumers can choose between pathogen-rich meat or meat that has been produced cleanly. And then they can cook it.

AMY GOODMAN: Eric Schlosser, you mentioned Upton Sinclair, who exposed the meatpacking industry and the conditions that the workers worked under. Nebraska’s Republican governor who publicly backs Trump’s ICE raids in Nebraska.

ERIC SCHLOSSER: I am shocked that he would be employing undocumented immigrants! Here’s the reality, and here’s really why I wrote Fast Food Nation. I wrote Fast Food Nation after I visited the meatpacking towns in the United States. I have been in meatpacking communities in Washington State, Colorado, Nebraska, Texas. My entry to the food system of America was spending a year with migrant farm workers in California, and I wrote about the exploitation and demonization of immigrant workers in California by the governor at the time, Pete Wilson. He wanted to be president and he thought that depicting immigrant workers as welfare cheats was going to be his ticket to the White House.

The reality is—and I wrote Fast Food Nation because I was stunned to find, in the meatpacking plants of the Midwest, the same workers—some of them had worked in strawberry fields in California, now in our industrial workforce. Meatpacking once was a high paid, union job, a higher paid job then an autoworker. So when Upton Sinclair wrote about the exploitation of immigrants, we solved that problem. If you were a meatpacking worker in the 1960s or 1970s, you had a solid middle-class life. Your spouse may not have to work. Your kids could go to college. In a very brief period of time, the meatpacking industry recruited immigrants, many of them undocumented, to serve as strikebreakers because they would be much easier to exploit.

And if there is one message that I have from my work on food, from this book, it’s that we are fed by immigrants. If you look at who’s working in the fields harvesting the fruits and vegetables, who’s working in the meatpacking plants, who’s working on the hog farms, the dairy farms of Vermont, it’s recent immigrants. Huge numbers of undocumented immigrants in the fast food restaurants behind the counter. And the idea that we are demonizing and terrorizing these hard-working people who feed us, it’s an absolute disgrace.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: This book came out 25 years ago. Has anything changed with respect to the consumption of fast food in the U.S. in these last 25 years?

ERIC SCHLOSSER: What has really changed is the eating habits of well-educated, upper-middle-class, and wealthy people. There is such an incredible awareness of food now. I mean, I couldn’t get a publisher for this book in New York City, and the reason I couldn’t get a publisher is that I was told, “Nobody will read a book about food that doesn’t have recipes in it.” It was one of the last independent publishers in Boston that published this book. And now there are—

ERIC SCHLOSSER: I have no idea. I’m very grateful that I’m here with you 25 years later. But there’s now a whole literature on food. There are bookshelves of books on our food system. I’m not taking credit for it; I just am so gratified. So we now have two food systems. We have a food system for the well educated, the wealthy, the upper middle class who are buying sustainable food, organic food, grass-fed beef, watching cooking shows. Then you have a food system for working people and the poor. It’s this food system; It’s the fast food industrial system. And the reason that I am still talking about this is that it’s entirely possible for us to have a food system that provides healthy food for everybody.

One of the things that we don’t talk about are the ways in which the external costs of the industrial food system are imposed on us. Since this book came out, there’s a whole new field of economics called true cost accounting. What’s the real cost of what we’re doing? In the food system, you add up the cost of obesity, food safety, the poverty of food workers, et cetera. One of my goals is to make the real cost of this industrial food appear on the menu.

And, if we are going to give government subsidies, government subsidies shouldn’t be going to soybean and corn farmers in the Midwest to grow animal feed. I mean, 100 million acres are devoted to corn in this country—that’s the size of California—and of that 100 million acres, maybe 350,000 go to the kind of corn that we eat—you know, sweet corn or canned corn. The rest of it is going to—anyway, I don’t want to speak for three hours. But I am hopeful that this system could be changed.

And the one thing I give Bobby Kennedy Jr. credit for—and, you know, a broken clock is right twice a day—is that he had the crazy nerve to talk about the problems with our food system in a way that only a handful of other politicians were willing to do. He did it for very selfish political reasons, but there is an underlying popular opinion to change our food system, and that’s what MAHA has shown.

AMY GOODMAN: Eric, before you go, I want to ask you about the notorious former ICE jail in Florida, in the Everglades. You wrote a piece in The Atlantic headlined The Alligator Alcatraz Boondoggle. Earlier this week, the Department of Homeland Security’s internal watchdog revealed immigrants jailed at Alligator Alcatraz were held in outdoor metal cages that were no bigger than a phone booth. At least 79 immigrants were caged this way, which DHS called “calming areas. They were caged for several minutes up to nearly two hours. Investigators also found Alligator Alcatraz did not comply with standards related to medical care, food quality, personal hygiene, recreation. The jail eventually shut down after legal challenges and scrutiny over its abuse and inhumane conditions. Why did you shift to focus on Alligator Alcatraz, and what should we know about it today?

ERIC SCHLOSSER: My next book is on prisons in America. I’ve been going into prisons in this country since the early 1990s. Unfortunately, President Trump has given me a concluding chapter of the book, which is the outrageous, unconscionable arrest and detention of immigrants in this country who are being completely brutalized. I have gone into maximum security prisons that are terrible and it costs about $50,000, $70,000, $80,000 a year to house someone in a maximum security prison. Maybe $100,000. The immigrants at Alligator Alcatraz had been sent there for civil violations, not criminal violations, and the state of Florida and the American taxpayers were paying a million dollars a year per detainee to be held in the most brutal conditions that you will not find in most maximum-security prisons.

I wrote that article for The Atlantic on Alligator Alcatraz to provide a roadmap for a Democratic Congress to investigate where that money went. I’m an investigative reporter; I could not find out the name of the warden of Alligator Alcatraz. It was being run by a private corporation with no background in corrections. It looks like they were friends of the governor of Florida who were earning a fortune off of exploiting and brutalizing immigrants who were there for a civil—not even—they hadn’t even been convicted of a crime.

So my hope is that we will get a full federal investigation, not only of the human rights violations that occurred at Alligator Alcatraz, but follow the money. Who made a fortune off of this? The only prison that the United States runs that spends more money per detainee is Guantanamo. The idea that we were subjecting busboys, landscapers, people who had been in the United States for decades and committed no crime, to the kind of brutality that we saw at Guantanamo when Guantanamo first opened—it’s a shameful episode in American history and it needs to be fully investigated by Congress.

AMY GOODMAN: Eric Schlosser, congratulations on your work. Investigative journalist, author of several books, including Fast Food Nation: The Dark Side of the All-American Meal. The 25th anniversary edition of the book is being published next week. We will link to all of your recent articles.

SUSAN NEIMAN: Look, part of it is the other parties are just awful. They are incapable. In fact, the almost funny thing—I mentioned Mamdani at a book talk I gave just before I left Germany. What has changed is that the other parties don’t—possibly the Left—have a real program that they’re able to communicate. What has changed is also—one has to understand, Saxony-Anhalt is a state in East Germany. Now, one of the West German myths about East Germany is, oh, well, the reason why there are proportionally larger numbers of people voting for far-right parties is they never learned anything about democracy because they lived in a totalitarian system. The rest of it are West Germans who have come to East Germany. So there’s a lot of resentment, understandably, and a lot of desire for change. There is racism. You know, there’s racism everywhere. But I’m not convinced yet that it’s a sign of where Germany is going.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: Lets go from what you said about the left in Germany taking inspiration from Zohran Mamdani and then go to your book, these extremes of the political spectrum that are now in evidence in the U.S. Unfortunately, one of them is more powerful than the other. SUSAN NEIMAN: I wrote it because, first of all, I was astonished by the number of people, who didn’t need to, who have quickly capitulated in the second Trump administration. I always thought the point of having more money than any family could possibly spend on luxury in a lifetime—I thought the point of having that kind of money was being able to say, “No.” [laughs] And it’s apparently not. I mean, that Google changed its maps to “Lake America” was for me [laughs]—thank you!

SUSAN NEIMAN: Yeah! And thank you for your—that expression. It’s a trivial kowtow but it’s fairly unbelievable, I think. People worry that it’s religiously tinged if they’re secular. It’s not. This is something I know something about, having written a—

SUSAN NEIMAN: Yeah, and having written a book 25 years ago called Evil in Modern Thought, so I know something about the history. Moral concepts come before religious concepts in every culture. Religion is a way to express certain moral concepts. It’s an attempt to solve certain moral problems. But the initial feeling of evil—you can call it a sense of injustice—is something that children have in every culture—and according to some researchers, even primates and other higher animals like elephants. So, it’s not a religious term although religious people tend to feel comfortable with moral language. People worry that it’s polarizing, that it’s demonizing. And, by that worry, we leave the strongest weapons we have in the language in the hands of people who are least responsible in using them.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: Let’s go actually to senior White House aide Stephen Miller speaking to CNN earlier this year.

STEPHEN MILLER: We live in a world in which you can talk all you want about international niceties and everything else, but we live in a world, in the real world, Jake, that is governed by strength, that is governed by force, that is governed by power. These are the iron laws of the world.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: That’s Stephen Miller. Susan Neiman, your response to that and what that shows about the entire political conversation here under in the Trump administration?

SUSAN NEIMAN: Stephen Miller is echoing two sources; I don’t know if he knows it. One is the Nazi legal theorist Carl Schmitt but the other is something that goes back to the ancient Greeks, and it’s about the Athenians defending a genocide. The Athenians committed a genocide, the island of Melos, because the Melitians [sic] would not bow to their superior power. So that’s the first time you see that argument in history. I have no idea whether Stephen Miller knows that. What’s more disturbing is the fact that he is stating that as the iron law of the world.

And unfortunately, that is what many people today believe—that self-interest and interest in power is the only thing that motivates human beings to action, and we’d better just face it. Now, this is a philosophical idea. People treat it as a fact; it’s a philosophical idea. It’s a piece of ideology. And it’s a piece of ideology that we have been trained for a good century—or bad century, if you like—a good century to accept as fact. What my book does is talk about the ways in which, through misinterpretations of both Adam Smith and Charles Darwin—mixed in the late 20th century with a little but of Michel Foucault—how those ideas got into the water stream. You don’t have to have read any of those people to read them in the media, to hear them on television. But they are ideologies that are every bit as strong as any other totalitarian ideology.

NERMEEN SHAIKH: I just want to ask you, because you do talk about this in the book—what do you think explains the fact that a large portion, a significant portion of the American population, electorate, voted for Trump in the second term?

SUSAN NEIMAN: Because although we could see the immorality in the first term and the tendencies towards fascism—I don’t think that’s too strong an expression—we’re so convinced that moral concepts are for suckers, as he would say, or as Stephen Miller would say—you know, they’re niceties that don’t actually make a difference in the world—that people threw up their hands and were unable to condemn what was actually going on in a shocking kind of way. Many of us have been shocked.

We talk about the contempt for the rule of law that we already saw to some extent in the first administration and we’re seeing even more blatantly now. What we forget is the rule of law depends on people having a commitment to abide by the rule of law even when a legal decision goes against you. That is a moral commitment. James Madison knew that.
