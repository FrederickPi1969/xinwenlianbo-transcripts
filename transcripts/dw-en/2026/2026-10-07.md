## German politicians warn of antisemitism on October 7 anniversary

https://p.dw.com/p/5NvG2Demonstrations were held across Germany commemorating victims of the October 7 attacksImage: Fabrizio Bensch/REUTERSAdvertisementHigh-ranking German politicians marked the anniversary of the terrorist attacks that took place in Israel on October 7, 2023.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz spoke about doing more to fight antisemitism within Germany, while also pressing for a two-state solution between Israelis and Palestinians.

"We are witnessing rising antisemitism in Germany — antisemitism that far too often goes unchallenged," Merz said, adding that his government "will do everything" to combat it.

"Jewish people must be able to live without fear throughout Germany," Merz said in a statement, also emphasizing Germany's support for Israel. "Our Israeli friends can count on our commitment to ensuring that such a crime never happens again."

German President Frank-Walter Steinmeier said antisemitism in Germany "flared up in an intolerable manner since October 7."

"Germany must continue to stand up against antisemitism and for Israel’s security, for the dignity and self-determination of the Palestinians, and for the prospect of peaceful coexistence in the Middle East," he said in a statement.

Federal Parliamentary Speaker Julia Klöckner commemorated the day by emphasizing that more needs to be done to combat antisemitism through "political action."

"Antisemitism should not be allowed to have a place on our streets or our political debates," she said.

Some 1,200 people were killed in the attack, carried out by Palestinian militant group Hamas and its affiliates, while 251 people were kidnapped to Gaza, in the deadliest attack on Jews since the end of the Holocaust.

More than 74,000 people have been killed in the Gaza Strip in Israel's war following the attack, with some countries and human rights groups saying Israel is committing genocide against Palestinians.

Demonstrations were held across Germany for victims of both the Hamas attacks and the Israeli war that followed.

In Berlin, a crowd of around 1,700 people, many waving Israeli flags, gathered at Bebelplatz, according to police estimates. Israeli Ambassador Ron Prosor and German Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt also attended. Bebelplatz was the site of Nazi book burnings following the party's seizure of power in 1933.

Dobrindt called antisemitism a "poison" that threatened to infect parts of society, adding, "We must clearly and consistently stand against it every day."

Germany has supported Israel throughout the war, although Merz did halt of delivery of some military equipment to Israel following growing legal concerns over the war in Gaza, including arrest warrants issued by the International Criminal Court (ICC) against Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and then-Defense Minister Yoav Gallant.

Although Germany is a party to the treaty that created the ICC, Merz vowed not to arrest Netanyahu should he travel to Germany.

## Transgressive, edgy DIY: What the 90s meant for British art

https://p.dw.com/p/5Nur3A new exhibition at Tate Britain takes viewers back to the 90s in BritainImage: Eddie OtchereAdvertisementThe 1990s in Britain are often remembered through a haze of n,ostalgia consisting of rave culture, Britpop and provocative art and fashion by the likes of Tracey Emin and Alexander McQueen.

Undeniably, the decade was a time of major change in British society — the long reign of Margaret Thatcher had come to an end and the West entered a period of relative post-Cold War stability.

Meanwhile, British cultural icons like Oasis, the Spice Girls and Vivienne Westwood garnered international fame.

But Tate Britain's new exhibition "The 90s: Art and Fashion," which opens on October 8 and runs through February 14, 2027, urges viewers to look beyond the familiar names and images.

Even though now prominent artists such as Damien Hirst and Juergen Teller are among the show's 70 contributors, the exhibition also aims to highlight the creative input of those in communities which were left out of the mainstream discourse.

After all, says chief curator Edward Enninful in the exhibition's program, "the energy of the 90s was not concentrated in a single movement, but distributed across communities, disciplines and geographies that have not always been equally represented."

For Enninful, the exhibition is personal: The Ghanaian-born British fashion editor, stylist and creative director became the youngest-ever fashion director of i-D Magazine in the 90s at age 18, helping shape the diverse and experimental London fashion scene of the 1990s. He later became editor-in-chief of British Vogue in 2017.
Enninful writes that he and his co-curators aimed to present a body of work which "reflects a broader landscape of practices and voices that shaped the period, many of which operated beyond the structures of visibility at the time." The exhibition pays special attention to artists of color and women whose work was not necessarily showcased as prominently at the time.

Black masculinity became "a site of cultural negotiation" in Britain in the 90s, writes co-curator Helen Little in the program text: "Figures such as Chris Ofili, Steve McQueen, Keith Piper, Koto Bolofo and Ozwald Boateng reimagined the Black masculine, challenging the racist specter of Black men, who had been defined as part of a masculinity crisis linked to post-industrial decline and excluded from the narrative of 'Cool Britannia.'"

Just like "Swinging London" in the 1960s, "Cool Britannia" became a widespread phrase to refer to the 1990s as an era during which British youth culture, pop music, art and political optimism boomed.

The 90s also offered artists plenty of chances to create without waiting for support from traditional gatekeepers. Britain entered the decade in recession, but that economic uncertainty also left artists free to do what they wished.

Empty buildings and industrial spaces were turned into informal venues. A 23-year-old Damien Hirst orchestrated "Freeze," a self-organized student exhibition in 1988 in a disused London warehouse. It became a landmark early art exhibition for the generation of artists known as the "Young British Artists" or YBA.

Artists Sarah Lucas and Tracey Emin opened The Shop, a temporary art gallery and social space in 1992, as a place where exhibitions could develop outside established galleries. Lucas and Emin also paved the way for women artists thanks to their deeply personal works and their edgy expressions of female liberation.
While the Spice Girls helped make feminism accessible to a younger generation of Brits in the 90s by popularizing the "girl power" slogan and shaping "identity through agency power and resistance," as argues Tate curator Helen Little, Emin and Lucas meanwhile "presented a new visual language of clothing as art to tackle societal taboos around female sexuality, vulnerability and desire" — from t-shirts with provocative slogans to a grunge look of scuffed Dr. Martens boots with razor blades. Their work "held a mirror up to gender, behavior and identity," Little writes.

In a feminine sensuality counter to the gender stereotypes dismissed by Emin and Lucas, waif-like Kate Moss came to represent the feminine ideal of the 1990s. Dubbed "heroin-chic," the style was an antithesis to the glamorous, athletic physiques of the supermodels who preceded her. Corinne Day's photographs of the model led to the raw aesthetic to be labelled "grunge" after the anguished style of rock music hailing from Seattle and led by Nirvana.

The trend has faced a significant cultural reexamination since: "Along with the look also came criticism that the strung-out look of drug or alcohol addiction was glamorized while encouraging serious eating disorders in a generation of young women," points out Alex Farquharson, director of the Tate Gallery in his foreword to the exhibition.

The 1990s were also an exciting era for Britain in haute couture, as legendary designers John Galliano led Diorwhile Alexander McQueen started directing Givenchy at the age of 27.

McQueen's runway presentations were more akin to performance art than commercial fashion shows — and they often included historical critiques. Among them was the famous finale of his Fall/Winter 1998 collection at a London warehouse, during which the model stood in a circle of flames. Titled "Joan," the show was a reference to the killing of Joan of Arc.
McQueen designed several other runway shows that reflected on British history, with topics of conquest and punishment repeatedly coming to the forefront. His 1995 presentation "Highland Rape" referenced the history of Scotland's subjugation, with the designer describing it as "England's rape of Scotland."

As co-curator Jessica Vaughan points out in her exhibition text: "Art, fashion and photography were not simply transgressive reactions … but reflected a genuine public unease with Britain's global identity and its past."
In a world that can now feel increasingly mediated by screens, algorithms and established platforms, the artwork of the 90s offers a reminder of the value of physical spaces and fresh DIY creativity of the last smartphone-free decade.

## EU enlargement: European Commission tables reforms to prepare bloc for new members

https://p.dw.com/p/5Nv2bEU Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos presented the results of the bloc's pre-enlargement policy review on Tuesday: 'Gradual integration is not a substitute for full membership. It is the accelerator for enlargement,' she saidImage: Romeo Boetzle/EUAdvertisementThe European Commission wants to inject new momentum into its enlargement process, which has seen some candidate countries waiting for decades to join.

Albania, Montenegro, Moldova and Ukraine are currently the frontrunners to join the bloc, with EU officials expressing the hope that Montenegro could do so as early as 2028.

"A larger union is a long-term investment in Europe's peace, security and prosperity," Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos told journalists in Strasbourg on October 6.

One key question is how the EU can take in more countries in the near future without becoming harder to run or running the risk of democratic backsliding in new member states.

The Commission's answer to this question is to simultaneously require candidate countries to meet EU standards and get the bloc itself to reform by removing obstacles in the accession process and protecting its democratic values.

One of the main stumbling blocks in accession negotiations so far has been the need for unanimous decisions across all 27 members. In the past, individual states have often blocked a candidate's progress because of bilateral issues.

The delays caused by Hungary's blocking of Ukraine's negotiation process are a prominent example.

Now — and not for the first time — the Commission is recommending that member states move away from the principle of unanimity for decisions like the opening of negotiation clusters, replacing it with qualified majority voting.

"Every cluster that you open is subject to three individual decisions, and every single decision is subject to unanimity. It's mind-blowing, and I think this clearly needs to go," a Commission official, who wished to remain anonymous, told journalists on Tuesday.

The catch is that a shift to qualified majority voting would itself require a unanimous decision.

"I have some reservations as to whether there will be unanimous agreement from the member states to move to qualified majority voting," Tinatin Akhvlediani, head of the enlargement program at the Centre for European Policy Studies (CEPS) told DW, pointing to previous attempts to change voting rules that were shot down by member states.

To incentivize reform in candidate countries, the Commission is proposing gradual integration, which means that these countries could be granted some of the benefits of membership before joining — as long as they meet the EU's criteria.

"Gradual integration is not a substitute for full membership. It is the accelerator for enlargement," Kos said on Tuesday, underlining that the process would stay merit-based.

This could have a motivational effect on candidate countries, Nina Vujanovic, a research fellow at Brussels-based think tank Bruegel, told DW: "Reforms are important, but it is also important to reward that process a little bit earlier, ahead of accession," she said.

Earlier access to EU benefits is not entirely new: Montenegro, for example, already participates in the Single Euro Payments Area (SEPA) even though it is not yet a member.

The Commission now wants to give candidate countries such early access more systematically and link it to strategic alignment.

"This is not something that will be available [to candidate countries] unless they really orient themselves on the EU," a Commission official told journalists.

Making strategic alignment a condition is new and reflects the EU's efforts to adjust to the geopolitical tensions members and candidates are facing.

"The EU is trying to keep candidate countries out of the hands of Russia," Heather Grabbe, a senior fellow at Bruegel, told DW. "Strategic alignment is aimed very squarely at politicians like Aleksandar Vucic in Serbia, who has clearly promoted Russian interests," she said.

The new procedures would demand alignment not only with the EU's foreign and security policy, but also with its standards on economic security. "Trading gas with Russia is simply not something that builds trust with the European Union," said Nina Vujanovic.

To prevent breaches of democratic rules by new member states, the Commission proposes a 15-year period within which it would be easier to sanction new members by cutting financial support or restricting voting rights.

In an interview with The Guardian and other European media, Commissioner Kos made it clear that the EU had learned its lesson from the democratic backsliding it saw in the past in countries like Poland or Hungary: "No more Viktor Orban. We have to have quicker mechanisms than only Article 7."

"It is inherently unfair," says Heather Grabbe. "In the 2040s, why should Montenegro face different conditions to, say, Slovenia?"

Many current candidate countries have a shaky record when it comes to democratic norms, as Tinatin Akhvlediani points out: "They are actually fragile democracies. So, this approach vis-a-vis these countries can be very much justified."

Experts say that while it might be desirable to apply similarly strict rules to all members, it is not realistically feasible as it would have to be approved unanimously and, as Grabbe says, "it's very hard to get turkeys to vote for Christmas."

Although the Commission's push for new rules in relation to enlargement is likely to move the debate forward, it is ultimately up to member states to decide how much of it becomes reality.

The European Council, which is made up of the heads of state or government of the 27 EU member states, the European Council president and the president of the European Commission, is expected to discuss the proposals at its upcoming meeting on October 15–16.

The outcome of those talks will determine how quickly the Commission's new enlargement agenda can move forward.

Sina Yousefi, a human rights lawyer, pointed to international law, specifically Article 12 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, to which Iran is also a signatory.

According to this provision, no one may be arbitrarily prevented from entering their own country. This right cannot be made contingent on a citizen's level of loyalty to the government, he told DW.

"The idea of seeking out certain well-known figures — who the reformists, in particular, believe are less likely to cross the red lines — and winning them over to collaborate or bringing them back to the country is not new," said Sepideh Raissadat , an ethnomusicologist who has been studying the history and development of Persian music for years.

As an internationally renowned performer of classical Persian music, she combines her artistic work with scholarly research, with her dissertation focusing on the lives of Iranian female singers since the 1979 Islamic Revolution.

"The 1979 revolution in Iran was a revolution against music. Music was one of the red lines," said Raissadat, referring to statements by the leader of the revolution, Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini, who as early as July 1979 had publicly described music as harmful to society and called for its complete abolition.

In a speech to employees of Radio Darya on July 21, 1979, Khomeini is said to have described music as "a betrayal of the country" and "a betrayal of the youth." A few days later, the Iranian newspaper Keyhan quoted him as saying that music was "like a drug” and must be completely eradicated.

During the Iran-Iraq War from 1980 to 1988, however, music made a comeback in the form of revolutionary and religious songs. It was also used to stir up feelings of national pride and a sense of belonging.

The regime also failed to completely banish the female voice from the public sphere. "They couldn't completely eliminate the voice of women. The female voice returned through choral singing," said Raissadat.

For years, musical instruments were not allowed to be shown on Iranian television. Instruments were confiscated or destroyed on the streets. Fearing repercussions, musicians did not carry their instruments in public, while some hid them in their cars.

"Listening to banned music at home was a political act. And when women began to sing, that, too, became a political act," Raissadat noted. The development of Iranian pop music was also part of this political and cultural tension, she underscored. "People began producing new domestic pop music to compete with music from Los Angeles."

Over the past ten years, social media — which is not entirely under government control — has become increasingly important and now wields considerable influence.

This change is particularly evident among young people, with young women now even playing the guitar without headscarves and performing in public.

Raissadat observes a particular trend among the families of the young people who were killed during the nationwide mass protests in late December 2025 and January 2026.

The demonstrations began on December 28, 2025, and were violently suppressed by security forces.

Human rights organizations report that thousands of protesters were killed, but the exact figures are unknown due to the extensive information blackout imposed by the regime.

"The most important thing we've seen in recent years has been the use of music by the bereaved families of those killed in the protests. This stands in complete contrast to Shiite tradition and our history," said Raissadat.

At some funerals, families turned the memorial service for their loved ones into a new form of protest.

Instead of religious hymns, music played, and the mourners sang and danced along to it. In doing so, they created a deliberate contrast to traditional religious mourning rituals, the musician said.

"Music became a weapon. Those who were mourning were able to connect with it on their own, and at the same time, it stood in opposition to what the political system stands for," Raissadat pointed out, adding, "Dancing in white clothing also became an expression of this resistance."

https://p.dw.com/p/5Nm2xThe ECDC is closely monitoring the situation in Siberia after the death of a Russian laboratory workerImage: Britta Pedersen/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementUPDATE: Following initial reports raising concerns that a lab worker had died accidentally of pneumonic plague, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) said it was now also "closely monitoring the situation."

All that is known for certain, at the time of writing and first publication (October 5, 2026), is that pneumonic plague is highly contagious.

Pneumonic plague is a form of plague that infects the lungs. Like every form of the disease, it is caused by Yersinia pestis, a bacterium usually found in small mammals, such as rodents and in the fleas that feed on them.

The World Health Organization describes the pneumonic form as the most virulent type of plague.

Its incubation period can be as short as 24 hours, and it is the only form that passes easily from person to person through respiratory particles released when a sick patient coughs. This is why, according to the WHO, it can trigger severe epidemics.

Symptoms appear quickly: high fever, headache and weakness, followed by a rapidly worsening pneumonia with shortness of breath, chest pain and a cough that often brings up blood.

A person can catch pneumonic plague by breathing in the bacteria directly, or by developing it when bubonic and/or septicemic plague go untreated and spread to the lungs.

Pneumonic plague is not a separate disease. It is one of three forms of plague: bubonic, septicemic and pneumonic.

What sets them apart is where the infection settles in the body, and that shapes how the disease spreads and how deadly it is.

Bubonic plague is the most common form and the one most people think of when they hear the word "plague." It usually follows a flea bite. The bacteria travel to the nearest lymph node, which swells into a painful lump known as a bubo. According to the WHO, it rarely spreads between people.

If bubonic plague goes untreated, the bacteria can move further through the body. If they spread into the bloodstream, the disease can become septicemic plague, which causes bleeding under the skin, tissue death in the fingers and toes, and organ failure. If the bacteria reach the lungs, the illness becomes pneumonic plague, the form that can pass from person to person through the air. Both septicemic and pneumonic plague can also develop directly, without a bubo appearing first.

Left untreated, bubonic plague kills between 30-60% of patients. The septicemic and pneumonic forms are always fatal without treatment.

Pneumonic plague is curable, but only if treatment starts quickly. The WHO warns that without treatment it can kill within 18 to 24 hours of the first symptoms.

Common antibiotics can cure the infection, and recovery rates are high when patients are treated within about a day of falling ill.

Speed is what decides the outcome. Doctors are advised not to wait for laboratory confirmation and to start antibiotics as soon as plague is suspected, according to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

The drugs themselves are not rare or specialized. They are standard, widely used antibiotics, and patients usually need a course of up to two weeks. To stop the infection spreading, patients are kept in isolation, and wearing masks helps protect those around them, the WHO says.

People close to a patient are treated as well, even if they have no symptoms. Both the WHO and the CDC recommend a seven-day course of preventive antibiotics for close contacts of anyone with pneumonic plague.

The WHO does not recommend a plague vaccine for the public. It reserves vaccination for high-risk groups, such as laboratory staff who work with the bacterium and health workers.

## Nobelitis — How winning a Nobel Prize can give you delusions of grandeur

https://p.dw.com/p/5NgHkWinning the Nobel Prize can go to anyone's head — James Watson, who was honored in 1962, is a prime exampleImage: Ivan Sekretarev/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementLinus Pauling won not one, but two Nobel Prizes — the first, for chemistry, in 1954 for his research into the nature of the chemical bond, and the Peace Prize in 1962 for his work in the anti-nuclear weapons movement.

The American chemist not only won two Nobel Prizes, but won them on his own, a rare feat for any scientist. Such an honor often turns laureates into global superstars overnight, with millions hanging on their every word.

That instant fame can be dangerous, said Nils Hansson, a professor in the Department of History, Theory and Ethics of Medicine at Heinrich Heine University in Düsseldorf. Especially, he said, when these world-renowned experts "start spreading crazy, unproven scientific theories" — ideas that, when disseminated by Nobel laureates, risk being taken seriously.

That's what happened with Pauling. Beginning in the mid 1960s he began claiming that vitamin C was a universal remedy, a cure for everything from cancer to cardiovascular conditions and degenerative diseases like Alzheimer's disease, multiple sclerosis or osteoarthritis.

Pauling developed an alternative treatment he called orthomolecular medicine, based on specific doses of vitamins and minerals for each patient. To this day, some 60 years later, evidence for his methods remains inconclusive.

Pauling's case is a classic example of "Nobelitis," or "Nobel disease" — not an official diagnosis, but an informal term used to describe how some laureates begin to suffer delusions of grandeur later in life. It was first coined by Paul Nurse, president of The Royal Society, the UK's national academy of sciences, and himself a winner of the Nobel Prize.

"Be skeptical of everything, including yourself and your own ideas," Nurse said in a 2019 interview with KU Leuven University in Belgium, when asked what made someone a good scientist. In cases of Nobelitis, that self-skepticism is the first to go.

That was also the case with virologist Luc Montagnier, who was jointly honored in 2008 for his discovery of the human immunodeficiency virus, HIV, with colleague Francoise Barre-Sinoussi in the early 1980s.

Montagnier would later go on to support homeopathic medicine, another alternative treatment of disease and claim that HIV transmission in Africa could be diminished through better hygiene and nutrition.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, Montagnier said the SARS-CoV-2 virus had been created in a lab with parts of the HIV genetic code. "It's difficult for a practicing virologist to say that a Nobel laureate in virology is spreading nonsense," said German virologist Christian Drosten on NDR's Coronavirus Update podcast in May 2020. "But that is utter nonsense."

James Watson, who was never known for his modesty, also let fame go to his head. In 1962, he was awarded the Nobel Prize along with Maurice Wilkins and Francis Crick for their discovery of the structure of the DNA molecule. But in the early 2000s, Watson repeatedly spread racist theories that Black people were less intelligent than white people.

For medical historian Hansson, Watson is a prime example that the Nobel Prize is not automatically awarded to a person who should be considered as a role model.

"You may have made a fantastic discovery, but that doesn't make you a universal genius," he said.

But the great expectations of an admiring public may also contribute to the development of Nobelitis. Paul Nurse, who won the Nobel Prize in physiology or medicine in 2001, soon found himself getting daily requests for interviews on all sorts of topics.

"As if suddenly I was acknowledgeable about all workings of the world and of human beings," he told DW.

Nurse had this advice for researchers: "Be careful quite what you say. Talk about things that you do know something about that would be fine. And if you don't know so much or you're not sure about something, be really clear that you're not sure."

Hansson has also noticed that the media eagerly report whatever Nobel laureates say — even when prize winners stray far from their area of expertise.

Nobelitis sufferers are leaders in their fields, and rightly honored for their work with the Nobel. But they're also regular individuals, not immune to megalomania and other behavioral problems — meaning that homeopathy, orthomolecular medicine and other pseudosciences don't automatically become credible just because they're backed by a Nobel Prize winner.

## The solar boom is rapidly transforming emerging economies

https://p.dw.com/p/5NT2NSolar panels are becoming a common sight in rural parts of Ethiopia. Farmers who previously used diesel generators for irrigation are switching to solar power, like here in the village Dore Bafeno in the Sidama region in 2026.Image: Shewangizaw Wegayehu/DWAdvertisementAs emerging economies and global living standards rise, is the world in for another fossil fuel boom? The data says not. Countries from Southeast Asia to Sub-Saharan Africa aren't investing in fossil fuels as the West did; they're investing in electricity powered by renewables.

When the US and Europe industrialized in the 19th and 20th centuries, they did so using fossil energy. From coal ovens, gas heating and combustion-engine cars to electricity produced in coal and gas power plants, entire societies relied on polluting fuels.

Yet while burning fossil fuels allowed industrialized countries to boom, it also caused the climate crisis that is linked to extreme weather events around the world today.

Many of the same countries that became economically powerful off the back of fossil fuels are now trying to transition to renewables, but centuries of legacy infrastructure hold them back.

Because a gas boiler won't run on wind power and a diesel truck cannot be solar-powered, societies looking to shift to green energy need to use more electricity and increase their share of renewables.

That is where many middle- and lower-income countries have an advantage. With less existing energy infrastructure built around fossil fuels, they are better placed for a faster rollout of renewable electricity sources.

In many instances, solar power in particular is now cheaper to produce than fossil electricity. And unlike large coal or gas plants, which take years to construct and millions in investments, solar and wind farms can be built quickly and with much less upfront cost.

At the same time, according to an analysis by renewables think tank Ember, electronic devices like refrigerators, electric stoves or air conditioners have all become much cheaper in the last decades, making electricity a more affordable option as an energy source for cooking, heating and more.

While electric cars are still a luxury item in most places, their prices are coming down too. And a UN study found that in India, Indonesia and Vietnam electric scooters and motorbikes already cost the same as petrol models. Yet a lot less to fuel.

All this means that renewable electricity is quickly becoming the best choice for the wallet — not just for the climate. And countries are taking note.

"We're seeing a leapfrog take place right across the emerging markets,” said Kingsmill Bond, energy strategist at Ember. "The whole of Asia is electrifying quickly, and the whole of Europe and the United States is stagnant."

In 2023, only around a quarter of global electricity was generated by renewables. But that figure is rising fast, and almost all new capacity added these days is based on solar or wind power.

Lower-income countries already use less fossil fuels and more renewables than higher-income ones.

And large middle-income economies are outpacing richer ones as well. In 2023, solar power alone made up at least six percent and ten percent of electricity in India and Vietnam, respectively. The global average was five percent.

This boom has meant middle- and lower-income countries are electrifying fast, consuming less energy directly from fossil fuels than richer countries. Vietnam, for instance, is already using more electricity than the US, Europe and even China, and India is following the same path.

But the potential for energy leapfrogging might be particularly high in low-income countries, especially across Sub-Saharan Africa.

Countries such as Kenya, Ethiopia and Namibia are rapidly expanding their energy infrastructure to meet the demands of a growing, young population. But rural areas in many countries are not connected to electricity grids.

Joel Nana, project manager at South African NGO Sustainable Energy Africa says that "even when there is access to power, it's often not reliable."

Until a few years ago, he says, people defaulted to diesel generators to meet their energy needs. That came at a cost because importing fuel is particularly expensive in many African countries, meaning energy crises and oil price fluctuations hit much harder.

These factors make solar power a solution for both energy access and independence. And it has become so cheap that Nana says it's a "no-brainer."

"Some businesses can make back their investment in just two years, so why would you want to deploy anything else?” he said. "Solar has rapidly become the de facto default technology that everyone falls back on.”

And once it is set up, it is very effective. Sub-Saharan Africa is extremely well-suited to solar power. It gets more sun than almost anywhere in the world, and has enough space for solar farms to cover electricity demand hundreds of times over.

"If you add solar plus batteries, you can get cheap electricity about 90 percent of the time from the sun alone”, said Kingsmill Bond.

Countries like Namibia, South Africa, Kenya and Senegal have expanded their solar capacity massively in the past few years.

In a 2026 report for African Tech Futures Lab and Ember, Nana analyzed import data from China and estimated that around 100,000 solar panels were installed across Africa every day in 2026, up 45 percent from just the previous year.

Similar trends are emerging all across the world. In Pakistan, for example, people have been buying solar panels from China en masse in the past few years to avoid steep electricity grid prices and frequent power outages.

Aleksi Lumijärvi, director for knowledge, policy and finance at the International Renewable Energy Association says these decisions can have a major impact on the global economy in the coming decades.

"Industrial companies will find that it will be cheaper to move production to, for example, somewhere in Sub-Saharan Africa, because they have this cheap, clean solar power plus batteries available.”

"You're going to have faster growth in the emerging markets, you will have fewer financial crises. It will make a material difference to people's quality of lives.”

Nana says the next step for African countries is to establish policies that ensure everyone benefits from the solar boom.
