## Ukraine tries to shut Moscow&apos;s airports with drone strikes on Russia

https://p.dw.com/p/5OZMDPassengers wait in line at Moscow's Sheremetyevo International AirportImage: Alexey Belkin/Russian Look/news.ru/press.media/picture allianceAdvertisementFor months, drone strikes by Ukraine have forced the suspension of flights to and from airports in Russia. Moscow's Domodedovo, Sheremetyevo, Zhukovsky and Vnukovo airports have had to shut down operations almost daily in October, according to reports from the independent investigative media outlet Agentstvo. On some days, the airports were reportedly closed for up to nine hours.

In early September, President Volodymyr Zelenskyy announced that Ukraine would seek to cut off the airspace around Moscow and other destinations in Russia. "Today, we would like to warn every airline that uses Russian airspace, every insurer, and everyone who continues to use major Russian airports: Russian airspace will become completely unsafe," Zelenskyy said.

The following day, Ukraine sent a corresponding warning to the International Civil Aviation Organization.

Ukraine's airspace has been closed since the start of Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022.

Russia's aviation authority, Rosaviatsiya, has issued an emergency safety "carpet" protocol that is designed to immediately shut down the airspace over a specific region or around affected airports if an aircraft violates Russian airspace, weapons are used, or unidentified objects appear in the skies. Once the protocol is announced, drastic restrictions apply to all air traffic.

"If there is a military threat, a specific section of airspace is closed off, and this carpet is declared," Oleksandr Laneckij, the chief executive officer of the Friendly Avia Support consultancy, told DW. "Essentially, every drone poses a military threat."

Laneckij noted that Yemen's Houthis have managed to significantly disrupt Saudi airspace. "I think Ukraine's military capabilities far exceed those of the Houthis," Laneckij said.

The military analyst Jan Matvejev also said Ukraine would have little trouble severely disrupting Moscow-area air traffic. Though Ukraine has not yet managed to completely shut down Russian airspace as Zelenskyy plans, Matvejev said, its armed forces "remain committed to this idea."

"To close off the airspace and trigger the 'carpet' plan, all you need are simple drones that don't have to be equipped with a warhead or even have a long range," Matvejev said. Ukraine's arms industry can produce hundreds of combat-ready long-range drones in a day, he added. That is why he believes Ukraine could have the necessary resources to shut down Moscow's airports.

Indeed, Ukraine's efforts to shut down Russian airspace are "just getting started," Simon Shuster wrote in October in the US magazine The Atlantic, citing well-informed sources.

Matvejev said Russia's air-defense systems were not entirely effective in efforts to prevent Ukrainian drones from penetrating Moscow's airspace. "If it were possible to shoot them down, they would have done so by now," he said. He added that Russia would have deployed additional air-defense troops if they were available.

Laneckij said Russia might lower the threshold for declaring the carpet protocol, which could increase the risk of deadly incidents such as the 2024 Christmas day crash of an Azerbaijan Airlines flight. Investigators say the flight was struck by a Russian air-defense missile that was being used to defend against drones, killing 38 people.

Matvejev seconded the fear that Russia's defenses could again mistakenly target civilian aircraft instead of military objects. He said little had changed about Russia's air-defense protocol since the Azeri flight was shot in 2024.

In an operation against a low-flying drone, the target is visually identified and fired upon with a machine gun. In such a scenario, the danger to aircraft is extremely low, unless they happen to be flying in the immediate vicinity.

The risk to civil aviation increases significantly when anti-aircraft missiles are deployed, Matvejev said. A missile could have defects that are practically unavoidable in mass production and could veer toward an aircraft flying nearby. Matvejev said such defense measures could not guarantee the safety of civilian aircraft during operations.

He said drones did not have to carry explosives to be disruptive. A collision between an airplane and a basic drone can have serious consequences — and, he said, a drone sent from Ukraine all the way to Moscow must be relatively large, "weighting dozens of kilograms."

In the summer of 2023, Ukrainian armed forces attacked the airport in the Russian city of Pskov, where both military and civilian aircraft were parked. Earlier this month, drone debris fell on the Sochi airport, on Russia's Black Sea coast.

Russia also attacks Ukraine's airports. In September, Zelenskyy said Kyiv's airport had been struck, as well as the Boryspil airport near the capital.

Laneckij said three factors would determine whether international airlines suspend their flights to Russia: the military threat, the degree of demand and the insurance cost.

He said issues could emerge around insurance. When insurers rate the security risk of flying to Russia as very high, they could significantly raise their rates for aircraft headed there. This would cause ticket prices to skyrocket.

Competition within Russia's aviation market has already declined because many airlines are no longer in business, Laneckij said. This has increased demand for tickets from the remaining foreign airlines.

"Whether they stay or not depends on the price they have to pay for the danger faced," he said.

## Japan-Russia ties erode as journalist sits in Moscow jail

https://p.dw.com/p/5OanLJournalist Yuki Imai seen being taken into custody by Russian security agents Image: Russian Federal Security Service/TASS/picture allianceAdvertisementJapanese Foreign Ministry officials were in Moscow this week in an attempt to contact a journalist arrested in Russia's far east on spying claims.

Journalist Yuki Imai was arrested on October 14 by Russia's FSB security service in the city of Yuzhno-Sakhalinsk on Sakhalin Island. Televised footage of the arrest showed FSB officers barging into his hotel room as he slept.

The correspondent for the Hokkaido Shimbun newspaper was accused of attempting to obtain information on soldiers in the region, which is near the Kiril islands, a geopolitical sore spot in Russia-Japan relations.

Russian authorities quickly transferred him to Lefortovo Prison in Moscow, where a court ordered him to be held in pre-trial detention until at least December 12. If convicted, 34-year-old Imai faces up to 20 years in jail, according to the International Federation of Journalists, which has called for his immediate release.

Officials of the Japanese embassy in Moscow have been refused access to Imai. In Tokyo, Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi has condemned the arrest and dismissed the claim that Imai was a spy.

Russian Ambassador Nikolay Nozdrev said Imai had been caught "red-handed," Russia's TASS news agency reported. He has claimed that Japanese intelligence agencies use Japanese journalists to collect classified information with the goal of "undermining Russia's security and defense capability."

Yoji Gomi, a veteran journalist and author who has reported from across Asia, told DW Japanese journalists may occasionally meet with diplomats in the course of their work and "share thoughts and impressions." However, he rejects the idea that reporters are paid spies.

"One-party nations often see foreign correspondents as spies because that is exactly what they use their journalists for," he said.

"I can completely see how the Russians decided that Imai was a spy, as it is how they operate, plus he was in a sensitive part of Russia where there are lots of North Korean laborers that they want to keep quiet," Gomi added.

Stephen Nagy, a professor of international relations at Tokyo's International Christian University, said that Russia may see Imai as a bargaining chip it can use to exchange for its nationals detained overseas.

"They are going to resist because it means that Russia, or any other country, can then just seize a Japanese national, put them on trial for espionage and demand the release of one of their people," he told DW.

The arrest comes after a member of a Russia-linked hacking group "Qilin" was arrested in Japan and extradited to Germany to face charges in connection with a 2024 cyberattack on a logistics center.

The last time a journalist was arrested in Russia was in 2019 for "attempting to obtain classified information about Russia's military potential in the Far East," Russia's Interfax news agency reported. However, in this case, the journalist was quickly released.

Imai's case is perhaps the most serious since 2023, when Wall Street Journal reporter Evan Gershkovich was arrested in Russia and sentenced to 16 years in a penal colony before being released in a prisoner exchange with Western countries in 2024.

Imai's arrest is the latest in a series of incidents that have sent bilateral relations into a tailspin.

Nagy said Imai's seizure is designed to put pressure on Tokyo. He added this is part of a coordinated approach with China to isolate Japan at a time when the US is preoccupied with domestic issues and the conflict in the Middle East.

"Moscow and Beijing are coordinating to create the image that Japan is not a reformed member of the international community but still the military state of the 1930s and that it poses a threat to stability in the Indo-Pacific region,"  Nagy said.

"This is all designed to smear Japan's efforts to lead diplomacy in the region at a time when the US is perhaps distracted," he told DW. "And it does not even matter much that it is not true; they will just continue to repeat the claim to discredit and weaken Tokyo."

Tadashi Anno, a professor of politics at Tokyo's Sophia University, believes Japan had been working hard to maintain a stable relationship with Moscow, even after Tokyo joined international condemnation of the invasion of Ukraine.

"Japan condemned the invasion of Ukraine in 2022 because it was a fundamental violation of international law, but Russia is only now pushing back hard," he told DW.

At the center of deteriorating ties is a territorial dispute about a chain of islands off the far north of Japan that were seized by Soviet forces after Japan's surrender in World War II.

Known as the Northern Territories in Japan and the Southern Kurils by Russia, there have been hopes in the past in Tokyo that a concerted diplomatic effort could be sufficient to convince Russia to return them to Japanese sovereignty.

That campaign has faltered in the last couple of years, however, and was further dented when Japan announced in the spring that it would be joining the NATO initiative to provide non-lethal assistance to Kyiv.

In August, President Vladimir Putin visited the disputed islands, only the second Russian leader to do so. Earlier this month, a delegation of Chinese businesspeople was welcomed on one of the islands and talks are under way on the possibility of economic cooperation.

## Uganda says it has no active Ebola cases, but infections in Kenya and Nigeria are prompting fresh scrutiny

https://p.dw.com/p/5OaQWBoth patients are known to have traveled through Uganda's Entebbe International Airport on October 3Image: Sumy Sadurni/AFP/Getty ImagesAdvertisementThe first confirmed Ebola patients in Kenya and Nigeria may have crossed paths at Uganda's Entebbe International Airport, according to African health officials, as new information from Switzerland contradicts Uganda's account of the Nigerian patient's travel history.

Uganda, a country reporting no active Ebola infections, has become a focus of investigations into cases thousands of kilometers apart.

Uganda insists it has no confirmed active cases of the deadly virus. Yet health authorities in Kenya and Nigeria are investigating Ebola infections involving people whose travel histories or contacts are connected to the East African nation.

Both patients passed through Entebbe International Airport on October 3, according to Wessam Mankoula, head of emergency preparedness and response at the Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC).

Health officials say the two may have crossed paths, although it has not been established whether they encountered each other or whether any transmission occurred at the airport.

The differing accounts have raised questions about whether Uganda was simply a transit point for infected travelers, or could infections be going undetected along some of Africa's busiest cross-border routes?

The latest developments come after Switzerland disputed claims that the Nigerian patient had visited the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), where an Ebola outbreak is underway.

Nigeria's Health Minister Muhammad Ali Pate confirmed an imported Ebola case in the Nigerian capital, Abuja, involving a woman who had recently traveled to East Africa, including Uganda.

"Today we are confirming that there has been one imported case of Ebola virus disease in the Federal Capital Territory," Pate said.

The woman developed symptoms on October 7 and visited a private health facility on October 11. Further laboratory testing confirmed Ebola, and she was subsequently isolated for treatment.

Pate said the patient was stable and that health officials had identified 44 contacts, including healthcare workers, caregivers, patients and people who may have encountered her during her travels.

Speaking on Thursday, Mankoula said the first confirmed patients in Kenya and Nigeria were both known to have traveled through Entebbe International Airport on October 3. Investigators are examining whether the two patients were at the airport at the same time and whether their movements overlapped.

Uganda's Health Ministry says preliminary findings indicate the Nigerian patient traveled to eastern DRCfor business before passing through Uganda without symptoms and returning to Nigeria.

"Travel through Uganda does not establish that infection occurred here. The location of exposure remains under investigation," the ministry said in an October 20 statement.

But on Friday, Switzerland's Federal Department of Foreign Affairs directly disputed the account.

"She and her husband did not travel to the Democratic Republic of the Congo," the department said in a statement to Reuters news agency.

The woman, identified by Swiss authorities as a Swiss national of Ugandan origin, is married to a Swiss diplomat based in Abuja.

On Thursday, the Swiss government told Reuters that she was believed to have contracted Ebola while traveling with her husband in Uganda, although that assessment has not been independently confirmed.

The World Health Organization (WHO) and the Africa CDC also said Thursday that they had not received confirmed information establishing that the woman had traveled to the DRC.

The conflicting accounts raise fresh questions about where the woman was exposed to the virus and how accurately her movements were initially reconstructed. They do not, however, establish that she contracted Ebola in Uganda.

Uganda maintains it has no confirmed active Ebola cases and says it is awaiting more detailed information from Nigeria about the woman's movements.

In Kenya, Uganda has emerged in a separate investigation involving the country's first confirmed Ebola patient, who died in the Kenyan capital, Nairobi, on October 5. The patient had traveled from the Democratic Republic of Congo through Uganda before reaching Kenya.

Health officials have now established that the patient passed through Entebbe International Airport on October 3, the same day as the woman subsequently diagnosed in Nigeria. Kenyan authorities have since reported additional infections linked to the initial case.

According to Kenya's Daily Nation, health officials are searching for a Ugandan national identified as a contact of the deceased patient.

"We know him (the Ugandan) by name and passport number and from his phone but we are not able to reach him," Kenya's Director-General for Health Patrick Amoth told the newspaper.

The Daily Nation reported that the man was using a South Sudanese telephone number, complicating efforts to establish his whereabouts.

Being an untraced contact does not mean he is infected. However, locating potentially exposed individuals is essential to preventing further transmission.

Uganda shares a lengthy border with the Democratic Republic of Congo, where Ebola outbreaks have repeatedly challenged health authorities.

Cross-border trade, population movements and international travel make it difficult to establish where someone was exposed to the virus.

Ebola symptoms typically develop between 2 and 21 days after infection. According to the WHO, people infected with Ebola are not considered infectious until symptoms appear.

## Serbia: Hope, tension and apprehension as voters prepare to elect new parliament

https://p.dw.com/p/5OZWQThree days before a pivotal parliamentary election in Serbia, supporters of the Student List poured into central Belgrade for a final campaign rallyImage: Marko Djurica/REUTERSAdvertisementA sense of hope swept through Belgrade on Thursday evening as thousands gathered for the final rally of the Student List, the group of candidates put forward by the student movement that has been protesting against the government for almost exactly two years.

The Student List is calling for an end to the current regime in Serbia via the ballot box in Sunday's parliamentary election.

Many of those who attended the rally had spent days walking to the Serbian capital from all four corners of the country, turning the campaign finale into a display of solidarity and peaceful determination.

"We have walked across Serbia from one end to the other, visited villages and neighborhoods, looked you in the eye and realized that we can count on you," biology student Arsa Stojanovic told the crowd.

"People, it smells like freedom," added Aleksandar Cupic, professor of transport and traffic engineering, and a candidate on the Student List.

After 24 months of protests sparked by the collapse of a concrete canopy at Novi Sad railway station, which killed 16 people in November 2024, Serbia is heading into an election unlike any other in the 14 years since the Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) first came to power.

For the first time in years, the ruling party faces a serious political challenge, which this time comes from a student movement that has channeled widespread public discontent into an electoral campaign.

"These are the first elections since [Aleksandar] Vucic came to power in 2012 that are competitive and whose outcome is uncertain," political scientist Dusan Spasojevic told DW. "The student movement has generated hope among citizens and substantial support. Polls suggest that they could even win more than 40% of the vote."

The Student List has framed the election as a chance to confront corruption and demand accountability for a disaster that became a symbol of institutional failure.

In doing so, the students have sought to put aside the ideological, social and foreign-policy divisions that have long shaped Serbian politics.

The message here was familiar: A vote for the SNS means stability, continuity and further prosperity, while a change of government would risk plunging the country into chaos.

The party framed the election as a battle for Serbia's future and sovereignty, accusing its opponents of serving foreign interests.

"They tried to destroy our country on foreign orders, and that is why this is the hardest battle," former President Aleksandar Vucic told his supporters. "They attacked us with billions of euros, and we defended ourselves with integrity, our program, love for our homeland and Serbia's achievements."

The campaign has increasingly been overshadowed by violence, reflecting a society in which political opponents are often portrayed not simply as rivals, but as enemies.

By the final week of the campaign, CRTA, an independent election-monitoring organization, had recorded 116 incidents involving physical violence or verbal threats. The incidents included physical confrontations, attacks on campaigners and attempts to seize campaign banners.

CRTA also reported that opposition activists, particularly those linked to the Student List, were also subjected to surveillance  and police questioning. In some cases, the organization said, police were slow to protect opposition candidates and supporters.

In the final runup to the election, there have also been a series of investigative reports examining alleged links between people connected to the ruling SNS and criminal networks.

Published by independent media in the days before the election, the reports have brought renewed attention to questions of corruption, political influence and accountability — issues at the heart of the student movement's challenge to the government.

But Spasojevic is skeptical that the revelations will make a significant difference to the election outcome.

He argues that those who trust investigative media are likely to have already made up their minds, while voters who rely on pro-government outlets may never even see these reports.

CRTA told DW that the election campaign has not been either free or fair from day one, citing "a narrowing of freedoms and deepening inequality" among candidates and voters.

Its report found a pronounced imbalance in media coverage: Almost three-quarters of airtime devoted to political players on national television has featured government representatives, while more than 80% of coverage on the Student List has been negative.

Ahead of the vote, the Balkan Investigative Reporting Network (BIRN) published the testimony of a former member of the SNS, who described how public-sector employees were pressured to deliver votes for the party.

DW has also learned from sources who want to remain anonymous that voters have been offered between €30 and €150 to vote for the SNS, with holiday packages reportedly offered to entire families if they pledge all their votes to the ruling party.

Many fear a period of turbulence, uncertainty and political crisis after the vote, with election night itself a particular source of anxiety.

For weeks, the authorities have warned that students, allegedly with the help of foreign intelligence services, are preparing violence in the streets. In the final days of the campaign, several people were arrested on what the prosecution has said was the suspicion of plotting disturbances on election night.

The best-case scenario, says Dusan Spasojevic, would be a decisive victory for one of the two opposing camps, leaving little room for the other to challenge the result.

"But polls suggest that the margin will be narrow, and we will probably enter some kind of post-election crisis," he said, adding that a narrow victory for the ruling party would almost certainly be contested, given longstanding concerns about the fairness of the electoral process and allegations that some votes may have been obtained illegally.

"I think the international response will be crucial in preventing major incidents and a further escalation of the crisis. It is the only thing that can persuade the authorities to accept defeat and refrain from an overly heavy-handed response to any protests or incidents," he told DW.

An EU Commission spokesperson acknowledged to DW that key recommendations on improving Serbia's electoral framework remain unimplemented and also stressed that democracy and the rule of law are key to the accession process. Serbia has been an EU candidate country since 2012.

## Sri Lanka: Death of iconic elephant raises habitat concerns

https://p.dw.com/p/5OYtBThe 11-foot bull elephant 'Kavantissa' died after being shot in the leg earlier in OctoberImage: newswire.lkAdvertisementThe recent death of an iconic male elephant in Sri Lanka has raised concerns over ongoing contact between humans and the giant animals, whose habitats have been greatly reduced.

The elephant known as "Kavantissa" had been considered to be the tallest living male elephant in the Sri Lanka. Thought to be around 45 years old and measuring roughly 3.3 meters tall (11 feet), he roamed Sri Lanka's northwest near the village of Galgamuwa, and was widely admired for his majestic stature.

In late September, villagers noticed the 5.5 ton-tusker (5,500 kg) was limping, and a closer look reveled a gunshot wound on his right leg. Despite the intervention of veterinary surgeons, the elephant died on October 16.

Sri Lanka records the highest number of elephant deaths annually and the second highest number of human deaths globally from violent interactions between elephants and humans, which usually happen when crop raiding elephants encroach on farmlands.

According to official statistics, since 2019, an average of 370 elephants and 125 humans have died in human-elephant conflict incidents each year.

The situation affects the natural habitats of elephants and the livelihoods of rural communities.

Home to about 10% of the global Asian elephant population and occupying about 2% of their total natural range, Sri Lanka has the highest density of Asian elephants in the world.

The Sri Lankan elephant is an endangered subspecies in the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List of Threatened Species, and protected under Sri Lankan law, with punishment by fines and jail terms for wildlife-related offences.

During the past few decades, forest cover in Sri Lanka has shrunk as the population has grown.

Statistics show the rural population of Sri Lanka increased from 8.25 million in 1960 to 17.8 million in 2020.

Agriculture remains the primary source of income for these rural communities. As a result, the land is cleared for agricultural activities.

According to Department of Wildlife Conservation (DWC) statistics, in 2024, an estimated 7,451 wild elephants were recorded in Sri Lanka. About 70% of these wild elephants live outside protected areas.

Research shows elephants have a massive daily dietary requirement of about 150 kilograms (330 lbs.) of green matter, which drives them into farmland.

"Driven primarily by habitat loss, agricultural expansion, fragmentation of elephant corridors, and increasing competition for land and resources, human-elephant conflict continues to cause hundreds of elephant deaths and more than 100 human fatalities annually," said Ravi Corea, President of Sri Lanka Wildlife Conservation Society, a Sri Lankan non-profit conservation organization.

The conservationist told DW that rural communities are severely affected, and the agricultural losses caused by elephants can threaten a family's primary source of food and income.

"This distinguishes human-elephant conflict from many human-wildlife conflicts in economically developed countries, where wildlife encounters, although sometimes serious, generally do not threaten household subsistence to the same extent," he said.

"Despite the efforts by the government to mitigate HEC, the numbers are rising demonstrating the continuing severity of the crisis," Corea added.

Elephants are shot, poisoned, or killed by improvised explosives hidden in food and train collisions, experts say.

In view of the escalation of human-elephant conflict, the Sri Lankan government in 2020 drafted a national action plan to mitigate the problem.

"The 2020 Action Plan provides a valuable foundation for reducing human–elephant conflict, but its recommendations must be strengthened through a comprehensive national conservation strategy with enforceable habitat protection at its core,"  Corea said.

"The ultimate measure of success must be not merely a reduction in human–elephant conflict, but the permanent protection of ecologically connected habitats, the survival of viable wild elephant populations and the ability of people and elephants to coexist sustainably for generations to come," he added.

Meanwhile, community-based programs by non-government organizations have proven effective and sustainable. These initiatives are complemented by elephant monitoring, scientific research, habitat assessments, community education, and the development of locally appropriate deterrents, Corea said

"Our flagship initiative Project Orange Elephant in Sri Lanka's Wasgamuwa region promotes the cultivation of citrus which elephants do not like to eat as a trophic deterrence. This reduces crop raiding while generating alternative income for farmers," Corea added, pointing to things like electric fencing protecting settlements and agricultural lands while encouraging local responsibility.

Another issue is confrontations on elephant corridors, that in some cases have discouraged children from walking to school.

Elephant corridors are designated strips of land that connect larger forest and habitat patches, allowing elephants to move safely between them for food, water, breeding, and seasonal migration.

To address this, the "EleFriendly" bus service in Wasgamuwa, provides safe transportation for schoolchildren through elephant corridors and has reduced dangerous human-elephant encounters along its original route by over 90%.

"An ethically just solution must protect human lives, livelihoods, and elephant populations simultaneously, said Kamal Shashendra, a Sri Lanka-based environmental ethics researcher.

He told DW that this required scientifically informed land-use planning, protection of elephant corridors, appropriate community-based fencing, effective early-warning systems, and fair compensation for affected families.

"A person's name is vital to their sense of identity," psychoanalyst Mikita Brottman told DW. "When someone has an embarrassing name, that embarrassment can be incorporated into their identity."

Research on name bias has linked negatively perceived names with romantic rejection,as well as lower educational outcomes, lower self-esteem and more frequent smoking.

This suggests that names could influence our life trajectory, namely through self-esteem and opportunity.

But a person's relationship with their name is not necessarily fixed. Pain's lived experience, and that of another woman with the first name Tiny, shows how people can find ways to reclaim, and even come to value, names that once triggered ridicule and rejection.

Names can act as "signals" when you first meet someone, personality psychologist Jochen Gebauer told DW. Those signals may lose importance as people get to know each other, but Gebauer said their effects could also accumulate over a lifetime, potentially contributing to "real measurable advantages and disadvantages."

Research led by sociologist Anthony Lee found that a name can evoke inaccurate assumptions about intelligence, competence, trustworthiness, sociability and attractiveness.
For Pain, other people's reactions have extended beyond jokes. In high school, a teacher refused to believe that 'Pain' was her real surname and reprimanded her in front of the class.

"Your name couldn't possibly be Pain. Write your real name!" she recalled the teacher saying.

"Something that once feels like an inconvenience or a source of self-consciousness can become a marker of distinctiveness, history, or belonging," Daum said.

## Airbnb scraps guest checkout chores such as cleaning

Under new rules unveiled this week, the short-term rental platform says hosts should only make "reasonable" requests of guests.

Airbnb said hosts are generally not allowed to "ask guests to complete tasks that require significant effort or time, or would typically be performed by a cleaner, including stripping the bed, taking trash offsite, or washing and putting away dishes."

Checkout chores have been a contentious issue for guests, especially in cases where they are also charged cleaning fees.

Airbnb has previously admitted that such fees can create confusion between guests and hosts.

"Guests expect checkout to be relatively simple, and most want to avoid unexpected chores," Airbnb said in a message to hosts on the platform.

"Asking them to do things a cleaner should handle can negatively affect their stay and your reviews."

The new rules come amid growing protests against housing shortages in many European cities, with protesters arguing that short-term rentals such as Airbnb have pushed them out of their own cities.

In September, the European Commission proposed more restrictions on Airbnb and other short-term rental companies in tourist hubs.

Guests can still be asked to leave dirty dishes in the sink, to gather used towels and separate trash and recyclables, for example.

Hosts can also require guests to put trash in animal-proof containers areas where bears are active or ask them to "limit shower time due to local drought-related water restrictions."

But hosts will no longer be allowed to ban guests from using amenities listed in the property description, such as air conditioners, nor will they be allowed to fine guests.

Instead, hosts must seek compensation for any damage from insurance that the platform provides.
