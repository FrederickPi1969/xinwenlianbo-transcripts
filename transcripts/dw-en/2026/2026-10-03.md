## Greece returns remains of Czar Samuel to Bulgaria

https://p.dw.com/p/5NhPcA warrior emperor who ruled the Bulgarian Empire in the Middle Ages, Czar Samuel is a symbol of Bulgarian national identityImage: ArTo/Zoonar/picture allianceAdvertisementThe remains of one of Bulgaria's most important medieval rulers were set to arrive in the Bulgarian capital, Sofia, on Saturday after decades of negotiations with neighboring Greece.

Czar Samuel ruled the First Bulgarian Empire from 997 to 1014, gradually extending his authority over a swathe of the Balkans as far south as Lake Ohrid, now in North Macedonia on the border with Albania.

But Samuel's rule was also characterized by conflict with the Byzantine Empire which ultimately defeated his army at the Battle of Kleidion in 1014.

Samuel is said to have died of shock when thousands of his defeated soldiers returned home having been blinded by order of Byzantine Emperor Basil II, known as Boulgaroktonos – the "Bulgar Slayer."

The Bulgarian lands fell under Byzantine rule four years later but Czar Samuel is still considered a symbol of Bulgarian national identity today, and his remains – discovered in a Greek border area 60 years ago – are set to be received with full state honors.

They are set to be flown to Sofia aboard a military aircraft accompanied by a fighter jet escort before a 21-gun salute and a procession to the Church of St. Sophia, where the public will be able to pay respects.

Free trains to the capital are being provided for the occasion, with some 750 police on duty during the handover ceremony.

"These are items that belong to both our peoples; they constitute our memory and our bond, and our compatriots and future generations deserve and must have the opportunity to pay tribute to their ancestors," said Bulgarian Prime Minister Rumen Radev at a ceremony held at Museum of Byzantine Civilization in Thessaloniki, Greece, ahead of the handover.

Bulgaria officially opened talks on the return of the remains in 2007, and they became a major issue in relations with their southern neighbor.

"If, in the past, Czar Samuel divided us, I can say that today he unites us," said Radev. "He strengthens our strategic cooperation and makes the historical relations between our states and peoples even stronger."

Greek Prime Minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis, speaking alongside Radev, acknowledged that the remains of Czar Samuel have "incalculable cultural and spiritual but also emotional value" for Bulgaria, adding: "The global community is re-evaluating its long-standing stance on protecting cultural heritage."

In return, Greece is set to receive 48 sentimental objects pillaged by Bulgarian troops from churches and monasteries in northern Greece during the First World War.

Some prominent Greek academics and religious leaders have criticized the exchange as inadequate on the Bulgarian side, but Mitsotakis said he hoped Saturday's ceremony would bolster Greece's international campaign to return the Parthenon Marbles, a collection of 2,500-year-old marble sculptures taken from the Acropolis in Athens and later displayed in the British Museum.

"This shift is embodied by today's initiative, which rests on the obvious principle that monuments and historical heirlooms cannot be treated as spoils of war, nor as the result of circumstances subsequent to their creation."

However, Bulgaria is not the only country to claim Czar Samuel, who is also celebrated with a huge statue in Skopje, the capital of North Macedonia.

"The bones return, the conflict remains! Samuel once again [contested] between Skopje and Sofia!" declared the Albanian-language newspaper Koha, while Skopje daily Sloboden Pechat called the handover another "battle over history."

In 2022, a bilateral commission recommended that Samuel be presented as the head of a state that "most contemporary historiographers consider to be the Bulgarian Empire" – territory which is now North Macedonia.

"Samuel occupies an important place in the historical narrative both in Bulgaria and in North Macedonia," Dimitar Bechev, a researcher at the Carnegie Europe think tank, told the AFP news agency.

Indeed, history has been playing a major role in the run-up to this month's Bulgarian presidential election, with incumbent President Iliana Iotova, backed by Prime Minister Radev, promoting a more neutral position between the European Union and NATO – both of which Bulgaria belongs to – and Russia.

"Radev is looking to attract the patriotic electorate," said analyst Bechev, adding that the return of the remains to Sofia "aims to underline the Bulgarian character of the Slavic population of Macedonia."

## Spain: Floods leave 102-year-old woman dead in southeast

Madrigueras, which has a population of around 4,000 residents, is located near the city of Albacete in the Spanish autonomous community of Castilla-La Mancha.

Local Spanish media said the elderly woman was found dead in her home after the floodwaters had reached a depth of one meter (3.2 feet).

Spanish meteorological authorities said heavy rainfall is expected to continue in eastern Spain through Sunday.

The torrential rain and flooding comes after the Mediterranean country witnessed its hottest summer on record this year.

Deadly wildfires were reported in Spain's north and south in recent months due to frequent hot, dry conditions.

## Morocco&apos;s first female head of government: Symbol or real reform?

https://p.dw.com/p/5NfFoMansouri has been nicknamed the "pasha's daughter" because she comes from a prominent Marrakesh family and her father was formerly a pasha or municipal governorImage: Abdel Majid Bziouat/AFPAdvertisementAfter the announcement that Morocco would appoint its first female head of government – Marrakech mayor Fatima Zahra Mansouri – citizens interviewed by local television reporters were mostly enthusiastic.

"It would be great if we had a female prime minister for a change. She'd lift our spirits a bit," one woman said.

"Why don't we give women a chance? We've never had one [a female head of government] before," said another. "May God bless her and help her stay on a straight path."

"Regardless of who takes on the role, it should be a change for the better in Morocco," a man added.

But there were also naysayers. Some locals questioned how she would take part in official events where tradition and protocol dictate that only men attend.

"People are saying that a nation cannot succeed if a woman is in charge, even if she's only half in charge," one man told a television reporter, referring to the fact that Morocco's King Mohammed VI has the final say in national politics.

Morocco is a constitutional monarchy so elections are held but the king has ultimate executive power. The head of the country's government, or prime minister, leads the cabinet and administration and executes public policy.

The king chose Fatima Zahra Mansouri, minister of planning and mayor of Marrakesh, over Fouzi Lekjaa, head of Morocco's football federation and budget minister. The pair are senior members of the Authenticity and Modernity Party, or PAM, which won the most seats in the country's parliamentary elections in September.

"If you talk to Moroccans, there are some who are really proud of having a woman at the top and they see a chance for her to have impact on women's representation in society," confirms Anja Freudenreich, who heads the Morocco office of Germany's Friedrich Ebert Foundation (FES) in Rabat. "But others see her appointment as bare symbolism."

Mansouri will lead what is colloquially being called the "World Cup government" as Morocco prepares to invest billions before it hosts the 2030 football World Cup along with Spain and Portugal. Mansouri is seen to have shown leadership after the devastating 2023 Morocco earthquake.

There is no doubt that Mansouri's appointment has important symbolic value, observers say.

Two Tunisian women, Najla Bouden and current prime minister Sara Zaafarani, became heads of an Arab government before Mansouri's appointment. A 2022 surveyby polling organization Arab Barometer found Tunisian attitudes towards women in power had changed as a result.

Researchers from Arab Barometer believed the change — with only 40% agreeing that "in general, men are better at political leadership than women" in 2022 compared to 56% four years earlier — was the result of a "role model effect." Before the appointment of Bouden and Zaafarani, there had been no major changes, they pointed out.

According to a 2025 survey by the organization, attitudes toward women's roles have also been changing in Morocco. Mansouri could accelerate that.

"We've already noticed how impactful this [appointment] is in terms of social and cultural norms," said Stephanie Willman Bordat, co-founder of Rabat-based advocacy organization, Mobilizing for Rights Associates (MRA).

"People in public spaces, in cafes, on trains on their commute and just regular folks are having conversations about women's leadership and the taboo that says women can't be heads of government or women can't be bosses of men. There's good public debate and dialogue on this."

"And on a global level, in terms of women's participation, this is really something to encouraging," Bordat, who has lived in Morocco for two decades, told DW. "Especially when you look at the global climate of backlash and regression in women's rights."

Mansouri's prime ministership "is certainly notable as female leadership in the region is rare," Freudenreich of FES agreed. "It does have a highly symbolic character and I'm sure she does, or she will have, a role model function for women and girls in Morocco as well as in the region."

However, Mansouri is unlikely to initiate a major change from past politics, Freudenreich suggested.

She's more of a pragmatist than a rebel — a quality that's likely to see her navigate the much-discussed issues of protocol — and her party's electoral manifesto doesn't go into any major detail on women's issues, she pointed out.

Given popular discontent with the economy and high youth unemployment and protests, "improving women's status is a promise the government can deliver on in a challenging period," the organization noted.

The country is still waiting to see how Mansouri will form a governing coalition, she argued. How many female ministers the new government appoints, and what powers they are granted, will also be closely observed.

## Bosnia-Herzegovina shaped by war, separatism, EU ambitions

https://p.dw.com/p/5Nfi1Sarajevo, host of the 1984 Winter Olympics, was under siege for four years during the Bosnian war in the mid 1990sImage: Gabriele Thielmann/imageBROKER/picture allianceAdvertisementTo call Bosnia-Herzegovina a complicated country is an understatement. The country in the Balkan Peninsula divides its economic, administrative and political responsibilities between the multiethnic central government, the self-governing neutral Brcko district and two semi-autonomous entities that, for the most part, operate independently — the Muslim-Croat Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina and the Serb-run Republika Srpska.

Both semi-autonomous entities are further split into several cantons and communities, home to mostly Bosniaks and Bosnian Croats in the central-western federation and Bosnian Serbs in Republika Srpska, to the north and east.

This principle of ethnic nationalism is key to the country's central constitutional and political organization. The Bosnian constitution recognizes Bosniaks, who are predominantly Muslim, the largely Orthodox Bosnian Serbs and primarily Catholic Bosnian Croats as "constituent peoples" with equal rights.

Today, the country has a population of around 3.1 million people. Around half are Bosniaks, while 31% are Serb and 15% are Croat. The rest of the inhabitants belong either to one of the 17 officially recognized minorities, like the Roma and Jews, or chose not to identify with any ethnicity at the last official census. Bosnian, Croatian and Serbian are all recognized as official languages.

The central government is led by rotating three-way presidency, made up of one representative from each of the three main constituent groups. The upper house of parliament and key government offices are also divided according to this principle.

This strict balance between the three groups is always maintained, both politically and socially, often leading to ethnic interests and political gridlock dominating the decision-making process.

This complex, convoluted and oft dysfunctional structure was set up during the 1995 Dayton Peace Accords, and then anchored in the constitution. It was intended to bring an end to the Bosnian war — sparked by the breakup of Yugoslavia in the early 1990s — and legally secure the balance of power between the three feuding ethnic groups.

During the war, from 1992 to 1995, the armed forces of the Bosniaks and the Bosnian Croats initially fought each other, before all three groups began to clash. Later, the Bosniaks and the Bosnian Croats joined forces to take on Republika Srpska. Throughout the war, neighboring Serbia and Croatia were actively involved in the fighting.

The fighting devastated wide swaths of the country, and resulted in countless war crimes — among them the nearly four-year siege of Sarajevo and the Srebrenica genocide, in which thousands of Bosniaks were killed at the hands of Serb forces.

By the end of the war, some 100,000 people had been killed. A further 2.2 million were displaced by so-called "ethnic cleansing," forced from their homes by expulsions and violence to flee to other communities or abroad.

The fighting only ended after the international community, especially the United States, stepped up its involvement, culminating in the peace deal agreed at a military base near Dayton, Ohio in November 1995.

Although Bosnia-Herzegovina has been recognized on the international stage since the signing of the Dayton agreement, and is also represented at the United Nations, the country itself is still, in effect, only partially independent.

The Office of the High Representative for Bosnia and Herzegovina, an international institution responsible for overseeing the civilian aspects of the Dayton agreement, has been in place since a UN Security Council resolution in 1995.

The high representative — Christian Schmidt of Germany until this past June, and currently filled by acting head Louis J. Crishock of the US — has sweeping powers. He can dismiss elected officials, pass laws, establish new government agencies and overrule all the country's democratic institutions.

This authority is often referred to as the Bonn Powers, as they were negotiated during a meeting of the Peace Implementation Council in Bonn, Germany in December 1997. From 2002 to 2011, the high representative also acted as the EU special representative for Bosnia and Herzegovina.

In recent years, there's been much discussion over whether the office is still relevant. Supporters have said the high representative remains a necessary guarantor of the country's unity and functionality. But critics have pointed out the negative impact on Bosnia-Herzegovina's democratic culture, turning the country into a de facto protectorate that, to an extent, frees local politicians of responsibility for their actions.

The sharpest criticism has come from the Republika Srpska and Milorad Dodik, its most influential leader. Dodik, in office as prime minister and president of the Serb entity until 2025, views the office and the international backing it provides for a unified Bosnia-Herzegovina as an obstacle to his separatist ambitions.

With strong support from the Serbian capital, Belgrade, Moscow — and, increasingly, the Trump administration — Dodik has flirted with several different scenarios to split his Republika Srpska from Bosnia-Herzegovina. In his interpretation, and that of Republika Srpska's political elite, Bosnia-Herzegovina is highly centralized and unitary, which undermines the independence and sovereignty of the Bosnian Serbs.

A similar sentiment prevails among the political representatives of Bosnia's Croats. Although they aren't considering separation, they are trying to squeeze more independence from the central government in Sarajevo — not least with the creation of a third, Croatian, entity. The plan, incidentally, also has the support of the government in neighboring EU member Croatia.

A potential path out of the current economic and political paralysis is rapprochement with the European Union — the "European perspective," as it's called in Bosnia-Herzegovina, officially supported by all the relevant political actors.

Bosnia-Herzegovina formally submitted its application for EU membership in 2016, and the European Council gave the green light for negotiations to begin in early 2024. But the actual start of membership talks hinges on the prior implementation of numerous reforms, notably in the areas of rule of law, fighting corruption, the prosecution of organized crime and the functionality of state institutions.
