## Harry Kane: Ballon d’Or for Bayern Munich striker?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OVffHarry Kane is already Bayern Munich's seventh top Bundesliga scorerImage: Carina Johansen/NTB/REUTERSAdvertisementTo most Bayern Munich fans under the age of 40, Karl-Heinz Rummenigge was the club CEO who regularly spoke his mind. Fans with longer memories, or a better grasp of Bavarian football history, recall Rummenigge as one of the club's all-time great players. He was also the last Bayern man to win the Ballon d'Or — in 1981. The last Bundesliga player to win was Borussia Dortmund's Matthias Sammer, in 1996.

That long drought looks set to end on Monday, with Harry Kane, born 12 years after Rummenigge's win, the favorite to pick up the trophy at a ceremony held, as a one-off, in his native London.

"It's obviously special for me that the ceremony is taking place in London. "I come from London and lived there for a long time — maybe that's a good omen," Kane told club magazine Säbener51. "The Ballon d'Or would be a massive success for me."

Lamine Yamal, the prodigious Barcelona and Spain forward who won the Euros at 16 looks to be the main competitor. At the end of the 2025-26 season, on which the award is judged, Yamal won the World Cup with Spain, as well as the La Liga title.

Kane's 73 goals for club and country last season make for a strong case, and the Bundesliga's most expensive signing of all time has already racked up 156 goals in 158 games for the serial German champions. But it's not just the numbers that tell the story, according to his club coach.

"People in England saw him as a goalscorer during his time playing there," Vincent Kompany said of Kane last season. "We've seen other qualities too, such as his footballing intelligence. We can appreciate that now — it was perhaps somewhat underestimated in England. He's like a fine wine that improves with age. I also enjoy that aspect of his game."

Kane continues to improve at 33, a point when many footballers start to regress. Though he is helped by being more reliant on guile and technique than pace, Kane's professionalism is key to staying at the top, according to his Bayern teammate Serge Gnabry.

"He always has the right attitude, always leads the way and sets the tone," Gnabry said ahead of Bayern's 2-2 draw in the Champions League against Arsenal on Wednesday. "He never strays from his path, helps the team with his performance and is a top-tier player."

Kane scored a penalty in that match and provided a sensational pass from which Alphonso Davies scored Bayern's opener, another reminder of his all-round game. "The pass was one of my best ones," Kane said after the match. "I knew Phonzy [Davies] would be able to get there with his speed. Whatever I can do to help the team, I'll do. At the moment, it seems to be with assists more than goals."

That attitude has been with Kane from the start. His first-ever coach, Dave Bricknell, from Ridgeway Rovers, an amateur club in northeast London, told DW after he joined Bayern from Spurs in 2023: "Harry's mentality is his greatest strength. He doesn't let anything worry him. If he misses a chance, he doesn't care — he will go and score from another chance."

His England coach, Thomas Tuchel, has also emphasized Kane's leadership. "He is the first one to push everyone, and that is his mindset, and he became a very, very hardworking forward on top of his finishing skills, Tuchel said in September as Kane extended his England goal-scoring record against the Czech Republic. "He is a complete package."

There are detractors who point to perceived "stat-padding," or scoring against inferior opponents in international qualifiers and in the Bundesliga, where there are fewer elite teams, and a lot less money, than in England. Though Kane's ratio of 213 goals in 320 games in the Premier League is a little off his Bundesliga stats, it bears comparison with any other player in the history of the league.

Should Kane win the award, it will be a boost in profile for the Bundesliga, which has been unable to keep pace, both commercially and on the pitch, with other top European leagues of late, particularly the Premier League and La Liga. Winning it in a league outside of England or Spain would make Kane's feat even greater. Other than Ousmane Dembele, who won it last year after Paris Saint-Germain's Champions League win, and Lionel Messi, a serial Ballon d'Or winner, the last player to take the Ballon d'Or for a season played outside England or Spain was was Kaka at AC Milan in 2007, when Italy's Serie A was considered a stronger league.

Kane's career hasn't been straightforward, though. After signing his first senior contract with Spurs in July 2010, he was sent out on various loans to lower league sides between 2011-2013 without ever making a huge impression.

For former teammate Jermain Defoe, who also coached Kane at Spurs, those experiences were formative.

"You have to show a resilience. It's not easy when you go out on loan, it can feel like you're being rejected by the club that you are at, which isn't the case," he told DW in 2023, before adding that Kane's ability was always clear.

"When he joined up with the [Tottenham] first team, you could see the potential," Defoe said. "He was always a good finisher, always hit the ball clean. … With Harry you already knew, he had that natural instinct and always felt comfortable in front of goal."

Those goals have, after a failure to win a trophy in England, led to two Bundesliga titles and a German Cup. But success with England remains elusive after a 2026 World Cup exit in the semifinals. Kane will be 35 by the time of the next Euros, in the UK and Ireland in 2028, and has a deal with Bayern until the following year. His form since joining Bayern suggests that he could get better still.

## Israel to close all West Bank land crossings on election day

https://p.dw.com/p/5Odv8Evacuated Palestinian homes were draped with Israeli flags during the Israeli operation in HebronImage: Mahmoud Illean/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementThe Israeli military said on Sunday that all land crossings between Israel and the West Bank will be closed during Tuesday's parliamentary election.

The Israel Defense Forces (IDF) said that, from Sunday, Palestinians would "not be permitted to enter Israeli communities in Judea and Samaria and the Jordan Valley for work" — using terms used by many in Israel to refer to the occupied territories in the West Bank.

The army added that it would enforce "a ban on the entry and exit of Palestinian workers … as well as the closure of all land crossings throughout Election Day" on October 27.

The announcement came amid an ongoing Israeli military operation in the West Bank following a deadly attack by Palestinian gunmen near the city of Hebron on Friday.

Two Israeli soldiers were killed and three others injured in the attack, according to the IDF, which said the two suspected attackers were also killed.

The Palestinian militant group Hamas hailed the attack but did not claim credit for carrying it out.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has vowed to exact a heavy price for the attack which came just days before the poll in which he is facing a tough battle to secure a new term.

The Israeli army said it had been operating in 40 areas throughout the West Bank on Friday and Saturday, with locals reporting that soldiers had announced evacuations over loudspeakers late at night.

Israeli forces said they had "apprehended approximately 40 suspects, including terrorists affiliated with the Hamas and Islamic Jihad terrorist organizations."

The mayor of Hebron, Yousef al-Jabari, told broadcaster Al Jazeera that some 2,200 people had been forced from their homes since Israeli raids began on Friday.

Israeli Defense Minister Israel Katz confirmed that figure, telling Israeli broadcaster Kan that "selected army units are currently operating there" and that Palestinians "will not be allowed to return … until we've finished dealing with all the Hamas issues in Hebron."

Israel's far-right finance minister, Bezalel Smotrich, who also holds a Defense Ministry position overseeing civilian affairs in the West Bank, said he personally ordered "the immediate demolition of a row of illegal structures" in the territory following Friday's shooting.

"Anyone who messes with us will quickly understand that he will pay a painful price," he wrote on social media.

In the Hebron neighborhood of Ein Deir Baha, vacated buildings were marked by X's and the streets were deserted, except for a handful of Israeli military vehicles, reported the AFP news agency, which added that Israeli flags had been draped on a house apparently taken over by troops.

Higazi Shawer al-Tamimi, 70, a shopkeeper with a senior role in the neighborhood, told AFP he was awakened at 1 a.m. by soldiers who said he had to leave and gave him only a short time to pack.

He added that, for other residents, leaving the neighborhood was much more difficult. "My wife's mother, who is 90 years old and lives in the area, had to be carried out because she is ill," he said.

In Al-Tamimi's opinion, the West Bank operation is taking place because Netanyahu wants to win the election with "the blood of the Palestinian people."

Netanyahu is Israel's longest-serving prime minister but his security-first reputation was shattered by the deadly attack by Hamas fighters on October 7, 2023, in which more than 1,200 Israelis were killed. The 80-year-old was previously head of state from 2003 to 2010, and became president again in 2023.

Flavio Bolsonaro has sat in the Senate since 2019 and is running for the presidency for the first time.

The rematch between Lula and Bolsonaro comes against the backdrop of corruption scandals, economic downslide and accusations of US interference, which Washington denies.

## Prague honours Roma resistance fighters against Hitler

https://p.dw.com/p/5OW7LSince the end of WWII, the role of Roma resistance fighters was largely unknownImage: Luboš Palata/DWAdvertisementFor more than half a million Roma in Czechia and Slovakia, October 15, 2026, marks an important milestone.

That day, an exhibition on a largely forgotten chapter of Czech history opened at the Museum of the Romani National Revival in Prague. The exhibit explores the role of Czech and Slovak Romani resistance fighters during the Nazi German occupation and the dismantling of Czechoslovakia from 1938 to 1945.

Until now, Czech and Slovak Roma appeared in the history of World War II almost exclusively as victims of the Holocaust against Roma and Sinti. In the so-called Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia, Nazi Germany murdered approximately 90% of the Czech Roma during World War II.

However, what is less well known is that a large number of Czech and Slovak Roma fought against the German occupiers as partisans in resistance groups and as soldiers in the Czechoslovak Underground Army, or Defense of the Nation, ON.

The exhibition at the Museum of the Roma National Movement therefore marks a turning point in how the Roma are viewed during World War II. At least that is the hope of museum director Emil Scuka, a lawyer, former prosecutor and one of the best-known Czech Roma activists.

"We were not only victims; we were also heroes," Scuka said in his speech to several hundred attendees at the exhibition opening, most of whom were Roma from Czechia and Slovakia. "Many people, not even among the Roma themselves, are aware that we Roma fought against Nazism," he noted. In his view, "organizing and opening the exhibition was something my generation owed to these Roma heroes and today we are repaying that debt."

Scuka called on those present: "Bring your children and grandchildren here so they can see for themselves and realize that the Roma have heroes too!"

He also pointed out that this topic had been completely marginalized in Czech and Slovak historical writing for decades and that the Roma had therefore decided on their own to document the fates of their war heroes.

The exhibition presents the detailed life stories of 100 of the most significant Roma partisans and Roma soldiers from the Czech underground army and resistance groups who fought against the Nazis.

"We found over 1,400 Romani fighters in the archives. From among them, we selected the 100 most interesting stories for this exhibition," said exhibition curator Radoslav Scuka at the opening.

Among these figures is Anton Facuna, a sergeant in the American intelligence agency Office of Strategic Services (OSS), who was flown to Slovakia in October 1944 on a secret mission. Slovakia had been a puppet state of Hitler from 1939 to 1945.

Another is Josef Serinek, known by the nickname "Black Partisan from Vysocina," who managed to escape from the Nazi concentration camp for Roma in Lety and subsequently founded and commanded the large Capajev-Cerny partisan unit in the Bohemian-Moravian Highlands.

In 2022, Czech President Milos Zeman posthumously awarded him the Medal for Heroism. Toward the end of his life, Serinek shared his memories with historian Jan Tesar, who later wrote the three-volume book "Ceska cikanska rapsodie", which translates to Czech Gypsy Rhapsody.

Except for Tesar's book, the history of Roma resistance against Nazism has not been researched by Czech and Slovak historians to this day. "I am not aware of any scholarly historical work that deals with Roma resistance against Nazism," Prokop Tomek, a historian at the Military History Institute in Prague, told DW.

"The number of 1,400 Roma who took part in the fight against Nazism as members of the resistance or in the foreign army does not strike me as an exaggeration and corresponds to the proportion of the Roma population in Czechoslovakia," Tomek said, adding that the "exhibition on the Roma resistance against Nazism during the war is undoubtedly groundbreaking."

To this day, many Roma in Czechia and Slovakia do not publicly acknowledge their ethnic identity. This is partly due to experiences with the Holocaust during the Nazi era that have been passed down through generations. Czech historians and politicians continue to debate the extent to which the Czech authorities of the Protectorate were complicit in the extermination of the Czech Roma.

However, one thing is certain: the police registration of all Roma, which had been enshrined in law in Czechoslovakia since 1927, facilitated the extermination of the Roma by the German Nazis.

According to estimates by the Czech government, approximately 250,000 Roma live in the country today. In the 2021 census, however, only 21,000 people identified as Roma.

Of all ethnic groups in Czechia, which has a population of 10 million, the Roma are among the three largest ethnic minorities in terms of numbers. However, they are the most socially disadvantaged, have very little political representation, and are therefore scarcely present in Czech politics.

## Saudi Arabia looks to US for an AI edge as Yemen&apos;s Houthis up pressure

https://p.dw.com/p/5OYkcAlmost 200 people, mostly children, were killed at a school when the US military used AI-assisted targeting in Iran in late FebruaryImage: Atta Kenare/AFPAdvertisementUnder attack from the militant Houthi group in neighboring Yemen, Saudi Arabia has asked numerous allies for help.

That includes the United States, even though media report that US President Donald Trump refused when Saudi ruler Mohammed bin Salman asked him to take direct military action against the Houthis.

However the US is providing Saudi Arabia with intelligence, which includes a targeting tool that uses artificial intelligence, or AI. A September report by US broadcaster CNN, quoting several unnamed American officials, said that between 100 and 200 US soldiers were in Saudi Arabia as part of new joint force command to facilitate this.

The US personnel were helping the Saudis "use a targeting tool that allows military officers to see in real-time what is happening on the battlefield," CNN reported.

Officials said the system in Saudi Arabia was similar to that known as the Maven Smart System, or MSS for short, which the US military has also used as it sought targets in Iran, Iraq and Syria.

MSS came out of what's known as Project Maven, the US's flagship artificial intelligence program for the military, first launched in 2017. MSS itself is an AI-enabled digital platform that "aggregates, organizes and visualizes vast streams of intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance data," analysts at the Center for Strategic and International Studies in Washington explained in a June text.

The software system identifies patterns in data coming from, for example, live satellite feeds or drone cameras and then labels potential targets such as possible rocket launchers, tanks or suspicious vehicle convoys.

Previously human military analysts would have had to look at a wide array of different sources to try to find those, the CSIS researchers wrote. It was difficult and, in the case of hours of satellite or drone footage, almost impossible. But MSS now brings hundreds of sources together, analyses them all, then flags possible targets. It also calculates the best method of hitting the target.

Tech commentator Bilawal Sidhu, a former Google product manager, has described the MSS set up as like a "Google Earth for war."

Using the MSS dramatically speeds up what is known as "the kill chain" — that is, the time it takes to detect a target, decide if it's dangerous and then order a strike.

When the US military used MSS while attacking Iran in late February, the AI-powered system facilitated hits on more than 1,000 targets within the first 24 hours of the war. Previously American soldiers were hitting about 100 targets a day.

Up until recently, the long-simmering civil war in Yemen had been comparatively calm following an informal ceasefire negotiated in 2022.

The Houthi rebel group has held large parts of northern and western Yemen since 2015. Yemen's internationally recognized government and other groups held territory across the south and east. That latter is supported by Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates.

The Houthi's latest military campaign, which began in September, has seen the group take further control of Yemen's coast. They also ramped up attacks against Saudi Arabia again, hitting the country's main airport and oil facilities. So far, though, military campaigns against the Houthi group by Saudi Arabia, the United States and others have all failed to significantly weaken the group.

This is partially because the Houthis take a "zero-sum approach," Farea al-Muslimi, a research fellow focused on Yemen at UK think tank Chatham House, told DW recently. The group's founding father argued that contemporary Muslim societies were too "soft," and that poverty and hardship are a divine test, al-Muslimi said.

"They don't care if the Israelis bomb the airport or the US bombs a hospital," she said. "It actually serves them very well because their ideology is all about martyrdom and sacrifice."

There are certain technical issues users have noted with the MSS in the past that could also prove problematic in Yemen.

The Houthis tend to be highly mobile, using truck-mounted ballistic missiles and small drone swarms as they operate from hills and canyons in rugged countryside. It's possible that fighters might be long gone before they can be attacked  even with the help of AI-assisted targeting.

Though MSS is known to work best in relatively unpopulated areas, factors such as changes in desert terrain, dust storms, high wind, snowfall and dense foliage — and even deliberately planted decoys — can reduce its success rate.

There is also a worrying ethical aspect to shortening the kill chain. A human is always supposed to verify the target that the AI-assisted tool has identified and decide how to react.

But it doesn't always work that way. For example, among the sites MSS targeted in Iran on the first day of the war was a school. An American missile killed almost 200 people at a primary school in southern Iran, most of them children aged between seven and 12.

In its military campaign in Gaza, Israel — arguably the Middle Eastern country with the most advanced military-use AI — has also used AI-assisted targeting. An Israeli system known as the Gospel, or Habsora, is the closest to MSS in the way it works.

But, as has been documented elsewhere, including by Israeli and British journalists and in the recent documentary Naza, the use of such AI targeting tools have also been highly problematic because of the numbers of civilian casualties that can result.

"On the one hand [in Gaza], AI has enhanced commanders' situational awareness, lessened the human costs of tunnel mapping, improved the speed of military strikes, and boosted the survivability of troops," analysts Frederic Wehrey and Andrew Bonney wrote for War on the Rocks, a specialist media outlet. "Yet the benefits afforded by such functions have been overshadowed by mounting civilian deaths reportedly resulting from this technology's safeguards being loosened or eliminated."

All of which is why Antony Loewenstein, an independent journalist based in Australia who has researched the impact of Israel's AI-assisted technology, doesn't believe similar technology will lead to the defeat of the Houthi group.

"I don't think there's much evidence at all that using an AI tool is going to make much difference on the ground, except for causing huge amounts of civilian casualties," Loewenstein, who authored the book, "The Palestine Laboratory: How Israel Exports The Technology of Occupation Around the World," told DW. "And I think what the US and the Saudis will find is that the Houthis have an incredibly high tolerance level for destruction."
