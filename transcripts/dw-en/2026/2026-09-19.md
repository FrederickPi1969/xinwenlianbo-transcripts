## Summer of 2026 broke records in Europe, and caused deaths

https://p.dw.com/p/5MtrSOlder people are more vulnerable to extreme heat Image: Joel Saget/AFPAdvertisementThe summer (June to August) of 2026 was the warmest in Europe since records began, beating the record set in 2003, according to the Copernicus Climate Change Service,

Not only that, Germany's DWD weather service says that there was an "unprecedented" number of hot days this year. "On average across Germany,there were about 22 hot days with temperatures of at least 30 degrees Celsius and about five very hot days with temperatures of at least 35 degrees Celsius," the DWD wrote in in a press release at the end of August.

The estimated number of heat-related deaths also reached unprecedented levels, with at least 16,300 people dying by the end of August, even if few of them died directly because of the heat, but rather as a consequence of underlying health conditions exacerbated by the heat.

The figures were compiled from a statistical correlation between the number of deaths recorded by Germany's Federal Statistical Office and the temperatures measured by the DWD during a specific period.

Heat-related deaths in Germany are more likely if the average weekly temperature reaches 20 degrees Celsius or more. This is the average of all hourly temperatures recorded in a week, including at nighttime.

This year, the average weekly temperatures in the country rose above the 20-degree mark several times. It was particularly hot at the end of June, when the average weekly temperature reached above 26 degrees between the 22nd and 28th of the month.

Though it is not possible to determine a cause of death using statistics, researchers and medical professionals have a good understanding of how heat affects the human body, and there is widespread consensus that excess mortality during heat waves is possible.

"It was not just the high temperatures on the thermometer that were important, but also how long the heat lasted and the fact that it hardly cooled down at night," said Andreas Matzarakis, professor of environmental meteorology at Freiburg University.

Many people have trouble sleeping on hot nights. Yet, getting enough sleep is important so that the body can recover from the heat and stress of the day.

Matzarakis said that the severity of heat stress was not related solely to air temperatures. Humidity, solar radiation and wind also played a role — and what is known as the 'felt' temperature. He said that humidity and still air could place a heavy strain on the body, even in moderate temperatures.

The less privileged members of society were also among the most vulnerable, said Martin Röösli, environmental scientist at the Swiss Tropical and Public Health Institute in Basel. "They are more likely to live in homes that are not well insulated or are more likely to work outdoors."

A large proportion of those who died because of the heat probably had only a short life expectancy left, due to their age and medical conditions, but Röösli said that there was a consensus that "some of the deceased could have lived for a long time and were simply in a fragile state at the time of the heat wave."

For Alexandra Schneider, a meteorologist and epidemiologist at the Helmholtz center in Munich in southern Germany, the deaths that occurred during the heat waves were very likely not only because of the "harvesting effect," which describes "how a heat wave causes very vulnerable individuals in particular to die a few days or weeks prematurely."

Had this been the case, there would have been a deficit of mortality in the following weeks, which did not happen. Thus, she believes, a significant proportion of those who died could have lived on for months or even years.

## German-Russian relations at freezing point

https://p.dw.com/p/5MrY5Chancellor Friedrich Merz (left) has described Russian President Vladimir Putin as "perhaps the worst war criminal of our time"Image: picture allianceAdvertisementFor many years, Germany and Russia had a perfect partnership.

Russia supplied cheap energy, which helped German industry succeed in exporting its goods worldwide. This partnership began in the early 2000s. On the German side was Chancellor Gerhard Schröder from the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD), followed later by Chancellor Angela Merkel of the conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU). Their counterpart was Vladimir Putin.

Even when Russia annexed the Ukrainian peninsula of Crimea in 2014, business continued as usual. It was then that the agreement to build Nord Stream 2, a direct gas pipeline between Russia and Germany designed to bypass Ukraine was signed. At the time, other European countries and the US warned that the pipeline would provide Russia with powerful leverage.

The warnings proved to be correct. On February 24, 2022, Russian tanks rolled into Ukraine. German hopes for a stable European order, the democratization of Russia, and the continuation of its lucrative business model were shattered.

"Shortly before the invasion, the widespread view was that there were no rational grounds for an attack on Ukraine, because the economic, military, and political costs to Russia of starting a war would be far too high," Russia expert Mario Baumann of the German Council on Foreign Relations told DW. "People [in Germany] had projected their own rationality onto Russia. They underestimated the regime's imperialist and revisionist ambitions."

After that, nothing in the bilateral relationship was ever the same again. Chancellor Olaf Scholz(SPD) spoke of a Zeitenwende (epochal shift) in foreign and security policy, in the days after the 2022 invasion of Ukraine began.

Since then, the German armed forces have expanded. Defense Minister Boris Pistorius said the following year, "We must become capable of waging war," a statement that critics still say was an escalation of tensions.

Germany ended its decades-long energy dependence on Russia and shut down Nord Stream 2. In September 2022, the pipeline was blown up. The Federal Prosecutor's Office suspects a Ukrainian group was responsible.

A series of EU sanctions have long since reduced Russian-German trade relations to a minimum. Government cooperation in culture, science, and education has also practically ceased to exist.

While the German government under Scholz initially hesitated to take dramatic steps on military aid to Ukraine, Friedrich Merz, then the leader of the opposition, stepped up his rhetoric.

"Ukraine must win the war. Russia must lose the war," he said in 2024 as leader of the CDU/CSU parliamentary group in parliament. In 2025, as chancellor, he then spoke of Russia's "economic exhaustion," which the West must "help bring about." But that has yet to materialize. To this day, Russia has shown no serious willingness to compromise.

Meanwhile, Germany is feeling the consequences of its military support for Ukraine. The German government has long suspected Russia of being behind cyberattacks on government agencies and critical infrastructure.

So when an armed drone was discovered and defused in August 2026 at Leipzig Airport close to Ukrainian transport aircraft — which transport a large portion of the military aid to Ukraine — the government was certain: For the first time, it publicly blamed the Kremlin.

A Russian cultural center in Berlin and the Russian consulate in Bonn were closed, which the German government said were being used as espionage centers. Moscow responded by closing the Goethe Institutes and the German consulate in St. Petersburg.

Putin has denied Russian responsibility for the Leipzig incident, calling it a "serious mistake" to blame Moscow, while Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov even described the accusations as the "beginning of a real war." But Baumann says: "We shouldn't give in to the fears being stoked, because they are part of Russia's strategy to weaken Germany and Europe."

The German government is also facing domestic pressure as the far-right Alternative for Germany(AfD) has made gains in regional elections and is rising in the national polls. The AfD would prefer to end support for Ukraine and normalize relations with Russia, including deals on oil and gas. High fuel prices in Germany have only made the issue more explosive.

"German support for Ukraine worries many people," said Sven Leunig, political scientist at the University of Jena in Thuringia, following the election in the neighboring state of Saxony-Anhalt, where the AfD won the September 6 election by a landslide. However, he noted, "it is less a fundamental rejection of the support itself than a reaction born of fear. People want to 'stay out' of the conflict so as not to be drawn into it more deeply themselves."

In the latest ARD Deutschlandtrend survey, 42% of respondents believe military aid to Ukraine has gone too far. "If public support weakens over the long term, the government will eventually have to respond," says Leunig.

So far, the German government is sticking to its course, as Merz made clear once again following the AfD's election victory. But opinions on how to deal with Russia and the war in Ukraine differ not only among politicians but also among political scientists and experts.

Johannes Varwick of Halle University in Saxony-Anhalt believes it is long past time for a change of course: "German and European policy toward Ukraine resembles a mix of platitudes and wishful thinking," Varwick told DW. "It is high time for a course correction, and Germany should exert pressure on Ukraine to move closer to the latest American proposals, which aim for a compromise and could thus possibly bring about a political solution."

Baumann, on the other hand, argues: "Supporting Ukraine remains the best investment for safeguarding German and European interests. The government has recognized this and should stick to this course and continue to communicate it as such." However, it is important that Berlin act in concert with its European partners.

The war in Ukraine has now lasted longer than World War I. And there is no end in sight. Hopes that Russian-German relations will return to the way they used to be seem illusory for the foreseeable future.

## Ukraine steps up measures to protect railways

https://p.dw.com/p/5MtihUkrainian trains are increasingly the target of Russian drones Image: Ukrainian State Emergency Service/AFPAdvertisementRecent months have seen a rise in Russian drone attacks on locomotives, stations, and infrastructure. More than 1,600 attacks were reported in the first eight months of 2026, according to Oleh Holovashchenko from Ukrzaliznytsia, the state rail operator.

On September 16, a train traveling between the capital Kyiv and Mykolaiv in southern Ukraine was struck by a Russian drone. The day before, another train in the Volhynia region was attacked near the Polish border.

Two days earlier, a drone had struck the locomotive of a passenger train at the Yahodyn border crossing, just two kilometers (1.2 miles) from Polish territory. Nobody was injured, as Ukrzaliznytsia's monitoring system had previously detected the drone and the train had been evacuated about 15 minutes before the attack. At the time, CIA Director David Petraeus was on another train at the same station.

A separate train carrying several former European leaders, including ex-British Prime Minister Boris Johnson and security advisers had stopped at the station earlier the same day. The Ukrainian authorities believe that this diplomatic train was the drone's actual target, but the train departed earlier than scheduled.

Russia has damaged or destroyed more than 500 Ukrainian locomotives since its full-scale invasion of Ukrainebegan in 2022. More than half of these attacks happened this year, according to a post on the social media platform X by Ukrainian Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha. He said that part of "Putin's terror" was to destabilize Ukraine's logistics system.

The minister called on international partners to tighten sanctions against Russia further and to provide financial assistance to the Ukrainian government so that it can procure more locomotives that are compatible with Ukraine's Soviet-era broad-gauge (1,520 millimeters or almost 5 feet).

"Ukraine's railway keeps the country moving," Sybiha said. "It enables the rapid evacuation of civilians from frontline areas, transports essential cargo, and keeps grain and other critical goods moving across the country."

Russia was specifically targeting locomotives because they were ideal from a technical point of view, said Vasyl Shevchenko, a former Ukrainian deputy transport minister. "A locomotive's thermal signature is very large, and almost impossible to miss," he told DW. "The impact of a strike can be devastating, because 50 kilograms (110 pounds) of explosives in a diesel locomotive can cause several metric tons of fuel to detonate and completely destroy it. A strike on an electric locomotive will cause a fire that can paralyze an entire stretch of track. That's why they are targeting areas where the loss of a single train can bring traffic to a standstill."

Shevchenko also pointed out that locomotives could not be replaced immediately but had to be ordered in advance and this could take years. Ukrzaliznytsia says that negotiations are underway with various international partners and a contract has already been signed with the French company Alstom. These locomotives are scheduled to start arriving next year.

In the meantime, trains are also being repaired. "While our top priorities used to be saving on costs, providing comfort to the train crew and making sure the trains were attractive, these have changed," Holovashchenko said. "What is most important today is that the locomotive is technically sound and ready to transport passengers and freight." But he added that it was difficult to protect trains against Russia's weapons.

On September 15, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy held a meeting of the staff of the Supreme Commander-in-Chief to discuss strengthening the protection of critical infrastructure and logistics.

The key issues were contracts for the production of protection systems for critical infrastructure and logistics, but he added that there were plans to expand the electronic warfare component and reorganize the operation of designated mobile fire teams, as well as strengthening air defense lines.

Zelenskyy said that decisions on backup logistics routes in frontline regions were also being made, and he had instructed Ukraine's Finance Ministry of to find additional funding for the logistics sector.

According to Holovashchenko, Ukrzaliznytsia is also seeking its own answers to the new challenges and there are plans to develop early detection systems. He said that a monitoring center had been established to track the movement of drones and trains all over Ukraine.

The fact that Russia has started to use jet-powered Shahed-type drones, which are much faster than previous models, has made it more difficult and given less time to respond. "We used to wait longer to stop trains so we could evacuate passengers," Holovashchenko said. "Now, there are more precautionary stops. But in general, we try not to go into areas where there is a potential danger."

Buses are deployed in areas of particularly high risk and the "train plus bus" concept is being expanded in cooperation with military authorities. Passengers are also being given instructions on how to respond in the event of an alarm and advised to try to get further away from a station than previously.

The most difficult question remains where they should be evacuated to. Holovashchenko said that the monitoring centers had access to maps showing all the shelters along train routes and that dispatchers always tried to stop trains near them, but this was not always possible. He said that train personnel were receiving special psychological support to help passengers.

Ukrzaliznytsia is currently preparing for winter, while working with foundations and other donors to ensure that trains will be heated. Evacuations will be even more difficult as temperatures drop. "This is no easy task. We're providing blankets, rain jackets, and equipment so that train staff can supply passengers with at least the bare necessities if their train comes to a standstill in the middle of nowhere because of a snowstorm, Holovashchenko said.

## Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania: Will economy shape the vote?

https://p.dw.com/p/5MjGaMecklenburg-Western Pomerania is Germany's second-most popular vacation destinationImage: Jens Büttner/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementThe recent election victory of the far-right AfD party in Saxony-Anhalt has shaken up Germany's political landscape. Although Saxony-Anhalt is only a small state, the AfD's landslide win with nearly 44% of the vote has drawn considerable international attention, as it means this would the first time that a far-right party will likely enter a German government since World War II.

That is not expected to happen in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, where voters head to the ballot box on Sunday. State Premier Manuela Schwesig of the center-left Social Democrats remains popular, as polls show, though she isn't without her critics.

The latest polls suggest a neck-and-neck race between the Social Democrats and AfD, rather than a landslide victory for any one party. In fact, the Social Democrats' approval ratings have been on the rise and could indicate a successful final push, potentially returning Schwesig to power.

Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, like Saxony-Anhalt, is in eastern Germany, but its economy is relatively stable, even though unemployment stands at 7.8%, slightly above the national average, and the median gross annual salary is €47,750 ($54,800) — about €6,000 less than the German average. But Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania has seen solid economic growth in recent years.

Jochen Schulte, state secretary in the state's Economy Ministry, said Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania's economy has expanded by 5% in inflation-adjusted terms since 2020, compared with 4.6% for Germany overall.

Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania has a large agricultural sector, although it employs relatively few people. Back when the state was part of East Germany, farms were consolidated into large, state-run enterprises known as agricultural production cooperatives, rather than privately organized as in West Germany.

In many instances, these farm sizes have remained the same since reunification. A typical agricultural company in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania will control about 280 to 283 hectares (up to 700 acres) of land, whereas the national average is only about 65 hectares.

The state's now private agricultural companies continue to benefit from their large size, ensuring efficient labor use, strong market positioning, greater market power through centralized purchasing, and a strong negotiating position when selling produce.

The state's second major industry is shipbuilding and maritime services, which accounts for a significant share of economic output. Being on Germany's Baltic Sea coast, Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania has also benefited from the recent investment boom in its defense sector. This has brought business — and jobs — to major shipyards in Wismar and Stralsund, at companies like Thyssenkrupp Marine Systems and other shipbuilders.

Tourism is another key sector in the state. Until 1990, Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania was one of the most important travel destinations for East Germans. Since the fall of the Berlin Wall, Germans from east and west have continued to flock to the Baltic coast, and the region is Germany's second-most popular vacation destination.

That said, relatively few people are directly employed in the tourism sector. That is in part merely a statistical quirk, as many tourism jobs overlapping with service jobs, and also because many tourism jobs are seasonal.

According to figures from the Economy Ministry and industry reports, some 150,000 to 160,000 jobs depend on tourism, either directly or indirectly. Water-based tourism, whether on lakes or the Baltic Sea coast, accounts for an estimated 19,000 jobs.

Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania has few large enterprises, such as automakers and chemical companies, which makes its state and civil service branch the region's largest employer.

Rostock-based AIDA Cruises is another major employer. So is Nordex SE, a manufacturer of wind turbines with production facilities in various regions. German rail operator Deutsche Bahn provides many jobs through its service and maintenance hubs, as do the major ports of Rostock and Sassnitz.

Another key industry is the energy sector, with large quantities of Germany's fossil fuels brought in via the Baltic Sea.

When imports of Russian gas came to a standstill in 2022 following the full-scale invasion of Ukraine, the Nord Stream gas pipeline connection points near Lubmin lost their significance. However, a large portion of Germany's liquefied natural gas imports are still unloaded, processed and distributed in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania.

Energy availability, not only from fossil fuels such as oil and gas, but also from green sources such as wind and solar power, is becoming increasingly important for the state as well.

In August, the Schwarz Group — which operates the Lidl and Kaufland supermarket chains — announced it would invest €5.6 billion ($6.4 billion) in the construction of a data center south of Rostock.

During the project's presentation in the state capital, Schwerin, the company said its investments in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania could reach tens of billions of euros.

## Oktoberfest 2026: Everything you need to know

https://p.dw.com/p/5MmUHOktoberfest attracts millions of visitors every yearImage: Wolfgang Maria Weber/IMAGOAdvertisementOn Saturday, September 19, at noon, Oktoberfest will once again open with the ceremonial tapping of the keg, during which the mayor of Munich drives a tap into the first wooden barrel of beer and calls out "O'zapft is," a Bavarian phrase that translates to "It's tapped."

His call will be followed by 12 gunshots, a signal that lets all festival tents know that they can start serving beer.

More than 6 million people are expected at the world's largest Volksfest, which runs this year through October 4.

The festival grounds are enormous: Oktoberfest takes place on Munich's Theresienwiese, a 34.5-hectare site roughly the size of 48 soccer fields.

One reason is that nearly all 38 beer tents operate in two or three reservation shifts. When one group of guests leaves and the next arrives at the 14 large tents — which each accommodate several thousand people — huge numbers of visitors end up trying to enter and exit at the same time.

That's how, on one Saturday afternoon at last year's Oktoberfest, the crowds became so dense that movement temporarily came to a standstill. The crush caused some visitors to panic.

To prevent that from happening again, the city of Munich has overhauled its security plan for this year's festival.

Twenty-six new cameras, in addition to 56 police cameras, will monitor the grounds, while an AI system will analyze visitor flows and issue alerts if areas become overcrowded.

A newly established coordination center is also intended to improve communication between security agencies and event organizers.

Organizers and police have drawn up a plan to defend against drones over the grounds, though no details have been disclosed.

Oktoberfest may be known around the world as an international tourist attraction, but most of its visitors don't travel very far to get there.

A study commissioned by the city of Munich last year found that the festival remains overwhelmingly regional.

In 2025, 41% of visitors came from Munich, another 25% from the surrounding area and 9% from elsewhere in the region. In all, three-quarters of Oktoberfest's roughly 6.5 million visitors came from Bavaria. Another 4% came from elsewhere in Germany.

About 21% of visitors — roughly 1.35 million people — traveled from abroad. Americans made up the largest group of international visitors, accounting for just under 20% of the total, or more than 270,000 people. Italians were next, at just over 13%.

Alcohol, on the other hand, is available in whatever quantities visitors are willing — and able — to pay for. A Mass, the traditional 1-liter mug of beer served at Oktoberfest, costs between €14.80 and €15.90 ($17-$18.25) this year, only slightly more than last year.

A liter of table water comes in at a hefty €11, although the grounds also have several free drinking water fountains.

Visitors planning to enjoy one of Oktoberfest's many rides would be wise to stay relatively sober. Tickets cost anywhere from just under €5 to €14.

Germany's traditional fairground culture was officially added to the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2026, and that tradition remains very much alive at the 191st Oktoberfest. According to organizers, about 90% of the festival's more than 130 fairground operators can trace their roots back to the 19th century.

The festival remains decidedly traditional when it comes to payment, too: Many vendors still accept cash only. Visitors caught without any can withdraw money from ATMs on the grounds.

Bavarians who take pride in doing things properly generally show up in local traditional dress.

The dirndl is the traditional dress worn by women and girls, which consists of close-fitting bodice with a low neckline, a blouse worn under the bodice, a wide high-waisted skirt and an apron.

Men wear lederhosen, the traditional leather shorts or knee-length breeches with suspenders.

For those who are not Bavarian, lederhosen and dirndls are certainly an option, but they're by no means required. Munich's event marketing department, Simply Munich, advises first-time visitors to consider renting traditional garb rather than buying it.

Be aware that locals find cheap imitations of traditional dress off-putting, as Simply Munich points out: "If you want to avoid wry looks, steer clear of Seppl hats, 'leather pants' made of cloth and garish mini plastic dirndls. With jeans and a checkered shirt, you'll definitely be more suitably dressed."

## Rebels, workers, radicals: The making of &apos;Red Berlin&apos;

https://p.dw.com/p/5MqA6A Molotov cocktail explodes during the forced eviction of a squat in the Berlin district of Friedrichshain in 1990Image: Stefan Trappe/IMAGOAdvertisementBerlin has a reputation for being unruly. When Joseph Goebbels was appointed the Nazi Party's district leader in 1926, he described Berlin as "the reddest city in Europe besides Moscow." From the Tailors' Revolution of 1830 and the barricades of 1848, to the rise of a vast industrial working class later in the 19th century, revolution, protest and rebellion run deep throughout the city's history.

While the future German philosopher and revolutionary socialist Karl Marx was still in high school in the small town of Trier in Prussia's Rhine Province, Berlin was fast becoming a center of the country's labor movement. It was during the March Revolution of 1848, a failed attempt to create a democratic German nation-state, that the General Workers' Brotherhood was founded, one of the first workers' organizations to become active in Germany.

By the 1870s and 1880s, major industries from mechanical and railway engineering to ready-made clothing were located in the city. As the working-class population grew, this, in turn, impacted the city's political culture.

"It became more populist in a positive sense: moving away from the traditional, elitist politics of the bourgeois liberals who had long dominated Berlin, and toward the inclusion of ordinary people, since at least men were now also important voters," Axel Weipert, a historian and author of the book "Red Berlin" (2019), tells DW.

Berlin's labor movement became increasingly radical, partly in response to heavy-handed repression under the Chancellor Otto von Bismarck. Although Bismarck created the first modern welfare state to try and undercut the rising popularity of the socialist movement, he also introduced laws banning socialist and communist organizations, newspapers and public gatherings, as well as independent trade unions.

After Bismarck resigned in 1890, the anti-socialist laws were repealed, and the ideas of Karl Marx and his fellow revolutionary socialist Friedrich Engels became the ideology of what Weipert describes as a "genuine mass movement" in Berlin. The Social Democratic Party (SPD), Germany's oldest political party, was formed out of a merger between the General German Workers' Association and the Social Democratic Workers' Party in 1875. Before World War I, the SPD was able to win far higher vote shares in Berlin than elsewhere in the country.

In 1914, the labor movement was seriously discredited when the SPD leadership and the trade unions decided not to oppose the war pushed by Kaiser Wilhelm II. But from 1916 onwards, Berlin became the center of mass labor strikes against the war. One of the earliest strikes was against the trial of anti-war socialist campaigner Karl Liebknecht. Together with Rosa Luxemburg and other left-wing radicals, Liebknecht founded the Spartacus League in Berlin, an underground group that later became the Communist Party of Germany (KPD).

Triggered by war-weariness, severe food shortages and economic ruin, around half a million Berlin workers joined the January Strike of 1918. Supported by the Spartacus League, the anti-war strike was led by the Revolutionary Stewards, a clandestine network of factory workers and trade unions from large munitions and metal-working factories. The same group became a core organizing force behind the overthrow of the Kaiser in Berlin on November 9, 1918.

In the chaotic, war-torn city gripped by violent revolution, the Spartacist Uprising of 1919 was launched to overthrow Germany's provisional government and establish a republic of workers' councils. Its violent suppression, which resulted in scores of deaths — Liebknecht and Luxemburg were both murdered by soldiers of right-wing paramilitary militias — permanently fractured the political left.

As the capital of the newly founded Weimar Republic, Berlin remained a hotbed of radical politics in the interwar period. This was especially true in neighborhoods with large numbers of tenement buildings, such as Wedding, Kreuzberg and Neukölln, where thousands of factory workers lived in squalid conditions. Berlin's many large factories also created shared interests and laid the groundwork for collective action.

"Bloody May" in 1929 saw brutal attacks on illegal May Day demonstrations, which left over 30 people dead. The SPD police president of Berlin had issued a ban on all public demonstrations for May Day, which the KPD defied. In response, the police opened fire on protestors in the streets and also shot through windows in residential buildings, primarily in the working-class districts of Wedding and Neukölln.

During the Great Depression era, the SPD lost much of its support between 1929 and 1933, as joblessness ravaged the city. "Unemployment is critical — it meant that men were no longer attached to the SPD-led factory floor and trade union and the social bonds of these spaces and organizations," explains Pamela Swett, a historian at the University of Rhode Island. "Unmoored from the rhythm of daily life, men sought new bonds — new ways to feel self-respect and camaraderie with others." Some joined the KPD, which became known as the "Party of the Unemployed." Others joined far-right paramilitary groups such as the Nazi stormtroopers.

After the Nazis took power in 1933, many of the labor movement leaders were killed or exiled. Then, in the aftermath of World War II, Berlin was divided into two separate political systems. East Berlin became the capital of the communist German Democratic Republic. Isolated from the Federal Republic of Germany, West Berlin deindustrialized, the labor movement weakened, and the SPD moved to the right.

During the Cold War, many left-leaning young men moved to West Berlin to evade military service. This intensified the student movement, the center of which was Berlin's Free University (Freie Universität). It was there, in leafy southwest Berlin, that historic sit-ins were held to protest the perceived authoritarianism of the government and the presence of former Nazi officials in positions of power. The fatal shooting of student Benno Ohnesorg by a police officer during a demonstration against the visiting Shah of Iran in 1967 radicalized a generation that led the widespread protests of 1968.

In the early 1970s, a housing shortage led parts of the student movement, as well as apprentices and young migrant families, to squat in West Berlin's empty buildings.

"You have migrant laborers and young laborers participating, so this was part of working-class rebellion against capitalist housing," says Ralf Hoffrogge, a labor movement historian at Berlin's Free University.

Today, as gentrification sweeps the city, Berlin is home to the strongest and most radical tenants' movements in Germany. In a 2021 referendum, 59% of voters in the city approved the expropriation of property from private real-estate companies. Hoffrogge was part of the campaign, and his 2025 book "Loud Berlin" ("Das laute Berlin") explores how the housing crisis has become a catalyst for a "socialist renaissance" in the city.

"It's not Berlin tenants against Berlin landlords. It's tenants against global capital. I think people have started to realize these contradictions, and this is why class-based policy concepts are becoming popular again."

## Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania: SPD seeks to fend off AfD

https://p.dw.com/p/5MrPuMecklenburg-Western Pomerania's state legislature is housed in Schwerin CastleImage: Jens Büttner/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementStretching along Germany's Baltic coast, Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania is known for the tourist draws of Rügen and Usedom, the quiet island of Hiddensee with the grave of playwright Gerhart Hauptmann, and the historic universities of Greifswald and Rostock, among the oldest in the country. The state is also shaped by its industrial heritage, including its shipyards and the vast nuclear power plant at Lubmin, built in former East Germany and closed in 1995.

Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania is Germany's most sparsely populated federal state, with a population of just 1.57 million at the end of 2025. The unemployment rate in the structurally weak state stands at 8%, above the national average. At roughly 6%, Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania also has by far the lowest share of foreign nationals of any of Germany's 16 federal states.

For many former East Germans, the state is associated with long summers spent at campsites and holiday resorts along the Baltic coast. It is also home to 16 national parks, more than any other federal state in Germany, and to the Mecklenburg Lake District, centered on Lake Müritz.

Yet this federal state was also the scene of some of the earliest violent attacks on asylum-seeker accommodations following German reunification. Images of burning housing blocks in Rostock-Lichtenhagen in August 1992 were broadcast around the world. That year, the number of asylum seekers in Germany reached a provisional peak of 440,000, fueling heated debates across the country.

In August of that year, hundreds of far-right rioters attacked a residential building in Rostock that housed around 100 people from Vietnam. The building was set on fire as some 3,000 local residents applauded the attackers. Miraculously, no one was killed, though many people were injured. It was the worst outbreak of racist violence in Germany since the Second World War.

Former Chancellor Angela Merkel had her electoral district in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania. For around 28 years, the state has been governed by the Social Democratic Party (SPD) in coalition with various partners.

Most recently, it has been led by the popular state premier and former federal minister Manuela Schwesig. The 52-year-old heads a coalition with the socialist Left Party. Schwesig stands a good chance of ensuring that this remains the case after the election on September 20.

Schwesig, who for a long time trailed the far-right populist Alternative for Germany party (AfD) by a wide margin in the polls, has been campaigning on the slogan "The woman against blue" — a reference to the AfD's party color.

In a televised debate with AfD lead candidate Leif-Erik Holm just over ten days before the election, the state premier described the AfD as "the most dangerous party for our country, for the economy, for social welfare, and also for us women." Addressing Holm directly, she added: "You are talking the country down."

Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania is the only eastern German state where the domestic intelligence agency has not classified the AfD as "confirmed right-wing extremist."

The center-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU), the party of Chancellor Friedrich Merz, is polling at a historic low, the lowest in any German state. A poor showing in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania could further fuel debate over the increasingly unpopular chancellor. The environmentalist Greens are also fighting to remain in the state parliament, with support hovering around the 5% threshold for representation.

With a vote distribution like this, it is highly unlikely that a majority could be assembled to elect AfD lead candidate Leif-Erik Holm as state premier. After all, every party with a realistic chance of entering the state parliament has ruled out any cooperation with the AfD.

The party's lead candidate, Leif-Erik Holm, deliberately cultivates a down-to-earth image. At campaign events, he draws on his experience as a radio host, including with the public broadcaster NDR. A member of the Bundestag for the Alternative for Germany (AfD) since 2017, he repeatedly taps into widespread dissatisfaction with the government, the economic slowdown, and the comparatively difficult social conditions in the structurally weak and sparsely populated northeast.

Holm cannot match the popular SPD state premier, Schwesig, in head-to-head comparisons of the leading candidates. But the AfD's support in the polls, neck and neck with the SPD, underscores just how deeply divided Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania remains.

## Pakistan: Suicide bombing death toll rises to at least 31

https://p.dw.com/p/5MqgiAttackers stormed the Old Police Lines complex at Kohat, a town of around 220,000 people in Pakistan's Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province Image: REUTERSAdvertisementThe death toll from a suicide bombing in Kohat, Pakistan, near the Afghan border, has risen to at least 31 people, police said on Saturday, as fighting continued to rage.

Militants stormed the district police headquarters complex at Kohat in Pakistan's northwestern Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP) province on Friday.

An explosives-laden car caused a large explosion and severely damaged a mosque on a police compound.

"At least 31 people, including 16 police personnel, have been killed and more than 100 injured so far in the militant attack and suicide bombing," a police official told AFP on Saturday.

"Several militants are still holed up in the Special Branch section of the Kohat Police Complex. An exchange of fire with them has been ongoing since late last night. Their exact number remains unclear."

Police said in a statement that the suicide attack occurred near a mosque as officers, their relatives and other civilians were worshipping inside.

The statement added that security forces engaged other militants in a subsequent gun battle after at least two other attackers tried to to enter the facility.

Ittehad-ul-Mujahideen Pakistan, a local militant group linked to the Pakistani Taliban, Tehreek-e-Taliban Pakistan (TTP), has claimed responsibility for the attack.

Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif condemned the attack and expressed sorrow over the casualties. He directed authorities to accelerate rescue operations.

Pakistan's Interior Ministry said that the "cowardly actions" of the attackers would not weaken "the nation's resolve."

President Asif Ali Zardari appeared to blame the Pakistani Taliban, or TTP (Tehreek-e-Taliban).

"The complete elimination of Fitna-al-Khawarij and terrorists operating with foreign backing is essential for national security," Zardari said, using another term for the TTP.

The attack comes at a time when relations between Pakistan and Afghanistan's Taliban rulers are already fraught. Islamabad accuses the TTP uses safe havens across the Afghan border to plot and train for attacks in Pakistan.

Pakistan blames the increase of attacks in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the border province of Balochistan further south in large part on the Taliban administration in neighboring Afghanistan.

The accusations have sparked a bitter rift and even incidents of armed conflict between the neighboring countries.

Pakistan has carried out airstrikes it says targets militants taking refuge on Afghan soil, including this June.

## Ever more Israelis moving to and investing in Cyprus

https://p.dw.com/p/5MrQ0The Israeli community in Cyprus is growing, as is the infrastructure catering to itImage: Loucianos Lyritsas/DWAdvertisementThe volatile situation in Israel and the prolonged sense of insecurity following the October 7, 2023 attacks and the wars that followed have prompted a growing number of Israelis to turn to the Republic of Cyprus.

For many, the nearby island is evolving from a holiday and investment destination into a place to settle permanently — and, in some cases, a "Plan B" in the event of a deterioration of the situation in Israel.

That is precisely how several Israelis who spoke to DW on condition of anonymity described their decision.

"I want to have a Plan B in case the situation in Israel gets more difficult," said one Israeli who recently bought a home in a large residential development in Cyprus's Limassol district.

Another pointed to Cyprus's proximity and familiarity: "It's a 40-minute flight, the same landscape, the same climate. It's like being in Israel without actually being there."

A third linked his move to business, saying that many Israeli companies have transferred some of their operations to Cyprus, making it easier for them to do business with EU countries.

According to estimates cited by Israeli media, at least 3,000 Israeli families now live permanently in Cyprus, with their number increasing by several hundred each year.

Israeli media reports also speak of tens of thousands of Israelis who have purchased property on the island. The newspaper Israel Hayom has reported that around 20,000 Israelis own property in Cyprus without living there year-round.

Figures from the Cyprus Land Registry presented to parliament show that between 2021 and 2024, a total of 3,851 property transfers and sale contracts involving Israeli buyers were recorded in the coastal districts of Larnaca, Paphos and Limassol alone.

But Israeli interest in Cyprus extends beyond apartments and holiday homes to land, tourism developments and major investment projects.

One of the most prominent examples is the Israeli hotel group Fattal, whose investments in Cyprus are estimated to run into hundreds of millions of euros and include hotels in Limassol, Paphos and Larnaca.

In Larnaca, investors with Israeli interests are also behind the redevelopment of the historic Palm Beach hotel, an investment of more than €110 million ($126 million), as well as the roughly €40 million Aqua Residences project, which foresees the construction of two 15-story towers along the seafront.

In the Limassol district, an Israeli investor is planning to revive Trozena, a village that has been abandoned for decades, by restoring around 60 homes and developing tourism facilities, a campsite and a winery.

The project, located within a Natura 2000 protected area, has sparked opposition and an investigation by the relevant authorities after some work began without the required planning and building permits.

At the same time, infrastructure catering to the Israeli community is becoming increasingly visible.

Places of worship, businesses and services for Israelis — such as restaurants offering kosher dining — have opened in Larnaca, Limassol and Paphos.

Hebrew-language advertisements can also be seen along the roads — including political campaign material. Last summer, billboards for Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar party targeting Israeli voters appeared along Cypriot highways.

Perhaps the clearest indication of the increasingly permanent nature of the Israeli presence is a planned private Jewish school in Polemidia, near Limassol, for up to 1,500 students.

All of this has added momentum to an increasingly heated political debate over foreign investment and property ownership in general.

The debate is further shaped by what critics describe as Cyprus's unconditional diplomatic support for Israel and by the island's previous experience with the massive influx of Russian capital, which accelerated after Cyprus expanded its citizenship-by-investment program following the 2013 financial crisis.

Some of the strongest political criticism has come from Stefanos Stefanou, secretary general of Cyprus's Progressive Party of Working People (AKEL), and Fidias Panayiotou, MEP and leader of the Direct Democracy party.

AKEL is Cyprus's main left-wing party, and currently in the opposition. Stefanou warned of uncontrolled sales of Cypriot land to foreigners, specifically referring to purchases by Israelis and what he described as the creation of gated residential communities with their own infrastructure.

Panayiotou went further, claiming that "Israel is buying Cyprus" and warning that the Cypriot economy risks becoming overly dependent on Israeli capital.

Israel's ambassador to Cyprus, Oren Anolik, rejected claims of excessive Israeli influence, arguing that the thousands of Israelis living and doing business on the island have become part of its social and economic fabric.

He also warned that singling out a particular nationality and invoking stereotypes about "foreign economic power" could fuel antisemitism.

The Cypriot government has made attracting foreign investment a key economic priority, with President Nikos Christodoulides repeatedly stressing the goal of establishing Cyprus as an attractive destination for international investors.

When it comes specifically to Israeli investment, a statement by former Energy and Commerce Minister George Papanastasiou is indicative of the government's approach.

Speaking in 2025, while still in office, Papanastasiou said the government was "pleased to note that Israeli businesspeople are present in many sectors of the Cypriot economy," including tourism, technology, energy, health and real estate.

The debate has now reached parliament and has broadened to encompass property purchases by third-country nationals in general.

Lawmakers are considering legislative proposals from the country's two major parties, the left-wing AKEL and the center-right Democratic Rally (DISY), aimed at imposing restrictions on land purchases by non-EU nationals.
