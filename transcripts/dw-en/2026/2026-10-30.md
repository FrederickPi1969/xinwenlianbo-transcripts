## Binance crypto exchange suspends trades over North Korea-linked hack

https://p.dw.com/p/5OqTgPrefer DW on GoogleBinance has the largest volume of daily trading out of any crypto platformImage: Nikolas Kokovlis/NurPhoto/picture allianceAdvertisementBlockchain analysts said a North Korean hacking unit was likely behind the theft of more than $6 billion from Binance, the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange.

Binance temporarily suspended deposits and withdrawals for security checks and said about $3 billion in affected assets had been frozen.

US-based blockchain intelligence firm TRM attributed the attack to TraderTraitor, which it described as an elite North Korean hacking unit.

TRM said attackers moved approximately $6.36 billion out of Binance wallets across 10 blockchains, most of it within about 20 minutes. It described the incident as the largest cryptocurrency theft on record.

"Several features of this attack match earlier North Korea-linked heists, including the Bybit hack," the firm said.

Bybit lost $1.5 billion in a hack in February 2025. The FBI attributed that attack to North Korea and TraderTraitor.

Jonathan Levin, co-founder and CEO of blockchain analysis company Chainalysis, also said the Binance attack was likely carried out by TraderTraitor.

Experts say North Korea uses cryptocurrency theft and other illicit cyber activities to obtain foreign currency and help fund its nuclear program despite international sanctions.

Binance did not confirm the attribution to North Korea, saying it was "working closely with our law enforcement partners to confirm the identity of the criminals and bring them to justice."

Binance said it worked to secure the affected assets and that it had located and frozen about $3 billion (€2.7 billion) so far. It did not provide information about the remaining $3.3 billion.

"Binance will cover all hacking losses," co-founder Yi He said, without providing more details about the incident.

Binance said its initial investigation suggested an attacker had compromised a software infrastructure engineer's laptop, gaining access to an internal service and submitting unauthorized withdrawals. The company said it had found no evidence that wallet private keys had leaked.

Tether froze around $1.6 billion in its USDT currency, according to CEO Paolo Ardoino, and Circle froze some $800 million-worth of USDC, boss Jeremy Allaire said.

The news of the possible hack sent Bitcoin's value tumbling by 3.2% on Thursday evening, dropping below $79,000.

Zhao, who founded the Binance platform in 2017, said "as a shareholder, I expect all user funds will be fully covered."

He stepped down as CEO after pleading guilty in 2023. to failing to prevent money laundering on his platform. US President Donald Trump pardoned him in 2025.

Zhao and Binance have been key supporters of cryptocurrency ventures by US President Donald Trump and his family.

## Seoul fears being sidelined as US reaches out to North Korea

https://p.dw.com/p/5OrjOPrefer DW on GoogleSouth Korea has been facing pressure both from North Korea and from its ally, the United StatesImage: Evan Vucci/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementSouth Korea's relationship with the North, always strained, seems to be going through a particularly hostile patch in recent weeks.

Last month, Seoul accused North Korea of planting a mine on the southern side of the heavily fortified border. The device exploded, injuring three South Korean soldiers. Pyongyang refused to apologize and insisted this was a false-flag incident designed to tarnish the isolated nation. The North Korean regime has since conducted multiple weapons tests, held its first-ever exhibition of military drones attended by Kim Jong Un, brought up self-propelled artillery units to the border and started preparing loudspeaker systems that will blast propaganda into the South.

On Thursday, North Korea also fired around 10 short-range ballistic missiles towards the Sea of Japan just before a scheduled visit to South Korea by German Chancellor Friedrich Merz.

On top of South Korea's issues with its arch rival, many in Seoul are growing increasingly wary of their most important ally, the United States. South Korean politicians are worried that the US could push for a diplomatic breakthrough with North Korea at the cost of ignoring South Korea's security, or even leave it exposed to Pyongyang's nuclear arsenal.

US President Donald Trump famously claimed he and Kim Jong Un "fell in love" during Trump's first term. More recently, Trump has indicated he would like to resume talks . There have been suggestions that a meeting could take place on November 18 in the Chinese city of Shenzhen, where Trump is expected to attend the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit.

The North Korean side has made no official response to Washington's hints, although South Korea's National Intelligence Service informed a closed-door meeting of Seoul's intelligence committee on Thursday that Kim Jong Un discussed the possibility of meeting Trump when he met with senior members of China's politburo in Dandong on Saturday.

Seoul's fears were also fueled by the US proposing three-way talks with Russia and China on the fate of Ukraine without inviting a representative of Kyiv to attend. The worry in Seoul is that President Donald Trump may conduct a similar bypass manoeuvre on the Korean Peninsula.

"Right now, there is a problem of trust between the two governments," said Kim Sang-woo, a former politician with the left-leaning South Korean Congress for New Politics and now a member of the board of the Kim Dae-jung Peace Foundation.

A security and trade relationship that was previously considered secure has been rocked by tariffs, trade restrictions and demands from Washington that the South pay more for the US troops stationed in the country, Kim told DW.

President Lee's diplomats have lost access to key decision-making personnel in this White House, he added.

"I do not think this US administration is thinking long-term in the relationship with South Korea," Kim said. "They do not see South Korea as a part of their wider national security network and I do not think they are listening to experts on the importance of allies in the region."

"I also think that Trump is eager to meet with Kim because of his personal quest to obtain a Nobel Peace Prize."

Trump's widely publicized efforts to secure a Nobel have been thwarted so far. However, this might change if Trump somehow manages to formally end the Korean War that was put on hold by the 1953 armistice.

"The most serious concern is that the US recognizes Pyongyang as a nuclear power, which would legitimize the North's program and undermine efforts to convince it to abolish its nuclear weapons as well as the broader non-proliferation system," said Lim Eun-jung, a professor of international studies at Kongju National University.

In parallel, she said, there are fears that Trump could reach an agreement with North Korea that requires it to scrap its long-range nuclear missiles — those that are capable of hitting targets in the United States — but permits it to retain short-range weapons that can still easily reach South Korea and Japan.

That would raise questions about the US commitment to defend South Korea in case of a nuclear attack. That uncertainty, in turn, is likely to give credence to voices arguing that South Korea needs to develop its own nuclear deterrent.

"For years, we have depended on US extended deterrence guarantees under the nuclear umbrella, but this US administration is unpredictable and there are more and more people asking why we don't just go it alone," Lim told DW.

"Virtually, all our neighbors have nuclear weapons, and while no-one wants the dominoes to start falling, it could happen."

The latest escalation between Seoul and Pyongyang comes amid repeated attempts by South Korean President Lee to improve communication with Pyongyang. The perceived outreach has irritated South Korea's European allies, who are angry over North Korea deploying soldiers and supplying munition and missiles to help Russia in the Ukraine war.

Moscow is believed to be repaying Pyongyang's support with money, military technology and security guarantees.

During his visit to South Korea, Germany's Merz pointed out that "Korea's security affects Europe's security. And Europe's security also affects the security of Korea."

Berlin, just like Seoul, has been disoriented by the Trump administration turning hostile on many of its allies while seeking reproachment with geopolitical rivals such as Russia and China. During his Asia tour, Merz pushed for an alliance of middle powers to protect free trade and oppose "unilateralism, autocratic power politics and violations of international law."

"The fact remains that the United States is shifting its foreign policy, ⁠its foreign economic ​policy, and redefining its interests. As they do so, we must do the same," he said in South Korea.

It was not clear if Seoul will respond to Merz's appeal by moving closer to the European Union. At the moment, however, it seems apparent that the South Korean government is struggling to communicate with either North Korea or the US.

"It is a very difficult situation," said Kim Sang-woo. "There is no trust and no connections and that, to me, is very worrisome."

## Bollywood and the underworld — together in the spotlight?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OrDdPrefer DW on GoogleIndian actor Tiger Shroff (center) was recently shot at in Mumbai [FILE March 17, 2017]Image: Amal KS/Hindustan Times/IMAGOAdvertisement"In the entire film industry, whoever works without aligning with us, or works with Salman Khan, will be killed."

This warning was delivered in an audio message by presumed attackers on Indian actor Tiger Shroff in Mumbai earlier this month, as they claimed responsibility for the shooting.

The apparent perpetrators said they were connected to Lawrence Bishnoi, a student-politician-turned-gangster who has been in prison since 2014. Notably, police have not confirmed the gang's involvement.

The shots fired at Shroff, which injured his driver, were only the latest in a string of attacks targeting prominent figures from the Mumbai-based film industry, popularly known as "Bollywood".

Since 2022, gunmen fired shots at actor Salman Khan's apartment and at the home of filmmaker Rohit Shetty in Mumbai, as well as actor Disha Patani's family home in northern India. Actor Ranveer Singh also reportedly received an extortion demand of nearly $1.1 million (around €980,000).

The attacks have spread to the other side of the globe, with a cafe owned by comedian Kapil Sharma in Surrey, Canada, also hit three times.

For an industry that once lived in the shadow of organized crime, the attacks have revived old fears, harking back to the crime bosses of the 1990s. But experts say the threat facing Bollywood today looks very different from the one it faced three decades ago.

"The mafia was always fascinated with the movie industry," said S. Hussain Zaidi, crime journalist and author of "Dongri to Dubai: Six Decades of the Mumbai Mafia," who has covered organized crime in the city for over three decades.

Gangsters like Dawood Ibrahim, Abu Salem and Chhota Shakeel "were totally obsessed" with Bollywood, he told DW, much like mafiosi who sought a foothold in Hollywood.

To the gangsters, films offered glitz and glamor, but also a way to launder their profits. They invested in films "so that they could control the industry and have a legitimate way of making money," Zaidi added.

When producers didn't cooperate, the consequences could be fatal. In 1997, music mogul Gulshan Kumar, founder of the label T-Series, was shot dead. In 2000, filmmaker Rakesh Roshan, father of Bollywood star Hrithik Roshan, was shot and wounded.

Mumbai's streets were "made red with blood" in 1998, Zaidi said, describing the year as the pinnacle of gang killings.

D. Sivanandhan, the former Police Commissioner of Mumbai, who led the crackdown, says 101 businessmen were shot dead in 1998 alone, or one "every third day," mostly over unpaid extortion demands.

Police efforts were reinforced by a tough new organized crime law passed in 1999, which Sivanandhan calls the "ultimate weapon." Security forces used electronic surveillance and preventive detention, and seized some 2,500 illegal weapons.

"A few encounters also happened, but that is not a policy nor a strategy,” he told DW, referring to police shootouts.

By the early 2000s, the gangs' hold had been broken, and a new Mumbai seemed to be emerging.

Bishnoi, 33, comes from a landowning family in the northern state of Punjab and was studying in Chandigarh when he was drawn into violent student politics. Then, he was arrested, incarcerated and eventually turned to crime, says journalist Jupinderjit Singh, author of "Gangs of Punjab."

Bishnoi went on to build what Singh describes as one of the first criminal syndicates spanning several northern Indian states. Investigators say he still directs operations from prison using concealed phones and messaging apps.

Currently, Bishnoi is held in a jail in Prime Minister Narendra Modi's home state of Gujarat.

His network stretches beyond India's borders. In September 2025, Canada designated the Bishnoi gang a terrorist entity. Last month, the FBI added Satinderjeet Singh, popularly known as Goldy Brar, to its Ten Most Wanted list, offering up to $1 million for information leading to his arrest. US prosecutors described him as the alleged leader of Bishnoi's group in North America.

While Bishnoi comes from an affluent landowning family, his shooters are mostly poor. The gang usually recruits them online.

"There are so many youngsters who want to do something… they are just pulled by the lore attached to Lawrence Bishnoi," Singh explained. "Recruiting is no big issue… They offer money, protection, weapons."

In 1998, Salman Khan was accused of killing a blackbuck, an antelope revered by Bishnoi's community. Bishnoi vowed revenge, and many of the attacks targeted people close to the actor, including the 2024 killing of politician Baba Siddique, which police have linked to the gang.

Singh sees the feud as a bid for respectability. Criminals "always try to find something that makes them a moral guardian," he says, and this case was Bishnoi's "chance to redeem himself."

"Bollywood has a lot of money," Singh added, pointing that the previous generation of gangsters was no longer in position to take advantage.

"With Dawood Ibrahim and his counterparts away, there was a vacuum, and Lawrence Bishnoi is trying to exploit that."

Sivanandhan explained the old Mumbai gangs ran a whole "basket" of illegal businesses, from smuggling to protection rackets and cricket match-fixing. Their members were "local boys," while the Bishnoi gang has “nothing to do with Mumbai,” he added. It targets Bollywood because the industry is "loaded with money" and a "soft underbelly."

Meanwhile, Zaidi sees the attacks mainly as publicity. "It's a way to grab headlines," he told DW. Within hours of the recent attack on Shroff, someone claimed on social media that he was targeted for being close to Khan, a logic Zaidi called "absolutely illogical and absurd."

But publicity is useful to gangs that live off extortion. "The base material of extortion is: create fear," a security professional responsible for the protection of several Bollywood stars, who asked not to be named because of the sensitivity of the issue, told DW.

Still, experts say its hard to argue that the ongoing violence is comparable to the 1990s crime wave.

## Bayern Munich&apos;s Kenya friendly highlights Africa&apos;s growing pull for global sport

https://p.dw.com/p/5OomPPrefer DW on GoogleBayern Munich and Ajax will play the game that opens the new 60,000-seater stadium in NairobiImage: Simon Maina/AFPAdvertisementBundesliga champions Bayern Munich are to travel to Nairobi, Kenya during the winter break to play a friendly that opens the newly finished Raila Odinga International Stadium in the capital city. The German side will face Ajax Amsterdam as the game acts as part of the country's preparations for the 2027 African Cup of Nations (AFCON), which will be held in Kenya, Uganda and Tanzania next summer.

"Bayern has millions of fantastic fans in Africa with whom we feel a close connection – it's a continent with a huge passion for football," Rouven Kasper, Bayern board member for marketing and sales, said in a club statement.

"We will be making a further contribution to raising the profile of the Bundesliga and are very much looking forward to this special event."

The news is just the latest venture into global sports investing in Africa. Earlier this month, Formula 1 announced it was returning to the continent after Rwanda secured the rights for a deal from the early 2030s onwards. In September, World Athletics revealed Nairobi would host the World Championships in 2029. It will be the first time the event has been held in Africa.

Simon Chadwick, a business management and program director at the African School of Economics, believes that over the last two decades the professionalization and industrialization of sports has begun to emerge in Africa.

"Countries like Rwanda, Kenya, Morocco and obviously, South Africa, if we go back to the last decade of the previous century, have increasingly begun to deploy sport as a policy instrument," Chadwick told DW, adding Senegal and Egypt to the list.

"There are these image and reputational benefits which very often are bundled up as soft power. It's about attractiveness and appeal and engagement," said Chadwick.

"It's almost like a kind of professionalization, industrialization and politicization of sport that we're seeing elsewhere in the world has now arrived in Africa. We are seeing a much greater sense of kind of self-determination within Africa to do this," he added.

"This is not just about sport. It is also about diplomacy and bilateral trade, but also about the broader economic and industrial context within which sport sits."

For Kenya specifically, the building of the stadium is in line with the housing and sports infrastructural development that has been so heavily promoted under President William Ruto. Hosting this game is a symbol of the development currently happening in Nairobi.

"The bigger picture is that Nairobi now is positioning itself as a as an alternative sports venue," Richard Wanjohi, Editorial and Research Lead at the African Sports and Creative Institute (ASCI), told DW.

"I think we have seen what Kigali has done over the last number of years. For us to then come back into this conversation as a major city that can host such an event, it's huge. A few weeks later, we'll have the rugby sevens series again and then ultimately, the Africa Cup of Nations in July."

Chadwick believes that unlike Rwanda, Kenya's relative stability and pre-existing tourist industry allows for a more natural development of sports investment.

"This is not Kenya trying to build a tourism industry through an engagement in sport. I think it's an extension of what Kenya has been doing for a number of years through sport."

Combined with being a multi-sport country - athletics, rugby, football, volleyball – Wanjohi believes Kenya is ready to make the most of that performance on the field or track.

"It's about time that we also host the world and show them that we can do this from a hosting perspective, but also from developing infrastructure that is ready to host such sports events," Wanjohi said.

"It's beyond just saying that we have the talent that is already in the sport, but it's saying that we have the infrastructure, we have the skills, we have the capability to host this as any other African, if not global nation."

## The dachshund, a humorous icon with political symbolism

https://p.dw.com/p/5OA3DPrefer DW on GoogleThe hot dog costume for the sausage dog is a classic at the annual Paris Sausage WalkImage: Folglio Antoine/ABACA/IMAGOAdvertisementPablo Picasso was renowned for having numerous muses — mostly women with whom he also had harmful relationships. But there's at least one of these muses who was freed from toxic romantic entanglement: a cute dachshund called Lump.

Before becoming his most famous canine model, Lump originally belonged to US photojournalist David Douglas Duncan.

The dog and the photographer visited Picasso at his villa in Cannes in 1967. According to Duncan, upon their first encounter, Lump jumped right onto Picasso's lap. He apparently "decided" to stay with the painter, and they lived together for six years.

The story does not reveal how Lump's decision was communicated, but that immediate special bound was immortalized through art: Picasso painted the dachshund's portrait directly onto a ceramic dinner plate that day, and Duncan did a series of photos documenting them together while the work was created.

The plate and the photos are on show at the new exhibition "The Dachshund. An Icon off the Leash" held at the Kunsthalle Bremen until March 28, 2027.

Lump also appears in many of Picasso's series of paintings reinterpreting Diego Velazquez's masterwork "Las Meninas," in which the original work's large mastiff was replaced with Picasso's adoptive pet.

With their long ears and extreme length compared to their short legs, dachshunds have a silhouette that's instantly recognizable: "It's really a dog with an iconic shape, and that makes them interesting for many things" — from art to advertisement, says exhibition curator and art historian Annett Reckert.

Alongside Picasso, a surprising number of important artists, including Andy Warhol, Otto Dix, David Hockney, Max Liebermann and Rosemarie Trockel, have paid tribute to the dachshund. "It's striking how often the dachshund has served as a muse, model and studio accomplice for them," Reckert tells DW.

Combined with this prolific artistic career, the dachshund can equally be explored from a historical perspective, since "there's also the fact that we associate them with something German," the curator adds.

Bred to track and chase badgers, foxes, rabbits and other burrowing animals, the dachshund originated in Germany over 300 years ago. Breed standards were established in 1879, and the German Teckel Club (DTK) — one of the oldest dog breeding organizations in Germany — was founded in 1888.

The name combines the German words "Dachs" (badger) and "Hund" (dog), literally translating to "badger dog." But Germans more commonly call them Dackel or Teckel.

Because of their long, narrow build, dachshunds are also colloquially known in English-speaking countries as wiener dogs or sausage dogs.

Beyond their role as hunting dogs, dachshunds have a surprisingly rich place in German cultural history, from bourgeois stereotype to Olympic icon.

Germany's last emperor, Kaiser Wilhelm II, was an avid dachshund enthusiast and owned several of them.

Two of his dogs, Wadl and Hexlsome, made history by causing a minor diplomatic incident during a visit to the estate of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, Konopiste Castle, in 1908. The dachshunds reportedly killed one of the archduke's prized golden pheasants. The attack was widely commented on in aristocratic circles, and Kaiser Wilhelm II was forced to issue a formal apology.

The pheasant incident was obviously not as consequential as another event involving Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne: his assassination in Sarajevo on June 28, 1914, which became the primary catalyst of World War I.

Still, the dachshund remained present throughout this global conflict. In propaganda maps and political cartoons, Germany was represented as a dachshund, the British as a bulldog and the French, a poodle.

The popularity of the dachshund in the United States plummeted during the World War I because of this association; those who owned one called their dogs "liberty hounds" instead of using the German word.

However, their missions weren't as outlandish as the stories published by the UK's tabloid press relatively recently, which claimed that the Nazis aimed to build armies of talking dachshunds. The rumors followed the publication of a non-fiction book on dogs published in 2011, "Amazing Dogs: A Cabinet of Canine Curiosities," which outlined Nazi research into dogs' — including dachshunds' — intelligence and personalities.

The dachshund definitely didn't fit into the Nazis' aesthetics: "They were used in the war, too, but not in the same way as other dogs. After all, they weren't the kind of dog a Nazi would have wanted to be seen with for the sake of appearances," notes Reckert. "Take Hitler, for instance: He famously made public appearances with his German Shepherd, Blondie. They would rather be seen with a Doberman or dogs that looked big and dangerous."

The endearing potential of the dachshund is actually one of the ideas that guided Reckert in her curation of the exhibition. They "simply give the impression that they have a sense of humor," the art historian says. "That's an attribution, of course. But they really do spread good cheer and have a truly likable character."

In the 1970s, this slightly absurd and cheerful domestic companion inspired a figure embodying a new image for West Germany: the official mascot for the 1972 Summer Olympics in Munich, Waldi.

A stark contrast to the heavy symbols that represented Germany during the Third Reich — the eagle, Germania, forests, Wagner and Prussian discipline — the mascot created by renowned graphic designer Otl Aicher and his intern, Elena Schwaiger, was as iconic as the shape of the dachshund.

The designer was vehemently against including brown or any of Germany's national colors — red, black and gold — in the brand identity. Instead, he went for delicate pastel shades: "The approach to color was truly visionary," notes Reckert.
