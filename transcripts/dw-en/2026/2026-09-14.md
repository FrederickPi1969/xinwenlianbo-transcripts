## Finland: EU leaders meet to forge new Arctic policy

https://p.dw.com/p/5MYM0European leaders are in Finland to plot the increasingly important Arctic region's futureImage: Michael Kappeler/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementEuropean leaders are gathering in Rovaniemi, Finland, on Monday to plan a path forward for the bloc's presence in the Arctic.

The talks will focus on emerging shipping lanes, raw mineral deposits and security threats from the US, China and Russia.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen, the prime ministers of Estonia and Latvia, the presidents of Lithuania and Romania, EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas and EU Council President Antonio Costa are all scheduled to attend Monday's "European Arctic Summit."

"The idea is essentially to strengthen the EU's common strategic approach to the Arctic in this situation, where the geopolitical and economic significance of the region is growing considerably," according to Tuomas Tikkanen —  Finnish Prime Minister Petteri Orpo's senior adviser on European affairs.

Orpo, who initiated the summit, has emphasized that the Arctic is an, "increasingly important part of Europe's security environment."

Finland also insists European icebreaker capabilities are included in the strategy, saying they are key to Europe's ability to operate in the High North, adding that without them it would be impossible to protect supply and trade routes, or military presence across the region.

No formal decisions are expected Monday, but the European Commission has said the talks will help shape its new Arctic strategy, which will be unveiled over the next few weeks.

National leaders can still have a say impacting the final document, "so in that sense this is quite a timely meeting," said Finnish adviser Tikkanen.

The Arctic, which is warming nearly four times faster than other regions, has seen increased activity as receding ice opens potential transport routes as well as access to mineral resources.

That changing environment has spurred the US, China and Russia to increase energy, resource and military activity.

In August, China and South Korea both sent container ships through the region, testing the viability of the North Sea Route in Russian territorial waters.

Although Canada, China, Russia and Scandinavian countries are all considered Arctic nations, the most concerning development of late for the EU and member state Denmark have come from an entirely unexpected corner — the United States.

US President Donald Trump has threatened to blow up the NATO military alliance through his insistence that "the US must have Greenland" — a semi-autonomous island nation that is officially Danish territory — citing national security concerns, even threatening to take it by force if necessary.

Trump argues that Denmark cannot protect Greenland, which is not part of the EU, from China and Russia.

Last week, European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, on a visit to Greenland, also announced closer cooperation and an investment package of €200 million ($231 million) to be used in part to set up infrastructure to extract valuable raw materials.

During her visit, von der Leyen vowed that the EU would "be more engaged in Greenland and in the Arctic," saying the package would "create new opportunities and a strong and engaged EU in both Greenland and the Arctic."

## North Korea says recent drill shows &apos;huge destructive power&apos;

https://p.dw.com/p/5MXquNorth Korea staged live-fire drills on Saturday after US-South Korea military exercisesImage: Ahn Young-joon/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementNorth Korea said Monday that its recent "live-fire" drill demonstrated "huge destructive power."

During the test on Saturday, different types of weapons were fired simultaneously, with the aim of boosting the morale of North Korean troops and improving their capabilities to "completely collapse the enemy," according to the state news agency KCNA.

KCNA reported that the drill involved five army ​units and tested attack drones, tactical cruise missiles, tactical ballistic missiles, and other weapons systems.

Experts say North Korea is not slowing down its weapons activities, even after US President Donald Trump recently scaled back a military exercise with South Korea in an apparent effort to revive diplomacy with leader Kim Jong Un.

North Korean Marshal Pak Jong Chon was cited by KCNA as saying the drill intended to "improve the level of specialization to bring down the enemy's armed forces organizationally and structurally once an order is issued." He added that the mobilized weapons systems demonstrated "the huge destructive power of concentrated fire."

South Korea's Joint Chiefs of Staff said several missiles were fired on Saturday and traveled about 250 kilometers (155 miles) into North Korea's eastern waters.

The distances indicate that the weapons are designed to strike targets in South Korea, including US military bases.

The firing drill came a day after the United States, South Korea, and Japan concluded a trilateral military exercise that North Korea considers a provocation.

In August, Trump ordered the Pentagon to "substantially reduce" the Ulchi Freedom Shield exercise conducted jointly with South Korea. According to the US president, the decision was due to his good relationship with Kim Jong Un.

North Korea rejected Trump's move, saying that shortening the exercise did not change its "provocative, aggressive nature."

Experts say Kim Jong Un likely believes his position in any potential diplomacy with the US has improved since his first meetings with Trump in 2018 and 2019. Kim said earlier this year that he could return to talks if the US abandons its "hostile policy" toward his country and recognizes what he calls North Korea's status as a nuclear state.

## Can Iran replace sea trade with land routes?

https://p.dw.com/p/5MMRfIran is looking beyond its southern ports as it seeks alternative routes for trade and importsImage: Majid Asgaripour/WANA/REUTERSAdvertisementCan land corridors offset the impact of the maritime blockade on Iran? The US-led maritime blockade targeting Iranian ports has severely disrupted trade through its southern ports, but it is also accelerating a strategy Tehran had discussed for years: moving more commerce through northern ports, railways and land corridors.

Economy Minister Ali Madanizadeh, visiting Moscow for talks with Russian officials, said Iran should activate its northern borders and shift a significant share of imports and exports away from the country's southern maritime routes.

The idea is straightforward. Iran has seven land neighbors, access to the Caspian Sea and several routes linking it to Russia, Central Asia, Turkey, Iraq, Pakistan and the Caucasus. In theory, that gives Tehran more options than a country dependent on a single maritime gateway.

The question is whether those routes have enough capacity to replace what is being lost at sea.

Alireza Salavati, a London-based political economy commentator, told DW that Iran does have unused capacity.

"Iran has more room to maneuver than its dependence on southern ports might suggest,” he said.

Iranian authorities said in late 2025 that the country's northern ports had more than 30 million tons of nominal annual capacity, with less than one-third in use. That suggests room for more grain, food and general cargo through the Caspian Sea.

Turkey provides another route toward Europe. Rail freight between Iran and Turkey depends partly on train ferries across Lake Van, which carried about 477,000 tons in 2024.

To the northeast, Sarakhs and Incheh Borun connect Iran through Turkmenistan toward Central Asia, Russia and potentially China. To the northwest, Astara is part of the International North-South Transport Corridor linking Iran to Azerbaijan and Russia, he said.

The Astara freight terminal is designed for 3.5 to 4 million tons a year, but handled only about 345,000 tons in the first five months of 2026. The unfinished Rasht-Astara rail link remains an important bottleneck.

For specific goods, these routes matter. Grain from Russia and Kazakhstan, medicines, industrial inputs and regional trade can be redirected more easily than crude oil.

Jafar Ghaderi, a member of Iranian parliament's Economic Commission, recently said that 83% of what he described as Iran's 210 million tons of imports had traditionally moved through southern maritime borders, a tremendous volume of goods.

"Whenever we are under a maritime blockade, we face problems," hhe said. "How much capacity do our eastern, western and northern corridors actually have?"

The cost difference is also large. Majidreza Hariri, head of the Iran-China Chamber of Commerce, said shipping one container from China to Iran through southern ports costs about $3,000 (€2,580), and that moving the same container overland costs roughly $12,000.

With around 2 million containers entering through the south annually, Hariri estimated that a prolonged shift to land routes could add about $18 billion to Iran's trading costs.

A 50,000-ton shipload would require around 2,000 truck journeys if each lorry carried 25 tons. Those trucks would then be unavailable for domestic distribution, while longer journeys increase fuel, insurance, warehousing and border costs.

Low-value goods are particularly vulnerable because transport expenses account for a larger share of their final price.

Umud Shokri, an energy strategist and senior visiting fellow at George Mason University, said Iranian officials are often discussing two different kinds of alternatives.

One involves alternative navigation lanes around Hormuz. The other involves shifting trade toward Pakistan, Iraq, Turkey, the Caspian region, Jask and Chabahar.

A large crude tanker can carry roughly 1.5 to 2 million barrels. Replicating that volume by road would require thousands of tanker trucks.

Rail is more efficient, but still cannot match the cost, speed or scale of maritime transport.

Pakistan can help move imports through Karachi, Port Qasim and Gwadar. Iraq can support regional trade and limited fuel flows. Caspian routes can connect Iran to Russia, Kazakhstan and Azerbaijan.

"So the plan is realistic as a resilience strategy, but not as a replacement strategy,” Shokri told DW.

The Goreh-Jask pipeline was built specifically to reduce Iran's dependence on the Strait of Hormuz by moving crude to the Gulf of Oman.

But Shokri says actual usable capacity has remained far below the project's original ambition.

The lesson is broader: building a pipeline is not the same as creating a functioning export system. Storage, terminals, buyers, insurance, security and financial settlement all have to work together.

They can move food, medicine, industrial inputs and some regional exports. They can also deepen Iran's economic links with Russia, Central Asia, Turkey, Pakistan and Iraq.

If investment continues, the blockade may accelerate infrastructure projects that Tehran had previously treated as long-term opportunities.

But these routes come with higher costs, limited capacity and their own political dependencies.

## In cosmopolitan Berlin, elections still closed to foreigners

https://p.dw.com/p/5KP3rBerliners head to the polls September 20 to elect a new state parliament. The anti-immigrant AfD party has been making significant gainsImage: Sonam Mishra/DWAdvertisementAbdu Taviq, an Egyptian software developer, ended up in Germany's capital more or less by chance seven years ago. "I wouldn't say I chose Berlin," he told DW. "It was just that the company that offered me a job was here."

But now he loves it. The culture is "better than any other city I have experienced," he said. That's partly because of its diversity, but he also likes the way Berliners approach life. "They're more interested in living than just working," he said. "Here I feel life is more relaxed, and this also offers the opportunity for people to get more creative."

But there's plenty about the city that he's not particularly happy with, including Berlin's rapidly rising rental prices, and the notoriously opaque bureaucracy is a constant source of annoyance. "I myself would like to start my own business, but it is difficult because of all the bureaucracy," he said.

In general, Taviq is dissatisfied with Berlin's current government, a conservative-led coalition of the Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) — but the trouble is, as a non-German national, he has no say in deciding whether it should stay in office or not. That is particularly frustrating at the moment, when the streets are bristling with campaign posters ahead of the election on September 20.

"We are people who pay taxes, but we don't have any representation at all, even on local level," he said. "The cuts to kindergartens and German courses, for example, affect me directly."

The German capital is growing and becoming more international. While the voting population in the upcoming election will be bigger than ever — mainly because the voting age has been lowered to 16 — so is the number of the disenfranchised.

Of Berlin's population of 3.9 million, a full quarter — around 976,000 adults — cannot vote because they don't have German citizenship. That number has doubled since 2011.

This is partly because of Germany's political structure. In Germany and across Europe, citizens of other EU countries can vote in municipal elections. In London, people from Commonwealth countries can do the same as long as they have UK residency.  Spain, meanwhile, also has reciprocal voting agreements with several non-EU countries — in South America and elsewhere — that allow their citizens to vote in municipal elections, including for the mayors of Madrid and Barcelona.

Berlin's upcoming election is not a municipal one. Since Berlin is one of Germany's 16 federal states, this will be a state election, which means that voting rights are more restricted. Berlin's municipal elections, where EU citizens are allowed to vote, only decide who governs the city's individual districts, and their political influence is limited.

The disenfranchisement is particularly acute in central Berlin. For that reason, Stefanie Remlinger, Green Party mayor in the district of Mitte, has organized a "symbolic election" for the non-Germans in her area — some 135,000 adults out of a population of 365,000. The initiative invites foreigners to vote at 15 ballot stations over a two-week period in early September to "raise awareness for the issue of voting rights for all."

"Mitte is an extremely heterogeneous district with a very international population," Remlinger told DW via video call from her office. "We have people from 170 different countries living here, and a lot of them don't have the right to vote. It's important to us to signal to those people that we value their opinions, that we see them."

Getting people to vote in a meaningless election is a tough sell. Remlinger said she expected only a few hundred people to take part, and, anyway, she thinks the political climate in Germany is not exactly immigrant-friendly at the moment. Even in cosmopolitan Berlin, the anti-immigrant Alternative for Germany (AfD) is currently polling in second place, with nearly 20%.

"We as the Greens would liberalize voting rights even more, but I don't see the general debate in Germany going that way," she said. "We're fighting a rightward shift and a lot of hostility to immigration, and I think the chances of changing something on that issue right now are very bad."

To illustrate the state of the current debate, Remlinger mentioned a recent panel discussion she was part of, where a politician from the neoliberal Free Democratic Party (FDP) — a party that calls itself socially liberal — said voting rights shouldn't be opened to foreigners because they might start their own parties and take over the government. "And that from someone from the FDP!" said Remlinger, exasperated.

Taviq is also aware of this anti-immigrant shift in German politics. In fact, he said, that was one of the reasons he was considering leaving Germany altogether. "All the parties are trending right-wing, so it's getting more hostile," he said.

From an economic perspective, it might be considered a shame if qualified people like Taviq decided to leave Germany. The country lacks workers. The government estimates that the country will be short of about 440,000 workers by 2029, and even the AfD leadership claims it has nothing against immigrants who contribute to the economy.

Though some German cities and local authorities do have an elected advisory board for non-German residents, offering immigrants a real vote is frequently seen as a step too far.

Remlinger herself thinks that the best path to enfranchisement is not to offer foreign nationals voting rights, but to make it easier for immigrants to become German citizens.

That happens to be another one of Taviq's complaints about Berlin: "I applied for citizenship two years ago, and I didn't get any response," he said.

Germany has recently tightened citizenship rules again. Last year, Chancellor Friedrich Merz's government reversed the three-year "fast-track" citizenship option introduced under his predecessor, Olaf Scholz. Still, the government did keep another of Scholz's reforms, which allows people to apply for citizenship after only five years of residency.

More and more Berliners are in fact becoming German: 2025 saw about 39,000 people naturalized in the capital, 17,000 more than in 2024.

That avenue is still closed for Taviq, but in the meantime, a little more political participation would go a long way. "If I could vote, I would reconsider leaving, to be honest, because then I could affect things, but now I'm just like a bank machine — they come to me and take half of my salary."

## Germany: Merz&apos;s CDU comes out on top in Lower Saxony polls

Some 6.3 million people were eligible to take part in the elections, which saw a turnout of 64.6%, well up on the 57% participation at the 2021 polls.

The poll in Lower Saxony was seen not only as a test of public sentiment ahead of state elections next year but also as a barometer of how the AfD is currently faring in western German states, where the far-right party has less support than in the east.

The elections in the state come just a week before the next state polls in the eastern state of Mecklenburg-Vorpommern and the city-state of Berlin.

The vote in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern will be under especially close scrutiny, with the AfD expected to make strong gains while the CDU is polling at just 7%.

## Cuban dance teacher ordered EU sabotage attacks from Russia

https://p.dw.com/p/5MQHqOemis Romagoza Durruthy teaches Latin dance in northern RussiaImage: vk.ruAdvertisementOn social media platforms, the man called himself "Adrian" or "Dios," meaning "God” in Spanish. In 2024, accounts linked to the man posted job ads on Telegram. "Work as a photographer," one offered in Russian in a chat about opportunities in Poland. "To those interested in applying, send resume, phone number and passport," another advertised in a group for Cuban expatriates in Russia. "Looking for people with military experience in monitoring, intelligence, logistics and tactical maneuvers," one said in Spanish in a group for Latinos, promising $1,500 (about €1,300) plus a bonus for each operation completed.

Dios lured multiple people into carrying out sabotage for Russia in at least four EU countries, an investigation by DW and three other European broadcasters has found. Most of the recruits came from Spanish-speaking countries.

For months, DW and its partners conducted dozens of interviews with security officials and people close to the suspects. We pieced together classified court documents, official statements, social media posts and leaked data to find out who Dios is and reconstruct how he built a network of saboteurs to commit arson attacks across Europe.

One of the people Dios recruited was Luis Alfonso Murillo Diosa, who was 34 years old at the time.

Originally from Caicedo, a rural town in western Colombia known for its mountainous landscapes and its crops, Murillo was raised in a family of coffee farmers. But several years of poor harvests pushed the business into debt, according to someone close to the family.

Murillo spent six years in the Colombian army. According to court documents, he left in 2015. He then hopped between jobs like many ex-soldiers, juggling barista courses, stints as a bodyguard and his family's coffee business.

In early 2024, Murillo decided to enlist in the Ukrainian army, where foreign fighters can earn a few thousand euros a month when deployed to the front line, court documents show.

Looking for information about the process, he joined a Facebook group for Latin Americans seeking to fight in Ukraine. That's when he was contacted by Dios.

"It's often vulnerable people who are being targeted,” said Daniela Richterova, an associate professor in Intelligence Studies at King's College London. "Vulnerable people who need money and are willing to travel for money and are willing to not ask many questions about the purpose of what they're carrying out."

In a private Facebook message, Dios introduced himself as a member of a "special group," and asked Murillo to work with him on "special missions" in Europe instead of going to Ukraine, according to classified court documents reviewed by DW.

Murillo took the offer, and Dios organized everything remotely: flight tickets, accommodation, ground transport. They never met in person.

Once Murillo reached Bucharest in July 2024, Dios sent him a satellite image with a recycling facility and an oil well marked. Murillo was supposed to take photos and videos of the locations — and later start a fire. However, he was detained while walking near the recycling facility one night after taking photos. According to court records, Murillo told investigators that he never intended to commit arson. He said he was trying to get to Europe to reach Ukraine, and didn't know for whom he was working.

DW and its partners have confirmed that Murillo was one of at least a dozen people recruited by Dios for acts of sabotage across Europe. Such recruits are frequently referred to as "disposable agents": people often hired online who at times don't even know the identities of their employers or the real aim of their assignments.

The Kremlin has increasingly used disposable agents after EU governments expelled scores of Russian diplomats suspected of espionage and disinformation following the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, the conflict researcher Keir Giles told DW.

"That left a gap in Russia's capability, and it took time for them to find this new means of recruiting random people to carry out sometimes apparently random attacks," said Giles, who has written several books about Russia's military.

In 2024 alone, disposable agents burned down a shopping center in Poland, set fire to an Ikea store in Lithuania and delivered incendiary packages that ignited at logistics hubs in several countries. German authorities said throwaway agents, as they are also called, steered a drone with explosives toward a Ukrainian freight plane in Leipzig in August.

The suspected perpetrators of such attacks are often not Russian — nor do they have a known prior connection to Russian intelligence services. Many of the people prosecuted have been Ukrainian refugees, low-level criminals or local youths looking to make quick money.

"Russia doesn't have to be particularly careful about covering its tracks, because there is no downside to being detected," Giles said. "And, of course, it boosts the idea that Russia wants to project that it is omnipotent, that it can cause damage wherever."

DW confirmed that, over a span of four months in 2024, Dios instructed his recruits to set fire to two construction-supply businesses in Poland, a bus garage in the Czech Republic, a recycling facility in Romania and equipment destined for Ukraine produced by a company in Lithuania.

Lithuanian prosecutors were the only ones to publicly attribute the series of arson attacks to Russia's military intelligence service, known as GRU. They also put a Cuban man living in Russia on an international wanted list.

After reviewing classified court documents and dozens of leaked files, DW and its partners were able to confirm that Cuban national Oemis Romagoza Durruthy is the man behind the alias Dios.

Born in Camaguey, Cuba's third-largest city, Romagoza has lived in Russia for at least seven years.

A Latin dance teacher in the northern Russian city of Petrozavodsk, Romagoza is listed as a contact person on the social media channels of a local dance studio, Made in Cuba. In dozens of online videos of his dance classes, he shows off his salsa, bachata and kizomba steps.

Romagoza married a Russian woman in 2022 and later obtained Russian citizenship. A series of wedding photos DW found on social media show the couple posing next to a grand piano. Other pictures show them with their two children.

DW was unable to determine when and how he came to work on behalf of Russia's secret services. Leaked data indicate that Romagoza might have run into financial trouble. In 2023, he borrowed money from several credit lenders. In one of the entries, he listed a supermarket chain as his employer.

A few months later, Romagoza joined several Facebook groups of Latin Americans looking for work in Poland or seeking to fight for Ukraine. Around the same time, Dios started posting job offers on Telegram.

It is unclear whether Dios continued recruiting people or directed more attacks after September 2024. But Russia's relentless sabotage campaign across Europe hasn't stopped.

In August alone, Estonia arrested two suspects for an arson attack targeting a company that supplies unmanned military vehicles to Ukraine, and Poland reported fires at two other facilities where components for drones and helicopters are manufactured. Authorities in both countries suspect that Russia is behind the plots.

"It seems like there are still people out there who are either naive enough or are really hungry for money and motivated by monetary rewards for these kinds of activities," said Richterova, whose research focuses on Russian intelligence.

At least nine of the disposable agents Romagoza recruited have been caught. Two are serving sentences of six to eight years for attempted sabotage or terrorism. Others are currently awaiting trial.

## Germany&apos;s Zverev wins US Open in golden year

https://p.dw.com/p/5MMqIAfter waiting for years to win his first major title, Zverev has now won 2 this yearImage: Mike Segar/REUTERSAdvertisementAlexander Zverev has won his second Grand Slam singles title after the German top seed beat Ben Shelton in the US Open final on Sunday.

Zverev beat the American, who was making his first appearance in a major final, 6-3, 7-6 (7-2), 5-7, 6-2.

This success caps the best season of Zverev's career. Not only has he now won two of the four Grand Slams in the same season, but he has become just the ninth man to reach at least the semifinals at all four majors in the same season.

"We went 30 years without winning a Grand Slam title and now we've won two in three months," Zverev said on court after his second major win of the year, following his victory at the French Open in June.

"God has seen how hard we've worked and how much we've suffered. 2026 is the outcome of all the years that happened before," he said as he thanked his coaching team and family.

As a former junior world No. 1, there were great expectations on Zverev from the moment he started his pro career in 2013 at just 16 years old. Titles soon followed, as did a shock early win over Roger Federer in a tournament in his native Germany in 2016.

But the presence of Federer, Rafael Nadal and Novak Djokovic meant Zverev struggled to make a mark in the Grand Slams. After a string of disappointing exits, he had something of a breakthrough in 2020, reaching the semifinals of the Australian Open and the final of the US Open.

With age starting to catch up with at least two of the big three in modern men's tennis, Zverev looked well primed to become a dominant force in the majors. His 2021 Olympic gold felt like it might be just the start.

But he was unable to make the opportunity count, and the emergence of Jannik Sinner and Carlos Alcaraz has made the task even tougher. Finally, this year, Zverev got his hands on a major title, winning at Roland Garros in June. In doing so, he became the first German man to win a singles Grand Slam since Boris Becker in 1996 in the process.

"It was just relief, joy, everything together," he told ATPTour.com. "I didn't realize that I had won the match until I saw my box all jumping around and celebrating. In your mind, you think, 'Well, I've got to continue playing', but then I saw my dad raise his arms in the air. That's when it kind of hit me, 'Okay, we've done it. We've achieved it. We are Grand Slam champions.'"

Becker's triumph was a year before Zverev was born in Hamburg to Russian parents who were both tennis pros in the Soviet Union and moved to Germany in 1991. His older brother, Mischa, was also a pro until 2023, reaching the quarterfinals of the Australian Open in 2017 and becoming world number 25 in the same year. But it was Alexander who was destined for the even bigger stage.

In their youth, Mischa was generally coached by his father, Alexander Zverev Snr, while the younger brother was coached by his mother, Irina. His father has taken the reins in the professional arena though, and the family remain a constant fixture at Zverev's matches.

"I know there are crazy tennis parents out there as well," the 29-year-old said on the subject ahead of the US Open. "If you try to control every single aspect of your kid's life, even when the kid is 25, 26, 27 years old and at some point has a personal life… I think that is never helpful."

"But if you have smart tennis parents like we do have on tour now, I feel like Ben Shelton's father is incredibly smart, Casper Ruud's father is incredibly smart, I feel like I'm lucky enough to say the same thing about my parents. I think then it's beneficial."

Despite finally ending Germany's long wait for a major male tennis champion, Zverev is far from universally liked in his own country, or on the tennis circuit.

While there is acknowledgement of his work in promoting awareness of type 1 diabetes, with which he was diagnosed in 2022, allegations of domestic abuse have hung over his career and seen him heckled at various tournaments. His behavior on court has also seen him get in trouble before.

The first came in October 2020, when his ex-girlfriend, Olya Sharypova, claimed he had been violent towards her on multiple occasions. However, Sharypova didn't press criminal charges, and in January 2023, men's tennis governing body the ATP closed its own investigation into the matter, citing "insufficient evidence."

Welcoming that decision at the time, Zverev said: "From the beginning, I have maintained my innocence and denied the baseless allegations made against me."

But he faced a Berlin court in 2024 after similar allegations from Brenda Patea, Zverev's ex-girlfriend and the mother of his daughter. The court was told that "after a heated argument" in the German capital, Zverev allegedly pushed Patea against a wall and strangled her with both hands.

He denied the charges and the case was settled, with Zverev paying €200,000 ($233,000), €150,000 of which went to the state and €50,000 to charitable organizations. The case was officially declared "no verdict" and Zverev made no admission of guilt.
