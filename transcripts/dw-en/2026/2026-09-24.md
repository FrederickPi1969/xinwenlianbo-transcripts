## Three-way battle for power in world chess: Who will be elected FIDE president in Samarkand?

https://p.dw.com/p/5ND1hFIDE presidential candidate Wadim Rosenstein is also an avid playerImage: Meurer/Beautiful Sports/IMAGOAdvertisement"Gens una sumus" ("We are one family") is the motto of the World Chess Federation (FIDE). Yet when officials from some 200 countries gather for their congress in Samarkand on Saturday, September 26, it is not likely to feel like a joyous family reunion.

A new FIDE president is to be elected in Uzbekistan, and a three-way, politically charged race for the post has erupted, with the outcome uncertain. It has been clear since July that the incumbent FIDE president, Arkady Dvorkovich, would not be standing for re-election. The European Union had placed the former Russian deputy prime minister on its sanctions list due to his support for Russia's war of aggression in Ukraine.

The candidates standing for election are Kazakh billionaire Timur Turlov and two Germans: investor Jan Henric Buettner and entrepreneur Wadim Rosenstein, who is running as the official candidate of the German Chess Federation (DSB).

For the first time in over 30 years, FIDE will not be led by a Russian. Nevertheless, the three contenders repeatedly face two questions: What are their ties to Russia? And where do the millions come from that FIDE uses to finance itself and its competitions? For the Kremlin, chess remains a component of Russia's soft power, and little happens within FIDE without financial backers linked to Russia.

Buettner is arguably the best positioned to answer these questions. A native of Hamburg, he has made a name for himself in the chess world through his collaboration with former world champion Magnus Carlsen. The 62-year-old German has no known ties to Moscow that would require explanation. On the contrary: "We are the only team with no connection to Russia," Malcolm Pein — Buettner's candidate for vice president — told DW.

Buettner and his running mate say they intend to reduce reliance on Russian financial backers and make chess commercially successful. Buettner plans to distribute 20% of FIDE's revenue to chess federations worldwide. The former AOL executive looks to Formula 1 motor racing as his model – a vision likely considered too radical by many officials in the chess world. Consequently, Buettner is seen as having only an outside chance of winning.

The favorite to become FIDE's new president is Timur Turlov. Born in Moscow, he became a Kazakh citizen in 2022 and is one of the wealthiest men in the Central Asian nation. The entrepreneur is considered a close associate of outgoing President Dvorkovich and, by extension, a member of the FIDE establishment. Regarding finances, too, the status quo is likely to remain unchanged under the 38-year-old; after all, Turlov's publicly traded financial firm, Freedom Holding Corp., has been FIDE's main sponsor for many years.

The prospect of Turlov potentially holding the dual roles of FIDE president and major financial backer does not sit well with everyone in the chess community. Furthermore, Freedom Holding Corp. and its CEO are currently on Ukraine's sanctions list. Ukrainian authorities accuse Turlov's company of helping to finance Russia's war of aggression. Press reports also suggest that the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) has taken an interest in Turlov's financial firm.

In the spring of 2026, Turlov acquired the Hamburg-based chess software company Chessbase. Shortly thereafter, the German chess news website perlenvombodensee.de reported that the company's principal bank, Hamburger Sparkasse, had closed the Chessbase business account.

Chessbase declined to comment on the matter. Hamburger Sparkasse also did not address the specific case but told DW: "If a business relationship falls under currently existing sanctions – for example, against countries such as Russia, Belarus, or Iran – this may unfortunately result in us being unable to continue specific business relationships."

DW approached Timur Turlov for comment but had received no response by time of publication.

The position of Volodymyr Kovalchuk, vice president of the Ukrainian Chess Federation is clear.

"If Turlov wins the FIDE election and poses a threat to Ukraine's security, then we will push for him to be sanctioned by the EU as well," Kovalchuk told DW.

However, there are now signs of a rapprochement between Turlov and the Ukrainians: Turlov is currently advocating for a Ukrainian official to be elected as a vice president on the FIDE board.

The third figure in the mix, Wadim Rosenstein, is also facing intense scrutiny in Ukraine.

"It is absolutely clear that we will not support such a candidate and, should he be elected, will also propose him for sanctions," Kovalchuk said.

Rosenstein was born in 1990 in what is now Ukraine and moved to Germany at the age of nine. In the autumn of 2022, the entrepreneur and amateur chess player began organizing elaborate chess events.

Questions have dogged him ever since, with his business activities and connections to Russia being the primary focus of this scrutiny. According to Rosenstein, the companies within his Düsseldorf-based "WR Group" operate in various sectors, ranging from toilet paper trading and real estate to industrial logistics. Until 2022, a key focus was supporting German companies in regions including Ukraine and Russia, Rosenstein told DW a few months ago. However, he said he was no longer active in Russia; instead positioning himself "globally" across "70 countries," including Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, and Turkey.

However, one of the Düsseldorf entrepreneur's remaining companies in Russia has enjoyed economic success in recent years. In July, Rosenstein told DW that these companies were active "only on paper."

It has since become clear that "WR RUS," at least, is anything but a paper tiger. The Moscow-based company has been owned by Rosenstein since 2021. Publicly available data show that "WR RUS's" profit grew from around €300,000 ($341,230) to €1.7 million ($1.93 million) between 2022 and 2025.

Furthermore, records confirm that Rosenstein's German companies – "WR Logistics" and "WR Certification" – collaborated with "WR RUS" through the end of 2023. Documents from "WR RUS" also show a dividend payment equivalent to around €30,000 for the year 2023, which apparently went to him and one of his German companies.

DW again approached Wadim Rosenstein for comment on these matters but received no response by the time of publication of this report.

Since 2022, millions have been poured into Rosenstein's elaborate chess events year after year. The list of Rosenstein's events is long: it includes a top-tier tournament featuring world number one Magnus Carlsen, the 2023 World Team Blitz and Rapid Chess Championship at the prestigious Rheinterrasse in Düsseldorf, and, most recently, the global "WR Women's Chess Tour." A premium venue in Stuttgart has been selected for the tour's finale in November – a location whose name the sponsor is likely to appreciate, Rosenstein Palace.

The FIDE presidential campaign is also costly. On social media, Rosenstein presents himself aboard a private jet during an international campaign tour. According to the Frankfurter Allgemeine newspaper, a portion of the campaign costs is to be covered by Rosenstein's running mate for the vice-presidency – a wealthy real estate entrepreneur from Singapore.

Rosenstein has not disclosed the total cost of his numerous chess activities to date or the exact source of the funds. His company, "WR Logistics," is one of the main sponsors. Rosenstein told DW in July: "I have also used my own funds and brought on partners who trust me."

While Rosenstein was preparing his FIDE candidacy in April 2026, he introduced the Düsseldorf-based company "Glotech." He stated he was "proud to introduce our next partner." The sponsor is reportedly providing €250,000 for chess activities in Europe. According to Rosenstein, Glotech is "a true supporter of our vision."

However, according to Glotech Managing Director Evgeni Schukin, this support never existed: "There was no agreement, and we did not make such funds available."

Rosenstein is campaigning primarily on the pledge that FIDE would operate "professionally" under his leadership. On his official website, "WR 2026," he promises transparent and direct communication.

DW approached Rosenstein for comment on the financing of his chess activities, but he did not respond.

With the support of the DSB  and its president, Paul Meyer-Dunker, Rosenstein has succeeded in establishing himself as a serious challenger to the Turlov, the favorite within FIDE.

## After several difficult years, economists now expect German economy to grow

https://p.dw.com/p/5NEP1Leading economists presented their forecast with a bit of optimismImage: Sebastian Gollnow/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementAfter several difficult years, the German economy has regained its footing. Leading economic research institutes now expect the economy to grow by 1.3% in 2026 and by 1.1% next year — significantly more than was forecast back in the spring.

As to the reasons behind this, economists have pointed first and foremost to the global economy. Despite the war in Iran, they say, the economy is proving surprisingly robust.

"This is benefiting the German export sector, and underlying this is also the global boom in artificial intelligence," said Oliver Holtemöller of the Leibniz Institute for Economic Research in Halle during the presentation of the latest report in Berlin.

Because of the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz has cut off supplies from competitors, German companies have seen increased demand from abroad for petroleum and natural gas-based chemical products, for example.

In addition, the construction of AI data centers is driving demand for German companies. Some examples include machinery for energy production and services related to information and communication. Government spending on infrastructure and defense is also a significant driver of this upswing.

However, researchers believe it will not last. For 2028, the institutes expect growth of only 0.4%.

"The drivers of growth will continue to dwindle," said Stefan Kooths of the Kiel-based research institute IfW. High energy prices, a shortage of skilled workers, an aging population and years of low investment by many companies are slowing growth. The institutes therefore warned against misinterpreting the latest positive figures as the beginning of a new economic boom.

In this situation, the federal government is also playing a decisive role. The coalition of the conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU)/Christian Social Union (CSU) and the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) has decided to invest heavily in modernizing infrastructure and the Bundeswehr.

In the short term, this additional spending is also helping to generate business for companies and safeguard jobs. At the same time, investments in roads, railways, digital networks and military equipment should strengthen Germany's competitiveness.

Economic analysts agree that this spending has had a positive effect on the economy. The government has been an important engine of growth, especially at a time when consumers are tightening their belts and many companies are postponing investments. But this is also where economists are raising concerns. That's because the spending is financed through debt.

According to forecasts, the government deficit will rise from 4.1% of GDP this year to 4.7% in 2028. At the same time, the government's interest burden will grow. Future budgets will have to allocate an ever-larger share of funds to servicing debt.

"We have two major challenges: one is demographics, and the second is energy security and energy prices," said Holtemöller. In this context, he sharply criticized what the economists see as a lack of clarity regarding "the measures, reforms and regulatory framework" that Germany aims to use to phase out fossil fuels. They noted that a vision for how the energy transition could be carried out in accordance with the Climate Protection Act is "not even vaguely" outlined.

Germany's greatest challenge remains the declining labor force. As the baby boomers enter retirement, the economy is losing millions of workers, and many industries are already complaining of labor shortages. There needs to be greater openness "to the immigration of skilled workers," said Holtemöller.

Referring to the electoral successes of the anti-immigration Alternative for Germany (AfD) — a party that is, in part, right-wing extremist — Holtemöller cited studies indicating that "regions where populist parties are in power tend to have weaker economic growth than other regions." Openness, he emphasized, is the most important factor.

If immigration remains low, the average age of the population will only increase. This will drive up the cost of social security contributions, which in Germany are evenly split between employees and employers. On pension reform, economists are calling for doing away with the option to retire early after 45 years of contributions without any reduction in benefits. They argue that the debate about moving away from this policy is misguided.

While the government is mobilizing billions, the private sector's momentum is lacking in many places. Investors are being deterred "because they don't know what to expect or what the business landscape looks like here," Kooths explained.

"What companies need now are not new debates but decisions on economic policy," said Helena Melnikov, chief executive of the German Chamber of Commerce and Industry. "This can only be achieved through a decisive course of reform: cutting costs, reducing bureaucracy, streamlining procedures and modernizing infrastructure."

For many decades, Germany was a country with a strong industrial sector. But that sector continues to shrink, with about 15,000 industrial jobs lost every month. Particularly hard-hit are industries such as automotive, mechanical engineering and metals. At the same time, the past two years have seen the emergence of many startups in the fields of digitalization and AI.

But according to economists, these new companies lack support, particularly when it comes to financing. As a result, many are relocating to the US. "We're very good at things like development, patent applications and so on, but the problem is that the ideas developed here end up creating value elsewhere," noted Timo Wollmershäuser of the ifo Institute in Munich.

When asked what could further improve growth prospects in Germany, the researchers pointed to a catalog of reforms that was presented last spring. The proposals include, among other things, a cap on social security contributions and stronger work incentives for older workers. As for the energy transition, the institutes placed greater emphasis on price signals and advocate against taking national action on climate protection without other countries.

Notable criticism was directed at the fuel rebate scheduled to take effect on October 1. Researchers said it amounts to a blanket handout for which there is insufficient fiscal leeway.

The government cannot currently afford such expensive measures, and they are "also economically counterproductive," said Holtemöller of the Leibniz Institute. Cutting fuel taxes would "undermine efforts to reduce demand, which is precisely what is needed when energy supplies are tight."

## Houthi advances in Yemen drive displacement, deepen humanitarian crisis

https://p.dw.com/p/5NDHUThe conflict between the Iran-backed Houthis and the Saudi-backed government has prompted a humanitarian displacement crisis which is compounded by a lack of aid fundingImage: Abdulnasser Alseddik/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementMohammed Musleh is still in shock. Following the Houthi capture of the port town of Mokha earlier in September, his family had to flee their home without time to pack.

"We have no idea what will happen now," the 46-year-old told DW. His wife, Muna, is also worried, as the family couldn't bring their personal identification documents. "The Houthis told us to leave, basically immediately. We had no time to take anything," she said.

For now, the family and their two young daughters are staying with relatives in Aden, the largest city controlled by the government in southern Yemen. "We tell our kids in the meantime that we hope to return home soon," the couple said.

Rashad al-Alimi, chairman of the Presidential Council, the leadership body of Yemen’s internationally recognized government, said that recent battlefield changes would not alter the government's goal of restoring state authority nationwide.

However, given the latest advances by the Iran-backed Houthis and their territorial gains in Yemen, a return of the Musleh family to their home in Mokha is unlikely for now.

Since late July, the Iran-backed Houthis have expanded their areas of control from the north and center of the country to much of Yemen's Red Sea coast, seizing territory from forces of the internationally recognized and Saudi-backed government. The conflict continues to escalate, along with an exchange of missile and drone attacks between the Houthis and Saudi Arabia.

The Houthis are currently pushing further into the country toward the strategic Kahboub Mountains. The mountain range is significant as it overlooks areas around the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, a key waterway that had been serving as an alternative route for Saudi oil exports amid Iran's blockade of the nearby Strait of Hormuz.

The mountains are also only around 40 kilometers (25 miles) southwest of Taiz, which is under government control.

Fatima, a mother in Taiz, has been telling her children that the sounds of shelling are from fireworks. "We are scared and we are also worried as gas has already become unavailable and prices are rising. Merchants are no longer coming into Taiz," she told DW, asking not to publish her full name for fear of reprisal.

Rania and her children have also decided to stay at home in Taiz for now. "But we fear that if the Houthis tighten their grip on the city of Taiz, we will find ourselves under siege again," she said. She also doesn't want to use her first name, and vividly recalls the "suffocating blockade of 2015 and 2016, when the entry of food and goods was cut off."

During the previous war between the Iran-aligned Houthi group and the Saudi-backed Yemeni government, which began in 2014 and largely ended with a UN-brokered ceasefire in 2022, Houthi forces blocked most routes into the government-controlled city of Taiz for almost two years. The restrictions on food, medical supplies and humanitarian aid drove up prices and left people on the brink of famine.

The World Health Organization said this week that at least 674 people have been killed and 2,998 injured since fighting escalated in August. However, due to the ongoing clashes, the death tally may already be higher, as fighting is ongoing.

The conflict has also prompted mass displacement of more than 132,000 people, according to the International Organization for Migration. In recent days, more than 90,000 people found refuge in Aden, Lahj, Abyan and Al Dhale'e governorates and even in Marib, the UN refugee agency's Yemen representative Armen Yedgaryan saidon Tuesday.

## Extremist parties are thriving ― and that scares people

https://p.dw.com/p/5NCPwThe far-right AfD, seen here celebrating their win in Saxony-Anhalt, has won over many voters. For many others, however, their rise is cause for concern.Image: Michael Kappeler/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementOn September 6, Germany's eastern state of Saxony-Anhalt held elections for its state parliament. The big winner: The right-wing AfD (Alternative for Germany) party, which garnered 43.8% of the votes; more than ever before.

It's still unclear who will govern the state because the AfD does not have an absolute majority.

But it was a landslide win for the AfD, whose Saxony-Anhalt branch is classified as right-wing extremist by Germany's domestic intelligence agency, and that worries many Germans.

The day after the election, Chancellor Friedrich Merz from the conservative CDU said he was "deeply shocked" by the election results. "Not only did something change in Saxony-Anhalt yesterday but throughout all of Germany."

Merz's CDU lost more than half its share of the ballot compared to the last state election in Saxony-Anhalt. The CDU dropped from 37.1% in 2021 to 17.2% in 2026.

Two weeks later, at state elections in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania on September 20, the CDU didn't even make the 5% threshold to enter parliament.

It was the first time ever for the CDU; they got only 4.9% of the vote in the northeastern state. The strongest party was once again the AfD with 38.2%.

That same day, in the city state of Berlin, the socialist Left Party won the election and will likely govern Germany's capital. In Berlin, the CDU was the second-strongest party and the AfD came in third.

These state elections show that parties on the extreme ends of the political spectrum are growing stronger in Germany.

The country has experience with both extreme right- and left-wing governments: the Nazis that led Germany into World War II and the Socialist Unity Party (SED) that ruled then-East Germany from 1949 until 1989.

In a representative monthly survey called "What worries the world?", the polling institute Ipsos found that almost one-fifth of Germans (18%) said they were worried by growing extremism in the country when they asked about 1,000 people between the ages of 16 and 74 years in March and April 2026.

Generally, people in Germany are concerned about where their country is going. In the Ipsos survey, 82% said they saw Germany as moving in the wrong direction.

Asking people about their fears or worries is just one way to take the nation's temperature.

Kerstin Pahl, a researcher at the Max Planck Institute for Human Development in Berlin, told DW that possibilities include quantitative and qualitative surveys, as well as analyzing online discourse and the content people share on social media. It's also possible to read a nation's sentiment by observing real-life displays of emotions, like public protests and demonstrations.

For researchers like Pahl, it's also interesting to see how political issues can come to symbolize much more than their material or factual meaning.

"People may see their region's ailing infrastructure not just as proof of a lack of investment, but also as a sign that they have been forgotten [by their leaders]," said Pahl.

Extremist parties winning a higher percentage of the votes is what we see on the surface. Below that lie two kinds of societal polarization: Affective and ideological polarization.

"Affective polarization is a growing hatred for the political opponent. Most studies investigated this in the context of the US between supporters of the Democrat and the Republican Party," Eva Walther, head of the social psychology department at the University of Trier in western Germany, told DW. "When this happens on the party level, it's a problem."

Politicians of different parties often have to work together and reach compromises. They don't need to like each other, but they should treat each other with respect, Walther said.

"When this respectful attitude starts crumbling, it's a problem for democracy," she added.

The other phenomenon, ideological polarization, is when society is split into distinct groups based on what issues people view as the most pressing problem for them.

For example, many people to the right of the political center in Germany see migration as the biggest problem, while many people to the left of the political center say it's the climate crisis.

When different crisis situations occur at the same time, such as migration and climate change, researchers refer to a "polycrisis."

Polycrises intensify ideological polarization, making it even harder for societies to find a common way forward.

"At the moment, we have the climate crisis overlapping with an affordable energy crisis, more of an economic problem," Walther said.

In the eyes of those for whom the climate crisis is the most urgent issue, fossil fuels should be abandoned as soon as possible. But for those most concerned with affordable energy, relying only on renewable energy sources would needlessly raise prices.

The belief that a politician is competent and willing to tackle major challenges, to act, is a big factor in how much people trust democracy.

But that's also what makes overlapping crises so hard to manage: No matter which problems politicians attempt to solve first, one part of the population will be disappointed.

"Polycrises make it difficult for politicians to act, which means that people have less trust that politicians can solve the problems important to them," Walther said.

Right-wing extremist parties, however, aim to "satisfy people by giving them simplified answers to complex problems," added Walther.

## AI systems shifted their answers toward both left- and right-leaning users in a study. Could sycophantic chatbots deepen political divides?

https://p.dw.com/p/5N3ocResearchers looked at an array of models and found they shifted their political answers to mirror the views of a hypothetical userImage: Philip Dulian/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementA recent wave of headline-grabbing warnings from AI luminaries has fueled fears the technology could escape human control and wipe out humanity. But before artificial intelligence (AI) turns on humanity, it could contribute to humans turning on one another.

Researchers at Brazil's State University of Campinas (UNICAMP) found that popular chatbots shifted their answers when prompted with hypothetical users' political views. The scientists warn that users could mistake this tailored agreement for an independent assessment, potentially deepening polarization.

In their study published in the journal Scientific Reports, the UNICAMP team tested 21 large language models from developers including OpenAI, Meta, Google, xAI, DeepSeek and Microsoft.
The models rated their agreement with 112 statements covering seven areas of Brazilian politics, including the economy, public safety, welfare, corruption and the environment.

Each was tested under three conditions: with no information about the user's politics, with a prompt describing a left-leaning user and with one describing a right-leaning user.

When no information about the user's ideology was given, 20 of the 21 models produced answers that fell on the left of the researchers' political scale, although several were close to the center. Grok 4.1 was the only model that fell on the right.

When information was provided, every model shifted its answers toward the political orientation described in the prompt.

"The most striking finding was how widespread this behavior was," study co-author Zanoni Dias, a UNICAMP computer scientist, told DW. "All 21 models we evaluated shifted their expressed positions toward the user's stated political orientation, although the magnitude varied substantially."

The researchers described the models as "ideological chameleons" and developed a "chameleon index" measuring how far each shifted.

Meta's Llama 3.1 8B and DeepSeek V3.2 changed their responses least. Google's Gemma 3 27B and OpenAI's GPT-5 Nano showed some of the largest shifts.

Adapting an answer to its audience is not inherently problematic. A chatbot might change its vocabulary, examples or level of detail depending on the user.

But the models changed more than their language or tone: Their political positions shifted.

"The key distinction is between adapting how an answer is communicated and changing the substantive judgment being expressed," Dias said.

"We did not instruct the models to agree with the user or to answer as a partisan representative. Nevertheless, their judgments shifted toward the user's side."

The researchers interpret this as political sycophancy: AI systems reflecting what users appear to want to hear.

A possible explanation is that chatbots are trained to favor answers human evaluators rate highly. If agreeable answers get better ratings, models may learn to echo users' views.

Social media can reinforce beliefs by repeatedly recommending algorithmic content, but a chatbot could go even further. It could create a more personalized echo chamber, generating arguments for a particular user, answering their objections and refining its case throughout the conversation.

Yet users may mistake these tailored responses for impartial analysis, unaware that what a chatbot knows about them may shape its responses.

"The concern is that the same system could validate opposing political positions for different users, with each person interpreting that validation as an independent assessment," Dias said.

Research by Stanford University behavioral scientist Zakary Tormala suggests people may be particularly receptive to arguments they believe came from AI.

"People tend to see AI as more informative, more objective, less biased and less interested in persuading them than another person would be," Tormala told DW. "This can lower people's defenses and make them more receptive to hearing out what AI has to say."

"Hearing their own views validated by others is well known to increase people's certainty about their beliefs," Tormala said. "Once people become more certain, they do tend to become more resistant to persuasion."

Whether AI validation has the same effect remains unproven, he added, but it is plausible.

The UNICAMP study did not test whether political mirroring could really change users' beliefs or behavior. It measured only changes in the models' responses.

"Our study establishes a change in model responses under controlled conditions," Dias said. "It does not establish that users became more polarized, radicalized or likely to engage in conflict. Those are distinct outcomes, and the steps connecting them cannot be assumed."

Petter Tornberg, a University of Amsterdam researcher who studies AI and political polarization, agreed that the study cannot show an effect on users. He also questioned whether models would give the same answers outside the test setting.

He cautioned against assuming that chatbots would necessarily reinforce users’ views. In some conversations, they might help people reconsider them.

Tornberg said a private conversation with a chatbot differs from a public argument on social media.

"They can often provide fairly rational and evidence-based explanations without the social identity dynamics of public political debate," he told DW. "So it is at least possible that in some contexts these systems could be depolarizing rather than polarizing."

https://p.dw.com/p/5N9tzSaint-Etienne Cathedral in Metz has been draped with a welcome banner for Pope Leo XIVImage: Jean-Christophe Verhaegen/AFPAdvertisementFrance is gripped by "Pope fever." From Friday, Pope Leo XIV will spend four days in France, visiting Paris, Lourdes and Metz. All his public events have been sold out for weeks. Organizers expect around 1 million people to fill the streets of Paris on Saturday for a massive open-air mass at the Place de la Concorde.

"The pope is popular among both believers and nonbelievers," political scientist Olivier Roy told DW. He added that Leo XIV's conflict with US President Donald Trump in particular had lent him "a moral standing that extends far beyond the Catholic Church."

Massive crowds are expected for the pope's visit. Some 600,000 people who were able to get tickets will fill the entire Champs-Elysees, going right up to the Arc de Triomphe. Many more are expected to show up without tickets.

Authorities have announced that a number of metro stations will be closed. Across the city, churches have been draped with large portraits of the pope and Vatican flags. Security forces are gearing up for a major challenge.

With France's presidential election just over six months away, the visit comes at a delicate time. The left, Roy said, "appreciates Leo's personality but is skeptical about the state's involvement in organizing the reception."

Those on the far right, he continued, welcomed the visibility of Catholicism but had objected, for example, to an interfaith meeting planned in the eastern city of Metz, which they say places Islam on an equal footing with Christianity.

Because of the separation of church and state, the president will not be able to attend the large public Masses.

Leo's visit is likely to boost the pope's profile in the country, which last saw a papal state visit in 2008 when Benedict XVI traveled to Paris and Lourdes, the world-famous pilgrimage site in the country's southwest. He drew smaller crowds than those expected for Pope Leo.

His successor, Pope Francis, visited Strasbourg in 2014 and Ajaccio in Corsica in 2024 on one-day trips, and in 2023 he spent a night in the Mediterranean port city of Marseille. But each time, he came for a specific occasion rather than visiting the country as a whole. In his speeches to the European Parliament and Council of Europe in Strasbourg, Francis criticized the migration policies of what he described as an "aged and weary" Europe.

Roy described the contrasting perspectives of recent popes. He said John Paul II, pope from 1978 to 2005, viewed Christianity as "primarily European," while Francis saw it as "more shaped by the Global South."

This, Roy argued, explained Francis' "certain disregard for Europe." Leo was likely to assume the middle ground, while extending his predecessor's message, Roy said.

"He affirms the universality of the Catholic Church, while also recognizing Europe's weight within that universality."

For Augsburg-based church historian and papal expert Jörg Ernesti, Europe has remained "the most important political point of reference for popes since the late 19th century," despite the growing internationalism of Catholicism. Ernesti pointed to the demographic shift: In 1900, more than two-thirds of all Catholics lived in Europe, he said; today, the figure is only around 20%.

This internationalization had also shaped the College of Cardinals, albeit with a time lag. "While Europeans have historically always made up the majority, they now account for just 39%," he said.

During his four-day visit, Leo is scheduled to deliver 15 speeches and homilies. Most of those in Paris will be aimed at wider French society and its political leaders, while the large Masses and events in Lourdes will focus primarily on France's Catholics.

The most keenly awaited speech is one scheduled for the final day in Metz, which French President Emmanuel Macron will introduce. It is titled "To the roots of Europe for peace and unity."

The choice of Metz itself is symbolic. Once a center of the Carolingian Empire, the city has changed language and allegiance several times over the centuries, at times being German, at others French.

While at the Robert Schuman Convention Center, Leo will be able to invoke the name of the man behind the building. Born in Luxembourg in 1886, Robert Schuman was a French statesman who had inherited his father's German citizenship at birth. In the aftermath of World War II, Schuman was an early advocate of Franco-German reconciliation. He pushed for a new, united Europe and in 1958 became the first president of the newly established European Parliament. After his death in 1963, he was buried in a small church in Scy-Chazelles, near Metz.

For Pope Leo, Roy said, "Europe is a real entity that must be sustained and developed beyond nationalism." That, he said, "has always been the message of the Catholic Church since World War II. It is Schuman's message."

Roy will be watching closely to see whether, and how openly, Pope Leo criticizes populism and nationalism in his call to strengthen the EU and whether he will speak about a form of nationalist Catholicism found in France. He said that defenders of the traditional Latin Mass were not infrequently "identitarian nationalists or even close to far-right circles."

It's hard to imagine a papal trip without mention of the long-running issue of abuse. When Pope Benedict visited France in 2008, he did not take the chance to meet any survivors of sexual abuse in the French Catholic Church. His successors did, though the institution has been slow to confront its past amid a spate of new scandals.

This time, a meeting with survivors is on the pope's agenda in Lourdes. But something else has changed. During the pontificates of Benedict and Francis, those who met with them were usually not named. At times, it appeared that access to the Vatican came in exchange for silence. In France, the seven survivors who will meet Leo have spoken out together ahead of the meeting, with five of them also making their names public.

"Dialogue does not mean approval," they said. "Being received does not mean giving up our demands." The issue is likely to remain a thorny challenge for the Catholic Church — in France and elsewhere for a long time to come.
