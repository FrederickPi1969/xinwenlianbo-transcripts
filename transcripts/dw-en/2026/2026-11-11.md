## Democracy study: One-quarter of Germans want a right-wing authoritarian dictatorship

https://p.dw.com/p/5PLY6Prefer DW on GoogleGermany's Constitution, the Basic Law, is the foundation of its free democratic orderImage: Joko/Bildagentur-online/picture alliance AdvertisementSatisfaction with democracy in Germany has hit a new low, while the past is being idealized, and people are expressing a longing for a radical break. The longing for an authoritarian leader and antisemitic sentiments are also on the rise. These are key findings of the authoritarianism study presented Wednesday in Berlin, which has been conducted every two years since 2002.

The study, led by the Else Frenkel-Brunswik Institute for Democracy Research at the University of Leipzig, is based on the answers to a questionnaire sent in by over 900 respondents, who the researchers say are a representative cross-section of the German population.

For the first time since the study series began, approval of democracy as an idea fell just below the 90% mark, which, the study said, is primarily attributable to a loss of trust among respondents in the former East Germany (85.7%). The decline in approval of democracy as experienced in everyday life is even more severe: Nationwide, only one-third are satisfied with democracy, with figures having fallen sharply in both the East (22.8%) and the West (36%).

The growing rejection of democracy is also reflected in the desire for some kind of authoritarian nationalist government: Just under a quarter of respondents explicitly supported the idea of a "single strong party that embodies the national community as a whole." In the east, the figure stands at 30.9%, above the Germany-wide average of 22.3%.

"Our democracy is under great pressure. It has lost acceptance among the general population. It is alarming how much an authoritarian yearning is taking its place," concluded social psychologist and study author Oliver Decker.

Far-right attitudes have risen for the second consecutive year. Of particular note in the study was what the researchers called a chauvinistic national pride, which, with 19.9% approval, has reached its highest level since the study series began. The proportion of people with a consistently far-right worldview has reached 6.9%, the highest level since 2014, the study found.

Just under 15% of respondents said they explicitly believe that "everything has to fall apart so that something new can emerge," while nearly one in three respondents believes that "something radical is needed for the current state of affairs to change." Another 29.1% agree with this to some extent.

According to the study, those who share this yearning for catastrophe exhibit significantly more antisemitic sentiments, both things linked to a preference for certain political parties. The study found that a large proportion of eligible voters with this orientation favor the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD).

"Through its violent rhetoric, the AfD promises to realize the political goals of far-right ideology," the study said. The party's form and content, in other words, correspond exactly to the message that a significant portion of the population wants to hear. "It offers a political home to those who adhere to far-right thinking, while at the same time presenting itself as capable of connecting with a broad segment of society."

Study co-author Johannes Kiess calls on political leaders at all levels to find countermeasures. "The rejection of the present state of the world must be countered with compelling democratic alternatives and the ability to take political action today," he said. This also applies to the significant rise in antisemitism, which has more than doubled since 2022.

When asked about the motives behind antisemitic attitudes, the results show that for 16 percent of respondents, the policies of the Israeli government are the decisive factor. This, too, is a record high in the context of the long-term study. While the figure in 2020 was significantly higher in the east (12%) than in the west (8.4%), the figures have since converged. Antisemitism related to Israel is actually slightly stronger in the West than in the East.

Hostility toward Muslims is also on the rise. It peaked at over 40% in 2018, before dropping to well below 30%, and currently stands at 34.1%. These figures are based on two statements respondents were asked to agree with in each survey: "Muslims should be prohibited from immigrating to Germany" and "because of the many Muslims here, I sometimes feel like a stranger in my own country."

Misogyny is another issue regularly surveyed in the study. In the current survey, 18.3% agreed with anti-feminist statements. Notably, until now, a higher proportion of East Germans had been opposed to feminist causes. As recently as 2024, the difference between the East (34.6%) and the West (19.6%) was enormous. Two years later, the figure in the East has halved to 17.8%, while in western Germany it has fallen only slightly to 18.4%.

Despite the negative trend, there is a glimmer of hope at the end of the Leipzig Authoritarianism Study: While the results are "alarming," long-term trends show that a reversal of this trend is possible: "In earlier surveys, far-right statements already achieved high approval ratings, which then declined again in subsequent years. In addition to the influence of societal crises, political action and its societal perception are of central importance."

## Brazil electoral court fines Meta, sees no deliberate election bias

https://p.dw.com/p/5PL2qPrefer DW on GoogleThe fine was based on Meta's overall handling of the court ordersImage: Thibault Camus/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementBrazil's top electoral court has ordered Meta to pay 7.5 million reais ($1.34 million; €1.22 million) for failing to fully comply with judicial orders during last month's presidential election.

However, the judicial body has so far found no evidence that Meta deliberately acted to influence the election outcome.

Ahead of the runoff vote on October 25, leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva's coalition filed a complaint, accusing Meta of "algorithmic manipulation" favoring his far-right rival Flavio Bolsonaro.

Meta told the court last month that it had implemented measures to guarantee equal treatment for both campaigns.

The Superior Electoral Court (TSE) unanimously ruled on Tuesday to impose the 7.5 million reais fine on the US tech giant.

On the other hand, the court's reporting judge Marco Aurelio Belizze wrote in his ruling that there were no preliminary evidence indicating that any intentional actions were taken to influence the election.

"That ​conclusion would require robust evidence, to be produced through the appropriate legal proceedings," he said.

According to Bellizze, the fine was based on Meta's overall handling of the court orders, including delays, incomplete disclosures, and challenges in verifying some of the information it provided.

"We will continue to present the facts and defend ourselves against false allegations in the ‌ongoing ​legal proceedings," Meta said in a statement on Wednesday.

Lula defeated Bolsonaro in a tight run-off in October 25 with him winning 51.7% of the vote against Bolsonaro's 48.2%.

In his victory speech, Lula urged more regulation of big tech platforms after allegations that Meta interfered in the race.

"We had to overcome foreign interference in the election, coming from outside through digital platforms that we will have to regulate," he said.

## Is AIIB&apos;s complaint mechanism failing affected communities?

https://p.dw.com/p/5PKovPrefer DW on GoogleThe AIIB finances major infrastructure projects across AsiaImage: Noushad Variyattiyakkal/SOPA Images/ZUMA/picture allianceAdvertisementFor members of the Indigenous Sasak community on the Indonesian island of Lombok, the conflict over their land is not over.

For them, a tourism and infrastructure project co-financed by the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB) is a source of frustration, as they believe it has led to the expropriation of their land and destruction of their livelihoods.

In late September, they officially filed a complaint with the bank. It remains unclear whether this complaint will lead to an independent investigation.

The AIIB, which finances major infrastructure projects across Asia, has a mechanism in place to deal with such complaints.

Individuals who suffer adverse effects from the bank-financed projects may, under certain conditions, file a complaint with the institution.

However, access to this process — known as the Project Complaints Mechanism (PPM) — remains controversial.

According to a report by The Diplomat, a magazine that covers political and economic developments in Asia, none of the 45 complaints listed on the AIIB's website had been deemed admissible as of mid-September.

The bank acknowledged that "no cases have passed the PPM's eligibility assessment," but it stressed that this is "only part of the story."

The PPM is "only one part of AIIB's grievance redress ecosystem," the Beijing-based bank said in response to a query from DW.

Those affected should first attempt to resolve concerns directly with the project team at the local level, it said.

According to the bank, "twelve complaints were in fact resolved at the project level — as a direct result of AIIB's follow-up and intervention — and one was resolved through domestic courts."

"The mechanism must be easily accessible," said Arntraud Hartmann, a development economist at the Johns Hopkins University School of Advanced International Studies (SAIS Europe) in Bologna, Italy.

She argued that people affected by the projects, for instance, are required to document potential violations of environmental and social standards from the start. "How is the affected party supposed to know which standards might have been violated?" she questioned.

The AIIB disputes this claim. "In terms of accessibility, we have made the submission form simpler and translated it into 12 languages," it said, adding that complaints can be submitted online, by phone, or by email.

Urgewald, a German environmental NGO, cites another problem with AIIB-financed projects. For many of them, information on environmental impacts and resettlements is either missing or published late, it noted.

For 2024, the NGO found that 11 of the 16 projects it examined lacked complete environmental and social impact assessments. In many cases, information on the potential number of displaced residents was also missing, it added.

"The information published on the bank's website is often insufficient and inconsistent. Particularly important documents, such as environmental impact assessments or resettlement plans, vary widely or are missing entirely," said Nora Sausmikat, a China expert at Urgewald.

The lack of information makes it difficult for those affected to respond in a timely manner and report violations early on, she underscored, adding that the situation is particularly difficult for people without formal land titles.

Christoph Steinert, a political scientist at the University of Konstanz, said the mere existence of such grievance redressal mechanisms "is not enough."

Particularly vulnerable groups often know little about their rights, he said, adding that information should be available in local languages and targeted outreach directed at affected communities.

A 2023 report by international aid organizations, including Recourse, Accountability Counsel, and Inclusive Development International, revealed that the AIIB's grievance mechanism had failed to accept a single complaint since its 2019 inception.

The study highlighted that 101 of the 219 AIIB-financed projects, or 46% of of the total projects at the time, were ineligible for review due to co-financing exemptions.

The AIIB typically provides funding without leading projects, a standard practice in development finance. When complaints arise, it directs complainants to the lead lender. As a result, nine of the 45 complaints fell outside its jurisdiction.

For Sausmikat, this is part of the problem. "The AIIB, however, decided against revising this requirement and established a mechanism that is very difficult to access," she said.

As of January 1, 2026, following two years of external consultations, the AIIB reformed its complaints mechanism. Under certain conditions, complaints can now be filed even while legal proceedings are ongoing.

But Sausmikat stressed that the changes are insufficient. Of the eight key recommendations made by an independent expert, only three were adopted, she underlined. "The revision did not remove the actual obstacles."

The AIIB stated that the reforms are intended to make the mechanism "more accessible and responsive."

The new rules have been in effect for only ten months, and their implementation is being closely monitored, the bank said.

Documented cases from Latin America, for instance, show that complainants have faced administrative or legal problems, and in some cases even violence.

"Requestors may ask for confidentiality, and the revised PPM policy strengthens protection against reprisals," it added.

## Wael Mohya could make German football history at 17

https://p.dw.com/p/5PKmpPrefer DW on GoogleWael Mohya has the chance to make German football history if he plays under Jürgen KloppImage: Marvin Ibo Güngör/GES/picture allianceAdvertisementIf you watch the Bundesliga, then you'll know Wael Mohya is a fast-footed, quick-thinking footballer. But not even the 17-year-old (Mohya turns 18 on December 31) Borussia Mönchengladbach youngster would have expected to be called up to the Germany squad so soon.

"Of course, I was absolutely thrilled. It means a great, great deal to me. I think everyone dreams of being selected for the national team at some point," Mohya said.

"And the fact that it's come to this now is, of course, just awesome and makes me very proud."

Mohya was at the gym when the call came in. He then shared the good news with his parents over dinner. "They started crying," the teenager said in a video posted on Gladbach's social media channels.

The youngster, who was born in a small town east of Düsseldorf, smiles and laughs a lot in interviews. He is always friendly, and in a good mood. On the field, too, he exudes the lightheartedness and carefree spirit of a 17-year-old.

As a technically skilled and creative player, Mohya often seeks out one-on-one situations and puts his opponents under pressure with quick dribbles. In nine Bundesliga appearances this season, Mohya has scored twice and delivered one assist.

"He's so cool and just plays really well. It's fun to watch him," Borussia coach Alexander Blessin said recently.

"Wael brings a lot to the team with his laid-back attitude on the field. He's not afraid to take risks, no matter how much pressure there is," added Gladbach's sporting director Rouven Schröder at a talk show on Sport1.

Borussia Mönchengladbach has been Mohya's sporting home since he was seven years old. Last season, previous head coach Eugen Polanski promoted him to the first team, and in December 2025, Mohya made his Bundesliga debut. Against Wolfsburg, aged 16 years and 347 days old, he became the youngest Bundesliga player in the club's history.

Mohya also recently completed a voluntary social year at an elementary school in Mönchengladbach. This is a program in Germany for young people who volunteer in the social sector — at hospitals, schools, or nursing homes, for example — for about six to 18 months. They gain work experience, help others, and earn a little money.

At the school's open all-day care program, Mohya helped serve meals, assisted children with their homework, and played with them. That's also where his nickname comes from. Because a girl hadn't understood his first name correctly or couldn't pronounce it, she called him "Waffel." Now, that name has stuck.

Even before being called up to the Germany team, clubs in the Bundesliga and across Europe's top leagues had taken notice of Mohya and were trying to sign him. His contract with Borussia Mönchengladbach runs through 2028.

With Gladbach currently in a relegation battle, his future appears likely to be elsewhere. If the club wants to generate transfer revenue from its biggest talent, next summer would likely be the opportunity to sell him — unless he extends his contract with Borussia.

The 17-year-old's career is managed by his parents. Whichever club can convince them that their son is in good hands and help him reach his full potential likely has the best chance of securing Mohya's signing.

"My parents take care of all that," he told Bild recently. "I get a monthly allowance, which is more than enough for me."

Germany head coach Jürgen Klopp recently revealed how much he had enjoyed his conversations with the youngster.

"The conversations I had with him were great," Klopp said with a laugh. "I wasn't that confident at 17, but then again, I wasn't that good either."

Since Mohya's parents are from Morocco, their son also holds Moroccan citizenship. As a result, he could have played for Morocco, but he has now decided against it. In fact, the Moroccan football association had been actively courting Mohya as early as last year and had already reached out to him in the run-up to the 2026 World Cup.

Now, like Frankfurt's Younes Ebnoutalib, Mohya will continue to play in the German jersey, unlike Schalke's Soufiane El-Faouzi, who chose Morocco, or Bayer Leverkusen's Ibrahim Maza, who likely would have been selected by Klopp as well if he hadn't already opted for Algeria.

For Mohya, who played for Germany's youth teams from the U16s through to the U21s, the door to Morocco remains open only as long as he hasn't yet played a competitive match for Germany. If Mohya plays in the upcoming UEFA Nations League games in Serbia or against the Netherlands, another record is on the horizon. Although there were two even younger players who made their debuts in the Germany jersey — Willy Baumgärtner in 1908 (at 17 years and 104 days) and Marius Hiller in 1910 (at 17 years and 241 days) — the era in which they debuted is hardly comparable. And so, while Wael Mohya would rank third overall on the list, he would become the youngest Germany player in the modern era. The youngest to date was Hamburg legend Uwe Seeler in 1954, aged 17 years and 345 days.

## Tundu Lissu: After escaping the death penalty, what does his guilty verdict mean for Tanzania&apos;s democracy?

https://p.dw.com/p/5PHqyPrefer DW on GoogleOpposition figure Tundu Lissu originally faced the death penalty for treasonImage: ChademaTZ2/X AdvertisementOn November 6, Tundu Lissu, leader of Tanzania's main opposition Chadema party, was sentenced to four years in prison for incitement following heavy unrest during the October 2025 elections.

The same day, he was cleared of treason charges — which could have meant a death sentence if he had been found guilty.

Lissu commented that the judiciary had acted under pressure; he rejected the verdict due to the fact that incitement was not among the charges which had brought against him at the start of his trial in 2025.

Chadema officials have now signaled an intention to appeal the court decision. The government has not issued a statement on his conviction yet.

Few opposition figures have shaped modern Tanzanian politics as dramatically as Lissu. A lawyer by training and one of the country's most outspoken critics of the ruling party Chama Cha Mapinduzi (CCM), Lissu has spent years challenging the political establishment.

Over the years, he has been subjected to repeated arrest and detention; in 2017, he survived an attempt on his life by unknown gunmen outside his residence in the capital Dodoma, sustaining multiple bullet wounds.

More recently, his trial in the run-up to the October 2025 general election continued to keep him and the opposition in the public eye following his arrest in April 2025 in the southwestern town of Mbinga.

Prosecuters accused Lissu of encouraging the public to prevent the 2025 election from taking place and charged him with one count of treason.

As legal evidence, they cited social media posts he had made, in which he was seen to be calling on Tanzanians to boycott the vote over concerns about possible electoral malpractice.

In the end, the election went ahead without Chadema on October 29, which was marred by violence on an unprecedented in scale in Tanzania; security forces even used live ammunition on protesters.

Francis Ibrahim, an independent political analyst based in Dar es Salaam, told DW that "heads have to roll from the law enforcement side, who overstretched their mandate, all the way to the people, who financed, planned and organized [this crackdown]."

But after a nationwide internet shutdown and curfew following the election violence, incumbent President Samia Suluhu Hassan was named winner with 98% of the vote.

Ibrahim believes that the removal of the capital treason charge could be interpreted as a move towards rapprochment with voters.

"What I can tell you is that they are looking for a political deescalation that could solve the crisis," he explained, adding that Lissu might yet get to have a small say in politics after all:

"There has to be a process of [creating] a new constitution, and it needs everybody on the table," he said, adding that Lissu might therefore perhaps be released early in order to partake in discussions and negotiations.

However when in late July, President Suluhu Hassan met with representatives of more than a dozen political parties to discuss national reconciliation and constitutional reform, Chadema officials were explicitly not party to the consultations.

Public opinion meanwhile varies on Lissu's verdict, with many Tanzanians however saying that the trial was politically motivated in their view.

Ruqaya Mohammed, the National Deputy Secretary of the oppsition ACT-Wazalendo party's youth wing, told DW, "ee are getting into a darker space in our political arena."

"I think it's the right time now, as a nation, to have open dialogue on some reforms, on the legal perspective and electoral reforms. Maybe if we manage ... that, we can see a light," she explained.

Most others, however, remain reluctant to go on record for fear of reprisal; a musician and human rights advocate was among few who agreed to speak to DW — on the strict condition of anonymity.

In his view, Tanzanians simply are not free to express themselves publicly at all: "You saw the terror atmosphere and climate of fear outside the court premises when Tundu's judgement was ... read. Such is the broader display of what Tanzania looks like," he told DW.

"Opposition politics has been suppressed for years, and his conviction is just a continuation of what has been happening. It's a miracle that he is still alive. To put him in jail from four years is far from the worst-case scenario."

## DW Science explainer on the default mode network: what your brain does when you aren&apos;t focusing on anything, and why it&apos;s important.

https://p.dw.com/p/5PHvPPrefer DW on GoogleThe average human brain consumes about 20% of the body's energy despite making up only around 2% of its weight. Interestingly, that stays roughly stable whether you are resting or busy with a complex task.Image: Irina Romanova/Zoonar/picture allianceAdvertisementYou know when you lock your phone and the screen goes black? It seems like it's not doing anything. But it's not doing nothing. It's syncing your messages, updating apps and running other background processes.

Imagine you're sitting on a bus, staring out of a window, taking a shower or walking, and you get that feeling of doing nothing and letting your mind drift?

It's going over your memories, shaping your sense of who you are, imagining possible futures, helping you understand other people and connecting ideas.

Scientists conducted brain-imaging experiments in the 1990s and early 2000s to find out which regions of the brain were active during demanding tasks, like solving a math problem or memory exercise.

But to measure that, they needed a so-called baseline. They needed to know the level of brain activity at rest and during demanding tasks.

So, between the tasks, they asked participants to relax, close their eyes, stare at a fixed point on a wall, or try not to think of anything at all.

And they measured the participants' brain activity both when they were solving a task and doing nothing. That's how scientists noticed the brain was active in up- and downtime.

They noticed that when participants started to focus on a new task, after having done nothing, some brain areas lit up and other areas dimmed down. That suggests those areas that dimmed down must have been active while the participant was apparently — physically and mentally — at rest.

It helps you connect and revisit memories, reflect on who you are, imagine what might happen in future scenarios, understand other people, build meaning from experience, and connect ideas in new ways.

It turns all your memories, experiences, knowledge, emotions and social information into your internal story. Think of that story as a constantly evolving, updated model of who you are and how you engage with the world around you.

But it's important to understand the DMN involves more than one area of the brain. It is a group of brain regions that light up when you're not focused on an external task.

There's another player in your brain that acts as a monitoring system. You can think of it as constantly asking "Is there anything that needs my attention?"

In a fraction of a second, your daydream disappears and your attention snaps back to the road. That player is called the salience network, or SN.

The salience network helps you notice important signals, like a sound, a mistake, pain, an emotion, a threat, or something happening in your body, and helps shift the brain toward attention and control systems when action is needed.

And the DMN doesn't just turn off completely with every hard task. Parts of it can also contribute when tasks involve memory, stories, meaning or other people.

Smartphones have filled many of the small empty moments of our days — waiting for the bus, standing in line, eating alone, lying on the couch. We are constantly looking at our devices.

So, it seems reasonable to ask: are we crowding out those moments of boredom where DMN kicks in?

Maybe. But so far, there's no strong evidence that scrolling directly affects mental health by reducing DMN time.

What we do know is that problematic smartphone use can increase stress, anxiety, task-switching and disrupt sleep.

And sleep loss and stress also have clearer evidence for altering DMN-related brain connectivity.

In depression, the self-reflection part of the DMN can get caught in repetitive negative loops that are hard to stop — what's known as rumination.

Scientists have also found that Alzheimer's disease targets key DMN areas, particularly those responsible for your autobiographical memory and remembering other aspects of the past. Which may help explain why memory problems are so central to the disease.

Protect your sleep and take stress seriously. There is direct evidence that sleep loss and stress disrupt DMN-related connectivity.

Take real, quiet breaks after mentally demanding tasks, like studying. It can even help you memorize facts for your exam.

Try to reframe boredom as this essential, energy-intensive active work in your brain that builds your internal narrative and sense of self, consolidates memories, creates connections and sparks creativity.

Try waiting in line without checking your phone. Look out the window next time you take the bus or take a walk without listening to anything.

## How AI worsens online violence against Middle East women

https://p.dw.com/p/5PIvSPrefer DW on GoogleIn Syria, young women's rights activists say their private pictures are being stolen from social media, then used against themImage: picture-alliance/dpaAdvertisementIt happens all the time, says Kholoud al-Ameri, an Iraqi journalist and the editor of a news website.

"Sometimes it's hard because the men say they're our readers and we don't want to discourage them. But I usually tell the women just to block these guys."

Syrian activist Yaman Amer tells of similar experiences as a result of writing about women's rights online — she says she gets hundreds of critical comments. "When I talk about women's rights online in a patriarchal society, that's just how things are," Amer, who is studying to be a lawyer in Damascus, told DW. "Not that I accept it. And then it's more than just harassment. It's on a whole other level, humiliating and degrading."

Online harassment of women isn't unusual anywhere. Studies by a variety of organizations show that somewhere between 40% and 60% of all women worldwide have experienced it, while 65% have witnessed it.

There is no single standard for assessing the level of online harassment because surveys often use different types of questions or research methods. But in general, women around the Middle East appear to be dealing with a higher-than-global-average level of what is known as "technology facilitated gender-based violence," or TFGBV.

The United Nations Population Fund defines TFGBV as a harmful act "that is committed, assisted, aggravated or amplified ... by the use of information communication technologies," or other digital tools, "against a person on the basis of their gender."

According to research by UN Women, the UN body dedicated to gender equality, 68% of women in the Middle East have had to deal with it, while almost all — close to 100% — have seen it online.

In a 2025 study, UN Women found that 49% of women using the internet in the Middle East don't feel safe from online harassment. The number goes up to almost 70% if the women are more publicly visible activists, journalists or politicians.

And it is already clear that the increased availability of artificial intelligence-powered applications is making online harassment worse.

AI has the potential to supercharge this kind of abuse, the UN Women briefing points out, "both by facilitating the spread of targeted disinformation as well as the proliferation of image-based abuse and deepfake pornographic videos."

Of the women who had experienced online violence while working, around a quarter had already identified "abuse that was generated or amplified by AI tools," the briefing said.

The potential for using the latest technology in order to harass people online has always been there, says Siddharth Venkataramakrishnan, an analyst at the London-based Institute for Strategic Dialogue (ISD), which investigates extremism. "But it's just become easier to produce harmful content at scale and in a hyper-targeted way. And the production of non-consensual imagery is a real marker of the democratization of that technology, so it's moving away from primarily targeting celebrities to just regular people."

Venkataramakrishnan has been researching incidents that occurred at the beginning of 2026 when Grok, the AI-powered chatbot belonging to Elon Musk's company SpaceXAI, became able to alter photographs to depict a fully dressed subject as nearly naked. The scandal caused Grok's services to be restricted but, as Venkataramakrishnan pointed out, safeguards across different AI models tend to be inconsistent and users can still find work-arounds to get similar results.

In conservative cultures in the Middle East, the threat of this kind of half-naked picture of a woman being published has serious real-life implications, mostly because of what is and isn't acceptable for women around the region.

What makes the increase in AI-generated fakes even more problematic in the Middle East is that online abuse also has a higher chance of having offline effects.

"Norms related to honor, family reputation and male control can turn digital abuse into real-world consequences," a June briefing by the World Bank notes.

It's also often not easy for people targeted with this kind of abuse to find legal protection or even somewhere to report it. While some Middle Eastern countries have cybercrime laws, most don't specifically address gender-based online violence nor have they been updated to grapple with AI-related abuse.

Last year's UN Women survey found that 36% of women said they were just told to ignore the online violence, while 23% were blamed for it themselves and 12% were subjected to physical punishment by their families because of it.

Other studies indicate that only about 7% of women who experienced technology-facilitated violence later report it to law enforcement. Various concerns stop them from doing so, including having to present sexualized images or answer personal questions in front of strangers — often also anathema to many women in the region.

The women who had experienced online harassment said they would often just block the perpetrators and around a quarter said they then became more careful about what they posted online. Twenty percent deleted their accounts or left social media altogether.

The rapid growth in AI-enabled harassment can also be more problematic in the Middle East because of the situations the women targeted find themselves living in.

Displacement, war, conflict and instability can all make women more vulnerable to online abuse and exploitation. Even the rapid development of AI-related technologies in a country without corresponding legal guardrails or digital literacy can increase the risk.

For example, observers point out that Saudi Arabia, a generally conservative country with strict rules for female behavior, is going through an accelerated program of digital transformation.

And in Syria, after the ousting of the dictatorial Assad regime, many Syrians "rushed into social media to speak freely," journalist Zaina Erhaim wrote for the Arab Reform Initiative in July this year.

Previously. digital polices had been closely policed by Syria's dictator and a political post could see the writer thrown in jail. But now Syrians "are navigating this newfound liberty without guardrails, oversight or protection," Erhaim argues, and female activists are paying a high price for it, especially where "gender intersects with sect."

In a policy paper published in May, the Syrian Women's Political Movement explained just how bad online abuse had become. In focus groups with 120 women from all around Syria, the majority of participants agreed that digital abuse had become a way of shutting them up. Younger activists reported personal pictures were being stolen from social media, then used against them.

"Smear campaigns targeting women's 'honor' and systemic defamation are actively deployed as punitive measures against any women who attempt to express a critical political opinion," the organization wrote, arguing that what was needed was a law that criminalized digital violence.
