## Indonesian VIP prison scandal exposes inequality across Southeast Asia

https://p.dw.com/p/5OF5DSofas, a flatscreen TV and a kitchen were some of the luxuries uncoveredImage: Tatan Syuflana/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementAir-conditioned apartments, flat-screen televisions and even a golf simulator are hardly what most people would associate with life behind bars. Yet these luxuries were among the facilities uncovered last month at Indonesia's Cibinong Prison, near Jakarta, during a surprise inspection by the country's Ombudsman.

Investigators discovered more than 10 apartment-style units equipped with refrigerators, sofas and private living areas. A gym was also reportedly under construction.

The revelations prompted an investigation into whether inmates had paid for preferential treatment.

Five prison officials were initially suspended, while the prison's warden was dismissed on October 5. Authorities demolished 10 unauthorized buildings and took further disciplinary action against prison staff.

It wasn't Indonesia's first luxury prison scandal. In 2018, investigators from the Corruption Eradication Commission discovered that wealthy inmates at Sukamiskin Prison in Bandung enjoyed accommodation resembling private apartments.

Similar controversies have emerged elsewhere in Southeast Asia. Last November, authorities in Thailand investigated allegations that Chinese prisoners at Bangkok Remand Prison received special privileges, including access to an executive lounge.

In Malaysia, allegations of preferential treatment for politically connected prisoners have repeatedly surfaced. The latest Indonesian scandal prompted renewed calls for independent inspections of Malaysian prisons.

"Just like in the outside world, money and influence act as capital within the prison system. Powerful inmates generally do not lose their wealth and often maintain strong connections with influential people," Iwa Maulana, a senior researcher at Indonesia's Center for Detention Studies (CDS), told DW.

"When certain inmates receive different treatment based on their resources and influence, it demonstrates that, much like in the outside world, a person's identity affects how they are treated, even within prison walls," he added.

Indonesia's detention facilities held approximately 279,000 people in late September, despite having an official capacity of around 154,000, according to government data.

Thailand's prisons are also severely overcrowded. At the end of 2025, they held approximately 301,000 inmates, around 21% above official capacity, according to the World Prison Brief.

Corruption within prison administrations also reflects wider governance problems, with Southeast Asian states routinely appearing at the bottom of Transparency International's annual Corruption Perceptions Index.

Where bribery and patronage already influence public services, prison officials may face similar incentives to exchange preferential treatment for money or favors.

"Corruption and political interference may explain particular cases," Nasrul Ismail, senior lecturer in criminology at the University of Bristol, who recently conducted a major study of prison governance in Malaysia, Thailand and the Philippines, told DW.

"When decisions are made behind closed doors, with limited transparency about who receives what and why, there are opportunities for personal connections and financial resources to influence treatment," Ismail said.

However, he argues the problem extends beyond a relatively small number of wealthy inmates enjoying extraordinary privileges.

Across Southeast Asia, many prisoners depend on financial support from their families for everyday necessities that prison administrations do not provide.

Ismail said poorer inmates often rely on money deposited by relatives into prison canteen accounts to purchase food, clean water and hygiene products.

"This is distinct from wealthy prisoners receiving luxury accommodation, but both illustrate how broader inequalities can be reproduced within prisons through different institutional arrangements. Where minimum standards are not consistently guaranteed, access to decent conditions becomes dependent on the resources and connections prisoners possess," Ismail told DW.

"The fundamental issue is not simply whether wealthy prisoners receive special privileges, but why access to basic welfare and humane treatment can be so unequal in the first place."

Repeated scandals suggest that dismissing officials and demolishing unauthorized facilities achieve little without broader institutional reforms.

Independent inspections are one possible solution. The UN's Nelson Mandela Rules call for regular internal inspections and external monitoring by bodies independent of prison administrations.

Greater transparency over inmate accommodation, transfers, medical treatment and disciplinary decisions would also make preferential treatment harder to conceal.

Reducing overcrowding, improving staff training and protecting whistleblowers could further limit opportunities for corruption.

But CDS researcher Maulana believes meaningful reform requires a more fundamental change in how criminal justice systems operate.

"The current system primarily treats imprisonment as the main form of punishment, leading law enforcement to frequently resort to incarceration for all types of legal violations," which leads to overcrowding and creates opportunities for corruption.

"It is essential to intensify efforts to implement non-custodial penalties and alternatives to detention," Maulana said.

Although prison authorities cannot eliminate the social inequalities outside their walls, Ismail argues that Southeast Asian governments have previously shown a "considerable capacity" to respond to emergencies inside prisons.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, governments quickly contained outbreaks in detention facilities and prevented infections from spreading into the wider population. Yet such interventions rarely translate into lasting improvements in prisoners' living conditions.

Ismail describes this as "selective state capacity," whereby "governments demonstrated considerable ability to act when prison outbreaks threatened public health beyond prison walls, yet failed to sustain comparable improvements in prisoners' everyday welfare," he said.

## Trump&apos;s diesel deal with Russia is unlikely to significantly lower global fuel prices despite expectations of rapid relief

https://p.dw.com/p/5OF6HDiesel supplies are under pressure and analysts are skeptical Trump's diesel deal with Russia will lower pricesImage: Patrick T. Fallon/AFPAdvertisementWith or without a deal with Russia, diesel prices on global markets are unlikely to fall significantly anytime soon.

That is the assessment of experts interviewed by DW regarding the latest fuel-supply agreement between US President Donald Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin.

"The main reason for high diesel prices is the war in Iran and the simultaneous disruption of shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab al-Mandeb Strait," said Samina Sultan, a foreign trade expert at the German Economic Institute (IW) in Cologne.

The situation has led to production outages and made fuel shipments "more expensive, less secure and more difficult," she said.

Trump's deal with Russia also has an additional geopolitical dimension, Sultan said: "It causes the wars in Ukraine and Iran to become completely intertwined."

Things are looking up, though. Roughly 13.5 million barrels of crude oil are once again flowing through the Strait of Hormuz a day, according to shipping and commodities analytics firm Kpler.

But for refined products such as diesel, volumes remain far lower at around 677,000 barrels per day, compared with 3.6 million barrels before the war.

Carsten Fritsch, a commodities analyst at Commerzbank, also attributes the price increases to the wars.

"If nothing changes in Ukraine and the Middle East, I see very few ways to resolve these supply bottlenecks," he said.

In a social media post on October 9, Trump announced that he would temporarily suspend some sanctions on Russia and begin importing Russian diesel.

He wrote "diesel prices for Americans and, indeed, the world, will be COMING DOWN, IN RECORD NUMBERS, AND FAST!" in a mix of lowercase and all caps.

Russia confirmed the arrangement. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov told Russian state news agency TASS on October 13 that supplying diesel fuel was "in the interests of Russia, the United States and global energy markets."

According to the US Energy Information Administration (EIA), prices in the US have climbed by about 60% and now stand at $6.30 per gallon, equivalent to $1.66 (or €1.49) per liter of diesel.

But even if Russia were able to flood global markets with cheap diesel, prices would not fall nearly as much as they recently rose, something all of the experts contacted by DW agree on.

Many refineries around the Gulf region have suffered disruptions or damage, while transporting fuel via shadow fleets and securing insurance remains costly, cumbersome and risky.

Another key shift is that Russia is no longer the world's largest diesel exporter. In fact, it is facing its own fuel shortages at home.

The US is now the world's biggest diesel exporter. In 2025, it exported an average of 1.26 million barrels of diesel per day, accounting for roughly 15.4% of global diesel exports.

At the same time, Russia fell to second place with a 9.5% share, and that was before major Ukrainian drone attacks on its refineries.

Besides the new US-Russia diesel agreement, Trump also recently floated the idea of US diesel export restrictions. But the idea was abandoned because it could lead to even higher diesel prices in the country, according to Samina Sultan.

"There simply is not enough diesel storage capacity in the United States," Sultan said. "Production would have to be reduced, and then prices would rise again."

If the US were to impose export restrictions on diesel, buyers in Latin America and the European Union would be hit particularly hard. Since Russia invaded Ukraine, EU countries have increasingly imported diesel from the US instead of Russia because of sanctions.

"An export ban merely redistributes the shortage; it does not solve it," said ING chief economist Carsten Brzeski.

"Global prices would continue to rise, while refineries would scale back production as margins shrink," he added.

Export restrictions aside, few people consider that the US-Russia diesel deal will have any big impact.

According to calculations by the platform The World Data, the promised shipment of 2.2 million barrels of diesel from Russia would not even cover a single day's US diesel demand of roughly 4 million barrels.

Even if all of the diesel volumes announced by Trump were combined, totaling 35.8 million barrels, they would only cover about nine days of US domestic demand.

"I CANNOT OVERSTATE HOW MUCH OF A NOTHING BURGER THIS IS," Canadian oil market analyst Rory Johnston wrote in an all-caps post on X shortly after the announcement.

Russia would normally export almost 10 times that volume if its refinery fleet was not being targeted by Ukrainian drone attacks, he added.

Canada also supplies diesel to the United States. Roughly 80% of all US diesel imports come from its northern neighbor.

## China&apos;s influence in the gaming industry is growing

https://p.dw.com/p/5NVcBThe Chinese game 'The Defiant' is set during the Second Sino-Japanese WarImage: 4DivinityAdvertisementIn a dark underground hideout, dim light spills from a handful of oil lamps. A comrade fiddles with a rifle. A red flag hangs on one of the roughly hewn stone walls, bearing a hammer and sickle on a white star — the symbol of the ChineseWorkers' and Peasants' Red Army. Weapons are scattered on the ground. One is picked up, and soon the action moves into the snowy forest, where gunfire erupts among the trees.

Such a scene could fit in a new installment of "Call of Duty," the popular first person shooter game that transports players to battlefields past, present and future. But it is actually from "The Defiant." The game is currently being developed by the Chinese studio Hoothanes Technology in Hangzhou. It tells the story of Chinese forces fending off the Japanese army in the 1930s and 1940s.

Developer Connor Xu tells DW the weapons featured in the game are unique. They were used only in Asia and are therefore unfamiliar to many Western players, he says. The same goes for the story, which Xu says has never before been told in a video game.

"The Defiant" is also designed to appeal to an international audience. In just a few years, China has emerged as a major player in the global gaming industry despite strict regulations, censorship and a former ban on video game consoles.

More than 700 million people in China regularly play video games. According to market research company Newzoo, most consider gaming an important part of their lives. Many use games to stay in touch with friends.

"Games make life more interesting," developer Xu says. "I can try different roles, different stories, different characters, because we only have one life to live, but we can play a role in another story. That's really attractive, I think, to Chinese users."

People in China primarily play games on PCs and smartphones, while consoles are much less common. There are historical reasons for that: While gamers in Europe and North America debated whether PlayStation or Xbox was the better console in the early 2000s, the devices were not officially sold in China between 2000 and 2014. Chinese authorities said the restrictions were intended to protect children and teenagers from becoming addicted to gaming.

Minors in China are currently allowed to play online games for no more than three hours a week. There are no such limits for adults, but gaming is subject to strict controls. Anyone who wants to play must register using their real name and a personal identification number. That effectively allows the state to track who plays what and when, as well as whom they communicate with.

There is also a gray area that the Chinese government appears to tolerate. Some players log in using VPNs, giving them access to games that are not officially available in China. Some younger players use their parents' accounts to get around the limits on gaming time.

Despite its strict controls on underage gamers, China has become increasingly receptive to video games in general, having recognized the industry's enormous economic potential. Early on, games released for Chinese players often appeared to be copies of Western titles. At the same time, Chinese tech companies such as Tencent and NetEase invested in numerous Western gaming companies.

One distinctive feature of the Chinese market is its licensing system. "Unlike a lot of the other big video games markets in the world, China has a tightly regulated and licensed official video games market," George E. Osborn tells DW. The journalist advises NGOs and governments on video games and spoke at the Gamescom congress. In his book "Power Play," he describes how authoritarian states use video games to pursue political objectives.

The rule for all games in China is simple: No license, no sales. "That system essentially forces developers who want to release their video games to go through multiple layers of approval from China's Ministry of Propaganda, Ministry of Culture," Osborn says.

Games are prohibited from containing material that threatens China's unity, sovereignty or territorial integrity, among other restrictions. Pornographic content and gambling are also banned. Games may not incite crimes that threaten national security or spread information authorities believe could disrupt public order.

"The whole point around this criteria is that it's deliberately designed to be pretty vague," Osborn says. Whether a game violates the rules is a matter of interpretation. Sometimes games are altered even after release. In the 2025 action role-playing game "Wuchang: Fallen Feathers," for example, enemies based on famous Chinese figures from the Ming Dynasty suddenly could no longer be killed. Instead, they would collapse from exhaustion.

The Chinese action role-playing game "Black Myth: Wukong" became an unexpected international hit in 2024. By then, it was clear Chinese developers could do more than cater to the domestic market — they could create successful games for a global audience as well. The game was inspired by the 16th-century Chinese literary classic "Journey to the West."

Osborn sees nothing wrong with bringing Chinese culture to audiences around the world. The question, he says, is how much the state actually intervenes in the games themselves. "I think in general, video game players are actually quite deeply skeptical of these kinds of things. They are generally very good at spotting when someone might be selling to them or someone might be propagandizing. And so I think if it's too obvious, it won't work," he says.

Because of the licensing system, Western developers also have to comply with government requirements if they want to sell their games in China. And they do want to, Osborn says, because the market is too big to ignore. "But I think when you start thinking about the potential impact this has on creative standards around the world, I think it's really important that developers ask themselves the question of what will we or won't we compromise on here?"

He fears the real danger lies in self-censorship. That could lead developers, for example, to leave queer characters out of their games. That would represent a major step backward, he says, because games help many people explore their identities.

China is not the only country where games are modified to comply with national regulations. In Germany, for example, symbols that violate the country's constitution, such as the swastika, may not be shown in games unless the game in question explicitly contributes to the country's culture of remembrance. Depictions of extreme violence also have to be removed from German versions.

Glorifying violence is prohibited in China, too, yet Xu's team is still allowed to develop a war game. How does this add up? Xu explains it this way: "I think in this game, the most important thing is not violence. The most important thing is to tell history."

Xu says the team is committed to delivering a historically accurate product. Whether it succeeds will be for historians to judge.

## Nord Stream: Germany puts suspected Ukrainian pipeline saboteur on trial

https://p.dw.com/p/5OByzMassive quantities of methane gas bubbled on the surface of the Baltic Sea after the explosion of the Nord Stream pipelinesImage: Danish Defence Command/ABACAPRESS/IMAGOAdvertisementThe aerial photos made headlines worldwide: On September 26, 2022, massive quantities of methane gas bubbled on the surface of the Baltic Sea near the island of Bornholm. Unknown people had blown up three of the four sections of the Nord Stream 1 and 2 gas pipelines from Russia to Germany.

The attack, which occurred seven months after Moscow launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine, meant the end of a mega energy project between Germany and Russia in which both sides had invested billions, and which secured affordable Russian gas for Germany. However, the flow of gas via Nord Stream 1 had been restricted or stopped several times by Russia since the Ukraine war began; Nord Stream 2 was not yet operational.

From October 14, Ukrainian citizen Serhii K. is facing trial before a court in the northern German city of Hamburg. He was arrested in Italy in August 2025 on a European arrest warrant and later extradited to Germany. This week he must answer for the attacks before the Hanseatic Higher Regional Court. He is accused of being an accomplice to a war crime by attacking civil energy infrastructure, disruption of public services, causing an explosion and destroying structures.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has repeatedly said that Ukraine was not involved in the 2022 attack on the Nord Stream underwater gas pipelines.

"Ukraine has never officially taken part in any operation related to the blowing up of Nord Stream," Zelenskyy told reporters again in August in response to a question about the ongoing investigation.

The fact that the accused is Ukrainian is especially politically sensitive. Germany continues to support the country after it was attacked by Russia: militarily, financially, with humanitarian aid and by taking in about 1.3 million refugees.

Critics of Germany's support for Ukraine, especially the right-wing extremist Alternative for Germany (AfD) and the left-wing populist Alliance for Social Justice and Economic Reason (BSW) want to improve relations with Moscow, resume energy deliveries from Russia – including via a repaired Nord Stream pipeline — and stop supporting Ukraine. Some of them have practically delivered their verdict on Serhii K., although the trial has not yet begun, and assume that Ukraine's political or military leadership was behind the attack on the pipeline.

Sahra Wagenknecht, former leader of the BSW, told news agency Reuters in 2025 following Serhii K.'s arrest: "It is totally absurd that Germany spends many billions on aid for Ukraine but never demands an explanation from [President Volodymyr] Zelenskyy. Questions should also be raised about possible compensation."

Alice Weidel, co-leader of the AfD, has shared similar sentiments. In early 2026, she asserted that the German government must seek financial repayment from Ukraine. If the AfD came into power, she would demand Kyiv compensate Germany for blowing up the pipeline and pay for its repair. According to her, a country that does such things is "no friend of Germany." She has, however, failed to provide proof that Ukraine was behind the attack.

## Fact check: Misleading graph about alleged Russian bot activity

https://p.dw.com/p/5OD3xThe graph shows significant inconsistencies, and its data source is unclearImage: x.com/tomaszdechovskyAdvertisementAfter Ukrainian drone attacks on data centers operated by the Russian technology company Yandex, claims began circulating online that the strikes had disrupted or even halted the activity of Russian propaganda bots. These accounts are often accused of spreading disinformation or artificially amplifying content on social media.

In response to BBC Verify, Zelenskyy's office said that, rather than referring to reduced bot activity, the president was "talking about the atmosphere on social media." The office added that it had seen "reports of fewer provocative and hateful comments" from many social media users.

So far, however, there is no clear evidence linking the attacks to a decline in bot activity.

According to Josef Holnburger, managing director of the Center for Monitoring, Analysis and Strategy (CeMAS), there is currently "no decline in Russian disinformation activities that could be explained by the attack on Yandex data centers." CeMAS systematically monitors such campaigns.

Nevertheless, a misleading graph has been circulated as supposed evidence. It was shared, for example, by Czech Member of the European Parliament Tomáš Zdechovský. His post was viewed more than four million times on X. He suggested that the graph might demonstrate a decline in "online activity around AfD," although he did not specify what kind of activity he meant. In reality, the graph most likely demonstrates nothing at all.

The German-language graph is titled "Daily activity over 3 months." It appears to show activity remaining relatively stable before dropping sharply in early October, roughly coinciding with the attacks on Yandex data centers. However, nothing in the graph itself indicates that this relates to AfD-related online activity.

The same applies to the graph's underlying data. It is unclear which data were used. No source is provided, nor is there any explanation of the data collection process or calculation method for the alleged "activity," which initially ranges between 6,000 and 7,000 before falling below 3,000.

Particularly striking is that the graph extends to October 15. Its final data point appears to correspond to October 14 – a date that was still in the future when the graph first appeared online on October 11. Data for a future date cannot exist.

The graph also contains scaling errors on both the X and Y axes. For example, on the Y axis, the distance between 2,500 and 3,000 is smaller than the other 500-unit intervals. Because the Y-axis does not begin at zero, the decline is visually exaggerated. On the X-axis, equal periods of time are represented by unequal distances.

It is also unclear which platforms were analyzed and how the variable "activity" was calculated. The absence of a known data source, the inaccurate scaling, and the inclusion of "future" values strongly suggest that this is not a meaningful graph based on factual data and sound statistical methods.

It is unclear whether AI was used to create the graph. Given the errors and the apparent "hallucination" of presenting future data, AI involvement cannot be ruled out.

Misleading and error-filled graphs, some of which may have been AI-generated, appear regularly.  German public broadcaster BR's #Faktenfuchs team recently reported on one such graph that falsely depicted industrial production in Germany (article in German). False or fabricated graphs can lend credibility to unsupported claims.

Cloudflare data are also frequently cited in connection with claims of declining bot activity. However, according to CeMAS managing director Holnburger, there is a misunderstanding about what Cloudflare's report actually means.

Cloudflare provides services to many websites that block traffic assessed as non-human. Automated web traffic is common and includes, for example, crawlers that check websites for freshly published content. Search engines, including Yandex, rely on these crawlers. Other bots may be used for spam in comment sections.

In a message to #Faktenfuchs, Holnburger writes, "a decline in bot activity on websites is something entirely different from a decline in inauthentic behavior by social bots on social networks."

Referring to Cloudflare data, he added: "When looking at overall activity from Russian IP addresses on servers using Cloudflare services, there is no major decline in bot activity." Furthermore, there is no reason to assume that the infrastructure used by disinformation-related social bots would be hosted on Yandex data centers. "It is more likely that these systems are distributed across many different providers," Holnburger writes.

## Do non-German nationals commit more crimes than Germans?

In a new research paper, Susann Prätor, a sociologist, criminologist and professor at the Lower Saxony Police Academy, has taken a very close look at crime data in Germany, together with her colleagues from the University of Zurich and the Lower Saxony Criminological Research Institute.

She looked not only at officially reported crime — that is, all offenses that appeared in police crime statistics — but also at unreported crime: These are all offenses that are not reported or detected, and therefore do not appear in any official statistics but are identified through representative surveys.

"According to police crime statistics, people who do not have German citizenship commit crimes more frequently than German citizens. However, studies into unreported cases show that non-German nationals are twice as likely to be reported. Differences between the two groups are also evident in crimes that go unreported, but they are significantly less pronounced," Prätor told DW.

Crimes committed by foreign nationals are therefore twice as likely to appear in police crime statistics as crimes committed by Germans.

These statistics record all reported cases and do not specify whether the allegations prove to be true.

When the statistics are adjusted for the bias in the reporting of crimes, foreigners are no longer 2.6 times more likely to commit crimes than Germans, but only 1.7 times more likely. This figure is also consistent with the results of studies on unreported juvenile violent crime. According to these studies, foreign youths are about 1.6 times as likely to commit violent crimes as German youths.

Other factors affecting crime include age and gender. Young men make up a larger proportion of the foreign-born population, and young men are the group most prone to criminal behavior across all societies.

According to Prätor, "categories such as being young, male, poorly educated and having a history of violence are factors that increase the likelihood of violent behavior. A certain concept of masculinity also plays a role, wherever that may come from. But we do not see culture as a factor, contrary to the prejudice that masculinity and violence play a greater role in other cultures."

Prätor points to a survey on attitudes toward violence. According to the survey, 5% of German youths view violence positively. Among those without a German passport, the figure is 10%.

The criminologist has identified a crucial factor: "Family upbringing plays an important role in shaping attitudes toward violence, because these attitudes are formed in the home. Germany is, however, on the right track, says Prätor, even if it doesn't always feel that way. "Fifty or sixty years ago, parental violence was much more common in child-rearing than it is today. Prätor emphasizes that it is important to bring objectivity to the debate. "Of every 100 German citizens, 0.2% commit violent crimes; among non-German nationals, the figure is 0.6%."

However, Prätor points out that "more than 99% of the people living here in Germany — whether of German or non-German origin — the overwhelming majority in this country do not commit any serious violent crimes at all."

It describes fast tech, which is booming, as charging and lighting devices under €10 (circa $11), high-tech items like power banks and fitness trackers, and gadgets and lifestyle appliances like cheap mini blenders and fans, as well as single-use electronic vapes.

"So, these electronics are a bit like fast fashion," Magdalena Charytanowicz, WEEE Forum's Communication Manager, told DW.

Nearly 40% of buyers recalled discarding a fast-tech item without using it at all or within a month of buying it, and nearly 60% said they had used a product for no longer than six months. Yet the vast majority of respondents were generally satisfied with their gadgets despite short-lived functionality.

This suggests consumers accept the "fast-tech bargain" of poor quality that "does the job for a while" and is easily replaced — similar to fast fashion — according to the report.

About 62 million tons of electronics are discarded worldwide annually. "That's the Great Wall of China of e-waste that is produced every year," noted Charytanowicz, adding that the figure is set to grow to 82 million by 2030.

In Europe, roughly 40% of electronic waste is recycled, yet the rate worldwide is just 17%. This means valuable materials are lost, while electronic waste ends up polluting the environment.

In 2022, a million tons of critical raw materials were embedded in the 10.7 million tons of e-waste generated across Europe, including manganese, gold, copper and aluminum.

These materials are at "the center of geopolitical tensions," Charytanowicz said, referring to escalating global tensions over access to rare earths and other critical minerals.

"Even the smallest" devices are worth recycling if they contain a circuit board or battery, she said.

Groups like WEEE Forum say more awareness raising is needed on how to dispose of cheap electronic devices, and that recycling should be made as easy as possible.

But the best way to reduce a product's environmental impact is to extend its lifespan through better design and repair, as recycling raw materials is energy intensive.

Charytonawicz said clearer, harmonized EU rules now under consideration could help improve e-waste collection. The bloc's 20-year-old directive is "not really adapted to the changing reality," she said.

Another growing area of e-waste is digital healthcare. While devices such as diabetes sensors and blood pressure monitors are "transforming healthcare," many are designed to be disposable.

A study published in Nature in December 2025 found sales of wearable healthcare electronics, for example, could reach 2 billion devices annually by 2050, and generate a million tons of e-waste.

Jeremy Faludi, associate professor of sustainable design engineering at TU Delft in the Netherlands, says making health tech — particularly digital devices used in, or issued by hospitals — recyclable or reusable comes with some "much bigger challenges" than normal electronics.

That's in part due to the potential for patient contamination, particularly with digital surgical devices that come into contact with blood or other bodily fluids.

"The way that a lot of things are designed now, you can't recover the stuff because it can't be easily sterilized without the electronics," said Faludi.

He worked on the Digital Health in Circular Economy (DiCE) project, an EU-funded initiative, to help redesign digital healthcare devices to "recover critical materials and reduce environmental impacts without compromising patient safety."

A modular design, for example, allows electronic components to be separated from the parts that need to be replaced between patients, making devices easier to reuse.

The four-year DiCE project, which included citizens, hospitals and manufacturers across nine EU countries, found reusable ECG cables — which connect patients to heart-monitoring equipment — cut environmental impacts by up to 81-94%, for example. The sets are usually designed for single use.

Rules around medical waste can also make reuse and recycling more difficult, particularly when requirements differ between countries and often come down to perceived rather than actual risk, Faludi added. He says some 85% of hospital trash is not infectious but everything ends up in the same bins. Better sorting as well as EU-wide regulations around treating non-infectious hospital waste would help here.

Like with other tech, consumer health devices such as blood pressure and glucose monitors often risk being forgotten in drawers or ending up in the wrong waste, simply because people do not know how to dispose of them, said Charytanowicz of WEEE Forum, which is also involved in DiCE.

Pilot schemes in Belgium, Spain and Slovenia that offered free postal returns and collection machines in public places found that more than 60% of people were willing to return unwanted devices if it was a straightforward process.

"What's really important is the convenience so that it can be integrated in your daily routines," said Charytanowicz, adding that "nudges" such as messages that recycling a device could help the health of another person motivate people to "do the right thing."
