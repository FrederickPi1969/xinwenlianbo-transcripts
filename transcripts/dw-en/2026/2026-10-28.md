## What would a new Israeli government mean for Arab neighbors? More of the same is expected, with some small positives predicted.

https://p.dw.com/p/5OmY2Arab commentators have celebrated the success of Israel's Arab parties as possible 'kingmakers' in the tight election, including the Joint List (pictured here) — even though most Israeli Jewish parties say they won't govern with them Image: Jamal Awad/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementAs Israeli politicians begin to try to build a coalition government, after an election that saw no clear winner, response from politicians in surrounding Arab countries has been muted or non-existent.

This may be because, for the time being anyway, it remains unclear as to whether the new Israeli government will continue with "business as usual" under incumbent Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu or whether a new government headed by Netanyahu's opponents will take what is expected to be a more moderate domestic course.

Whatever the eventual outcome, the consensus is that there is unlikely to be a huge change in Israel's foreign relations with neighboring countries like Syria, Lebanon, Jordan and Egypt, and, further afield, the Gulf states. Nor is it likely to produce very different policies toward Palestinians, which many Arab populations in the region feel strongly about.

Both Netanyahu and his main opponent Gadi Eisenkot "are two sides of the same coin," Egyptian politician Mostafa Bakry wrote on X after exit polls seemed to indicate an Eisenkot victory (something that's increasingly contested) and Israeli-Arab parties had displaced Netanyahu's far-right allies. "Arab joy at Netanyahu's near-failure to form the government does not mean that what is to come … differs greatly from his predecessor."

"An Eisenkot government would likely be politically fragile, limiting its room for maneuver," Beth Oppenheim, a policy fellow in the Middle East and North Africa program at the European Council on Foreign Relations, wrote in a commentary last week. "The biggest differences from the current government would be at home: It would halt the judicial overhaul, open a state commission of inquiry into October 7th and pass a conscription law."

However, Oppenheim explained, "when it comes to foreign policy, there would not be a fundamental transformation. Netanyahu's post-October 7th security doctrine — pre-empting threats, operating far beyond Israel's borders and holding 'buffer zones' in neighboring territories — has been embraced by nearly all Jewish Israeli parties."

One of the election campaign posters for Netanyahu's Likud party bore a stark message — "The right or Palestine" — the message being that if voters did not choose Netanyahu and his right-wing allies, his opponents would be too accommodating toward Israel's Arab-led political parties and would be open to the establishment of a Palestinian state.

But observers believe that message is exaggerated, and that the two-state solution is already dead.

"Support for Palestinian statehood has collapsed across most of the Jewish political spectrum, and permanent Israeli control over Palestinians has become normalized," Oppenheim pointed out. With the exception of one party, "all mainstream Jewish parties support, or at least acquiesce in, the settlement enterprise in some form and publicly oppose a Palestinian state on the 1967 lines."

In August this year, Eisenkot himself said that, "there is no Palestinian state, and no Palestinian state will be established under the government we form."

An Eisenkot-led government would, however, cooperate with more left-wing Israeli parties, which have previously supported a two-state solution. Any new coalition may also need the backing of Israel's Arab-led parties, who are currently thought to have increased their seats in parliament to around 19.

But as Mairav Zonszein, senior Israel analyst at the International Crisis Group, told the news agency Associated Press this week, "At best, [a new government] would be able to slow down the extremely crazy acceleration that we've seen in the last four years of every aspect of Israel's aggression in the occupied territories and inside Israel itself."

A less extreme government would tone down religious messaging, might be less demanding when it came to conditions on disarming Hamas in Gaza, and could potentially move forward with the next stage of the Gaza ceasefire, agreed to in late 2025, but barely implemented. It might also do more to contain settler violence in the West Bank.

"That's because [settler extremism] is a phenomenon that doesn't enjoy the support of the majority of Israelis, damages Israel's international reputation, and suppressing it is feasible," Elizabeth Tsurkov, a senior fellow at the Washington-based think tank, the Middle East Policy Council, explained on social media.

"[A new Israeli government] would keep expanding the settlements and Palestinian statehood would remain taboo," Oppenheim wrote. "Without a diplomatic track with the Palestinians, further flare-ups of violence in the West Bank and Gaza are inevitable, destabilizing the region — and normalization with Saudi Arabia will remain off the table."

After the October 7, 2023, attack on Israel by Hamas and Israel's ensuing military campaign, which has resulted in at least 70,000 Palestinians killed and Gaza devastated, Saudi Arabia's conditions for normalization with Israel have hardened.

The country, hugely influential in the Muslim world, has put out several statements saying there could be no path to normalizing relations with Israel without some progress on Palestinian statehood.

Last week, at a meeting of foreign ministers from Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, Turkey and Egypt, the Saudis co-signed a communique in which the four countries said Israel was "the fundamental source of instability in the region and beyond."

But there is some cooperation between the two countries behind the scenes. Analysts say that if a government less hardline than Netanyahu's gets into power in Israel, it will be easier for countries like Saudi Arabia — but also the United Arab Emirates, Jordan and Egypt, which all have varying levels of backchannel cooperation and contact with Israel — to admit to this kind of behind-the-scenes diplomacy.

According to a study published last week by Syrian think tank, Etana, Israeli troops have come as far as 1,160 kilometers (721 miles) into Syria from the two countries' disputed border, occupied Syrian land as far as 400 kilometers from it and carried out 1,996 ground operations in southwest Syria between January and September this year.

Before elections this week there was no chance of any kind of withdrawal from Syria because it would have been seen as "defeat," Ksenia Svetlova, an associate fellow at Chatham House and a former member of the Israeli parliament, told online publication Syria in Transition this month. But if the anti-Netanyahu bloc gets into power, that could change, she suggested.

"A security agreement with Syria will become more probable, as many potential stakeholders [in a new, more centrist Israeli government] have spoken favorably about it in the past," noted Svetlova, who is also director of the Tel Aviv-based Regional Organization for Peace, Economics and Security, which works on Israeli-Palestinian-Arab partnerships. "However I do expect that it will take time as this government will be constantly attacked by the extreme right."

For months this year, Israel and Syrian diplomats negotiated a security agreement under the US' stewardship. But it came to nothing in late 2026 after Israel apparently refused to withdraw back to boundary lines established under a 1974 disengagement agreement.

Observers and media commentators in Lebanon say the Lebanese government would prefer to have Eisenkot's anti-Netanyahu bloc in power.

Lebanon and Israel are continuing to negotiate an Israeli withdrawal from southern Lebanon, where the Israelis claim they have established a "buffer zone" to facilitate their fight against Lebanese militia group, Hezbollah. The Lebanese argue the Israelis have in fact invaded their country.

And when it comes to negotiations around disarming Hezbollah and withdrawing Israeli troops, there's a perception in Lebanese political circles that Eisenkot would be easier to deal with than Netanyahu and his extremist allies.

This is despite the fact that Eisenkot is widely seen as the inventor of the so-called "Dahiyeh Doctrine," a military strategy that calls for asymmetric warfare in areas like Beirut's southern suburbs, where Hezbollah is based, to pressure both militants and their civilian support base.

A similar strategy is thought to be being implemented in southern Lebanon now, where whole villages continue to be razed by the Israeli military, even as active fighting has subsided.

Lebanese commentators are also optimistic that Eisenkot, former commander of the Israeli army, would have something in common with their own president, Joseph Aoun, who used to head Lebanon's armed forces.

"When Netanyahu was ousted from power between 2021 and 2022 by Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid [now members of the camp led by Eisenkot], we were able to negotiate and conclude the maritime agreement," a Lebanese official told Lebanese daily, L'Orient Today, referring to a 2022 deal to share offshore territory.

## Ukraine&apos;s Zelenskyy irked by US-Russia-China summit

https://p.dw.com/p/5OmfAZelenskyy is concerned that Xi Jinping, Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin could divide up global spheres of influence between themImage: Dmitri Lovetsky/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementUkrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has warned that a planned meeting between Russia, China and the United States next month could present the three participants with an opportunity to redraw global spheres of influence.

"We really don't want this to turn into 'Yalta 2, 3, 4' with a redistribution of influence," he said in comments to journalists on Wednesday, referring to the 1945 Yalta Conference in Crimea , where Allied powers redrew the world map in the wake of the Second World War.

The Kremlin has previously indicated that preparations were underway for a meeting between Russian President Vladimir Putin and his US and Chinese counterparts, Donald Trump and Xi Jinping, on the sidelines of an Asia-Pacific summit next month.

The Wall Street Journal first broke the news about the three-way summit earlier this month and said that Trump had not agreed to attend the summit at the time.

But the Journal said Trump was open to the proposal — that had been raised by Putin — since he wanted to bring an end to the war in Ukraine.

"What China, Russia and the United States might discuss has not yet been clearly defined," Zelenskyy said, adding that "Russia wants this meeting more than anyone," warning against handing Moscow "another major diplomatic advantage."

For years now, Russia has been seeking a new agreement with Washington on European security architecture, and in particular an end to what Putin considers NATO's aggressive expansion towards Russia.

Putin continues to cast the ongoing invasion of Ukraine as a response to such a NATO threat – a position widely rejected in Kyiv and the rest of Europe.

Zelenskyy's warning came ahead of planned talks between Ukrainian, Russian and US envoys in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) next month.

No specific date has been set for the Abu Dhabi meeting, but Kyiv is keen for it to take place before the potential Russia-China-US summit.

Before that, it was also confirmed on Wednesday that Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov will travel to the United States for a meeting with G20 counterparts in Atlanta on October 30-31.

Zelenskyy's remarks were released as he traveled to the Netherlands for meetings with Dutch Prime Minister Rob Jetten and Dutch King Willem-Alexander in The Hague.

The Dutch government said on Wednesday it would provide Ukraine with €1.2 billion ($1.3 billion) in non-military support over the next ⁠years.

Zelenskyy also held a phone call with British Prime Minister Andy Burnham to thank him for an ​extra £10 ⁠billion ($13.29 billion/€11.94 billion) of support announced earlier ⁠on ​Wednesday.

"A truly ​strong very timely step coming right Russia needs to and ​feel that Ukraine will not be left ​alone to deal with the problems ⁠Russia ​is so ​eager to escalate," he wrote on social media.

But Ukraine's greatest assets in its defense against Russian aggression remain US Patriot air defense missiles, and Zelenskyy urged Washington to increase supplies.

Zelenskyy said Ukraine had been receiving 12 missiles a month this year but is being targeted with "150 ballistic missiles a month."

Meanwhile on the battlefield, officials said a six-day ceasefire in a Russian-occupied town in southern Ukraine held for its first day and night.

The localized truce in the devastated town of Oleshky began on Tuesday and is due to last until Sunday, enabling a planned humanitarian operation to provide around 2,000 remaining residents with urgently needed food and medicine, and evacuate those who want to leave.

"We are grateful that they are organizing this possibility to evacuate people," said President Zelenskyy.

The plight of Oleshky in Ukraine's southern Kherson region caught the attention of US first lady Melania Trump, who the White House said helped broker the humanitarian mission.

If the ceasefire holds, it will be the first time in more than four-and-a-half years of fighting that both sides have observed a significant truce, with previous attempts quickly unraveling.

"This is the biggest de-escalation since 2022," said Ukraine's negotiator, Davyd ‌Arakhamia, on Tuesday, adding every step brings peace closer.

Elsewhere, however, the war raged on with five people, including a four-year-old child, injured in a Russian drone strike on the northeastern Ukrainian city of Kharkiv overnight.

In Moscow, the Russian Defense Ministry said air defenses had downed 531 Ukrainian drones on Tuesday night and into Wednesday morning, but local authorities in the Belgorod border region said one person was killed in a drone attack.

And in Sofia, Bulgarian Defense Minister Dimitar Stoyanov said three maritime drones struck a container ship in the Black Sea off the coast of Bulgaria.

The crew evacuated the vessel and there were no immediate reports of casualties, according to Bulgarian state news agency BTA, but the incident took place as defense ministers from Bulgaria, Romania and Turkey were meeting in Sofia for trilateral talks focused on Black Sea security.

"We follow with concern the intense attacks on commercial vessels in the Black Sea ​and call on the sides to act with common sense, in line with the principle of navigational safety in open seas," said Turkish Defense Minister Yasar Guler.

"The necessary warnings are constantly being conveyed to both sides to keep commercial navigational out of the conflict zone and ​immediately halt attacks on civilian vessels."

A series of drone attacks have occurred this month off the coasts of NATO members Romania and Bulgaria. Russia has not taken responsibility, but suspicion has increasingly fallen on Moscow.

## Flavio Bolsonaro lands in Miami after fleeing Brazil for US

https://p.dw.com/p/5OmfrNews of Bolsonaro departing Brazil broke on Tuesday, just two days after Brazil's presidential electionsImage: Marta Lavandier/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementBrazilian right-wing presidential candidate Flavio Bolsonaro and his family landed in Miami, Florida in the US on Wednesday morning.

His plan to leave Brazil became public knowledge late on Tuesday, soon after he had refused to concede defeat in Sunday's presidential elections and pledged to challenge the results.

Bolsonaro refused to comply with a Brazilian Supreme Court order to surrender his passports, tied to a money-laundering investigation in which he denies wrongdoing, and the US State Department later said it would grant him and his family visas.

Bolsonaro, his wife and two daughters were seen disembarking a Gulfstream G400 plane and heading into the Miami-Opa Locka airport on Wednesday morning.

The quartet first flew into Argentina late on Tuesday, then took a private jet to the Ecuadorean city of Guayaquil. From there, they continued to Miami in Florida.

Bolsonaro would lose his immunity from prosecution in February when his term in the Senate ends. On Monday, he had said in an Instagram post that he had no intention of leaving the country and that he would "hand over my passports and defend myself against this travesty, which is a clear attempt to intimidate me."

According to the AFP news agency, Bolsonaro also holds Italian citizenship and so was able to travel despite his Brazilian and diplomatic passports being canceled.

Bolsonaro's departure and his arrival in Florida — like his father Jair Bolsonaro before him after Brazil's previous presidential elections — could deepen a rift between the two most populous countries in the Americas.

During the election campaign, incumbent Luiz Inacio "Lula" da Silva had accused the Donald Trump administration of colluding with the Bolsonaro family in a bid to help them return to power.

After the election results, Trump appeared to question the integrity of the vote, saying he thought the extension to voting times was a "very bad look." He had also alleged in the past that Jair Bolsonaro was the victim of judicial persecution in Brazil.

Bolsonaro senior is now in jail in Brazil on charges of leading a conspiracy designed to keep him in power despite defeat in 2022's presidential election.

## Is Iran headed to war or peace after Mecca alliance meeting?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OmZMSenior officials from Turkey, Pakistan and Saudi Arabia all traveled to Tehran to meet President Masoud Pezeshkian and other Iranian leadersImage: Iranian Presidency/AFPAdvertisementAmid rising fear that the US and Israel are preparing to launch new attacks on Iran after the US midterm election on November 3, three major Middle Eastern powers delivered a coordinated diplomatic message directly to Tehran.

Pakistan's army chief Asim Munir, Saudi Defense Minister Khalid bin Salman and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin all traveled to Iran to meet Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian and senior officials in Tehran this Monday. The three envoys serve as representatives for the Mecca defense pact — a military alliance of Islamabad, Riyad and Ankara, which was officially set up this summer.

Saudi Arabia said ⁠the ⁠trip aims to open negotiating channels and "prevent the return of military escalation." Details on the agenda, however, remain scarce.

Another official said Pakistan's Munir presented Iran with a solution to the conflict that would offer something both to Washington and Iran, with the three Mecca states and Qatar acting as mediators. The source told Reuters US President Donald Trump previously gave Munir the go-ahead to try diplomacy.

Iran hailed the "very positive atmosphere" during the Tehran talks, even though no breakthroughs were officially announced.

"Very good discussions took place between us," Mohsen Rezaei, head of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, told state television. "I feel that a new atmosphere is emerging in the region."

Meanwhile, Iran's President Masoud Pezeshkian travelled to Moscow for the Gas Exporting Countries Forum, where he met Russian President Vladimir Putin and Qatar's Emir Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani, with Pezeshkian and the emir also discussing the war and the Strait of Hormuz.

These and other diplomatic contacts were framed by Tehran as evidence that the US is unable to diplomatically isolate the Iranian regime.

The united front presented by the three nations in Tehran, however, can also be seen as a show of force for the new Mecca defense alliance. It comes as the Saudis grow increasingly wary of the attacks on Saudi infrastructure launched by the Iran-allied Houthi militants.

Under the terms of the Mecca agreement, an outside attack on one of the member states is to be regarded as an attack on all of them. The visit also came on the same day as Egypt's parliament agreeing to give military help to Ryad and take "all necessary measures" to help Saudi Arabia defend itself, in addition to the looming threat of the US and Israel.

"The presence of the three senior representatives at the same time sent the message that these countries are acting as a bloc," Babak Dorbeiki, a London-based political analyst and former official at Iran's Strategic Research Center, told DW.

Saudi Arabia has long been regarded as Iran's archrival in region. It has used its enormous wealth to counter Iran's influence, and now it can, at least in theory, also count on Pakistan's nuclear capability, and Turkey's conventional military strength.

At the same time, Dorbeiki argued that the Mecca pact is primarily designed for deterrence rather than offensive war. And having a defense pact does not mean its members want to fight Iran.

Dorbeiki believes the message from the bloc could therefore work in two directions: It can provide mediation, but continued escalation could also alter that position.

Saudi Arabia faces the most immediate dilemma. Further Houthi attacks have damaged its infrastructure and economy, but a wider war with Iran could threaten the investment and development projects at the heart of Riyadh's economic strategy.

"For Saudi Arabia, both the current situation and a war with Iran carry serious costs," Dorbeiki said. "Its preferred route is, therefore, a diplomatic solution."

## Gen Z protests in Germany: Is France-style violence looming?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OmW2Young people have already been protesting against the reintroduction of military serviceImage: Snowfield Photography/picture allianceAdvertisementAs politicians in Germany look ahead to the coming weekend, many fear that cities across the country could witness the kind of violent unrest that recently shook France. At the same time, they are concerned about their educational prospects and future opportunities.

## Can we regrow damaged seagrass meadows?

https://p.dw.com/p/5O4G5Regrowing underwater seagrass meadows takes time and patienceImage: GeomarAdvertisementHalf a dozen divers are navigating the cold waters of the Baltic Sea off the coast of Dahme, a small beach town in northern Germany. Keeping the roots intact, conservationists carefully lift shoots from a donor meadow of seagrass and place them in a bag.

They only take a few shoots from each spot, which will be planted where the conservationists hope to regrow another meadow. They've already picked out a prime area for the young plants, which need sandy sediment, shelter from waves and currents and sufficient light and oxygen to thrive.

The scientists see the work as critical. Myriam Spicka, who heads the NGO Seagrass Conservation, says she has "seen the changes" in the more than 30 years she's been diving. And she is not the only one who has witnessed the destruction.

Scientists estimate that around 60% of the Baltic coast's seagrass meadows have disappeared.

"That's the bad news," said marine ecologist Thorsten Reusch from the GEOMAR Helmholtz Center for Ocean Research Kiel . "The good news is that the decline has stopped."

Underwater seagrass meadows feed and shelter fish, crabs, shellfish and many other species. They improve water quality and slow waves, helping protect shores from erosion. And they store carbon in the seabed about 30 to 50 times faster than forests.

Around 70 species of seagrass can be found along the coasts of more than 150 countries. Some grow just below the surface, others at depths of 60 meters or almost 200 feet.

"Some countries have far more seagrass, particularly in the Indo-Pacific," said Reusch. "There, it can play a major role in their carbon balance."

But the value of seagrass goes beyond carbon storage. Its meadows in the Baltic are among the sea's most valuable habitats. They shelter wildlife, stabilize the seabed and act as natural filters by trapping particles and nutrients.

Planting individual seagrass shoots is painstaking work so people like Reinhard Helbig are trying to make it easier.

Through ZosteraTec, the start-up he co-founded, he helps restore marine habitats, including seagrass meadows damaged by offshore activity, such as wind farm construction. His work begins on the beach.

"Stormy weather helps us. Waves and currents wash seagrass out of the seabed. A day or two later, it washes up here, still fresh," he said.

The collected shoots are grown and multiplied in ZosteraTec's aquaculture. Several months later, the marine saplings are laid on a plant support. A specially developed machine binds the planted strands with biodegradable thread. The resulting lengths can be laid on the seabed with less diving work.

"We plant here on land to cut down on underwater work. That means fewer dives, and we can do the painstaking work on shore," Helbig said.

Researchers at the Geomar Helmholtz Center in Kiel are also testing new ways to restore seagrass across larger areas, including using seeds.

Marine ecologist Esther Thomsen reaches into an aquarium tank and pulls out a flowerpot containing seagrass.

"Ten percent of the plants in our meadows produce seeds. You can clearly see the seeds sitting inside the seed pod," she said.

"The big advantage of restoring meadows from seed is greater genetic diversity. And in the future, we should be able to sow seeds over much larger areas," explained Thomsen.

Reusch from Geomar said they currently "think the most promising way to grow meadows that can withstand climate change is to plant individual shoots as a foundation, then sow seeds between them to increase genetic diversity."

Thomsen and her colleagues are visiting the first seagrass meadow planted in Germany's Baltic waters in 2021. The divers count shoots, check how densely they grow and record how the meadow has developed.

They then compare it with the donor meadow that supplied the plants. Only that comparison reveals whether the new crop has merely survived or is beginning to function more like a natural one.

"We've just checked the seagrass meadow, and it's really dense. We counted lots of shoots per square," Thomsen said. "It's looking really good."

"73% of Turkey’s total automotive exports go to the EU," Zeytinoglu told DW. "If we encounter a 'Made in Europe' barrier in the EU market, this will cause great damage to our trade with the EU and to our exports."

Turkey's auto exporters association OIB calculated that the country's automotive exports to the EU totaled $30.1 billion (€26.8 billion) last year.

Tommaso Pardi, director of the international automotive research network GERPISA and a senior researcher at the French National Center for Scientific Research (CNRS), notes that the 70% target is remarkably generous.

"If you take an average European-made electric car and take away the battery, you would find that around 83% [of parts] come from Europe," Pardi told DW.

Under the revised German-French plan, some self-charging hybrids and petrol and diesel vehicles could still be sold after 2035 and would also be affected by the "Made in Europe" rules.

These cars contain a much lower proportion of European parts. Most of Turkey's nearly 800,000 vehicle exports to the EU last year would fall under this much larger umbrella.

South Korean automakers, meanwhile, could also be hurt. Hyundai and Kia build cars in the Czech Republic and Slovakia, but also ship models from Asia, which would not qualify. Korean battery plants in Poland and Hungary, however, would count as European.

"They want to limit the IAA to retain the biggest scope to still source from abroad, particularly from China," he said.

Ankara is also lobbying Brussels to be included in the "Made in Europe" program, while automakers are seeking a compromise that would allow Turkey to join at a later stage.

"A lost industrial job is very hard to win back, and the damage reaches well beyond large suppliers," Benjamin Krieger, Secretary General of the European Association of Automotive Suppliers (CLEPA), told DW.

## Russia in Africa: What is Putin hoping to achieve?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OkuAA poster in Burkina Faso depicts the country's military leader Ibrahim Traore with the Russian president Vladimir PutinImage: Christina Peters/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementThe conflict with the West sparked by Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine  prompted Russian President Vladimir Putin to seek support from other parts of the world. Africa has become a focus, with foreign minister Sergei Lavrov visiting the continent increasingly often.

Russian foreign ministry spokesperson Maria Zakharova has described it as a "historic return," and declared no one and nothing could be compared to what Russia did for Africa in the 20th century.

A Russia-Africa summit has been held in Russia at roughly three-year intervals since 2019. The third takes place in Moscow on October 28 and 29. The program focusses on economic cooperation. Other topics range from politics and security to science and culture. But Russia is actively working with only a few African countries, mainly in the north of the continent, and this is reflected in the trade figures.

"South Africa is still Africa's largest national economy, but its volume of trade with Russia is small, far less than with Egypt and Algeria," says Ivan Klyszcz from the International Centre for Defence and Security (ICDS) in Tallinn, Estonia.

"Egypt and Algeria in particular are key partners for Russia. Egypt plays an important role in this regard. Russian diplomats and business representatives are trying to extend relationships with other countries, especially Tanzania and Ethiopia, but with no appreciable success so far."

Egypt is the second-largest economy in Africa, and the bilateral volume of trade with Russia has more than doubled since 2021; in 2025, according to Russia's Federal Customs Service, it was $10.5 billion (€9.4 billion). Consignments of Russian grain are the main drivers, followed by products of the metal industry. Egypt's exports to Russia are primarily fruit and vegetables.

There are investment projects, too. Russia is building a big nuclear power plant in the Egyptian region of El-Dabaa on the Mediterranean coast. Construction is being financed by a Russian loan of 25 billion US dollars, which covers around 85% of the costs.

Egypt is one of the few African countries with which Russia enjoys stable business relationships, as well as having extensive experience of cooperation, says Nikita Smagin, an expert on Russian policy in the Middle East. "In the long term, Egypt could become Moscow's gateway to Africa and to other markets," he says. "But Cairo is pursuing its own interests. It would be too optimistic to hope that Egypt is lending its support purely out of political solidarity."

According to the Russian customs service, around 39% of Russian–African trade in 2025 was with Egypt, and total sales between Russia and all 54 countries of the African continent came to around 27 billion US dollars. Africa's share of Russia's foreign trade is therefore very small (3.9% in 2025), and not at all commensurate with the attention Moscow is devoting to its relations with African countries.

Russia is not a significant trading partner for Africa, either. The most important partner for African countries is the European Union. In 2025, the volume of trade with the EU came to around 385 billion US dollars. African countries' statistics also show that other partners were well ahead of Russia. The volume of trade with China was in excess of 348 billion US dollars in 2025; with the United States it was almost 105 billion, and 103 billion with India.

Fidel Owusu, a Ghanaian expert with Geopolitical Intelligence Services (GIS) in Liechtenstein, comments that Russia's economic presence in Africa has not been as significant as that of China, the United States, India, or other traditional powers like France and Britain. "If you look at the volume of trade and the kind of investment," he says, "in recent times, some middle powers such as Turkey and the UAW are doing far better than Russia."

Beverly Ochieng, a senior analyst with Control Risks, a global risk and strategic intelligence consultancy in Senegal, observes: "Western countries continue to dominate in strategically important areas such as raw material extraction, finance, and the energy sector, even if their political presence in the region is declining." According to her, "Russia will compete more intensively for access to critical mineral resources and energy projects. We see this with lithium projects in Mali as well as with nuclear power initiatives across the continent."

Russia's presence in Africa is more obvious in the military sector than in the fields of trade and investment. Starting with the arrival of the Wagner Group, Russian mercenaries have been supporting African regimes, particularly those of the unstable countries in the Sahel region. In exchange, their associated subsidiaries are granted access to raw materials. Following the death of the Wagner Group leader, Yevgeny Prigozhin, most of these operations were taken over by the Africa Corps, a paramilitary group set up and controlled by Russia's defense ministry.

According to the Congressional Research Service, the think tank of the US Congress, the largest Russian military contingents, each comprising several thousand men, are in the Central African Republic, Mali, and Libya. The Africa Corps is also keen to extend its presence in Burkina Faso and Niger. Russian mercenaries have also been spotted in Equatorial Guinea.

Irina Filatova from the University of Cape Town stresses that there are several reasons why it's important for Russia to maintain a military presence in Africa. "The countries have sizeable deposits of minerals such as gold and uranium. Through them, it's possible to exert influence over Europe's southern borders, including in matters of migration and regional security. With regard to Libya, the country is an important logistical hub for connections with the Sahel and central Africa. Goods can easily be supplied via Libya to countries without access to the sea," Filatova says.

Ultimately, Russia is still one of the biggest suppliers of arms to African countries, even though Russian exports have fallen over the course of the war in Ukraine. The Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI) estimates Moscow's share of the African market for the years 2021–2025 at 15%, which puts it in third place behind the United States (19%) and China (17%). Between 2020 and 2024, its share was even higher: It was in first place with 21%, followed by China with 18%, and the US with 16%.

## Experts urge &apos;global health emergency&apos; as climate harms accelerate

https://p.dw.com/p/5Oif2A heatstroke patient is treated in Chennai, India. Climate change caused more than half a million heat deaths in 2023 aloneImage: Pugazh Murugan/ Matrix Images/picture alliance AdvertisementAs heat deaths, drought and food insecurity accelerate worldwide, nearly 150 researchers from more than 30 countries are calling for climate change to be declared a "global health emergency."

The health and economic toll of planetary heating has risen sharply in the decade since countries signed the Paris climate accord — a landmark deal to stop climate change that was hailed by the World Health Organization (WHO) at the time as "the most important public health agreement of the century."

Yet, climate change was responsible for more than half a million heat deaths in 2023 alone and nearly 3 million since 2016. The area affected by extreme drought, which threatens food and water supplies and raises the risk of disease, grew more than 700% faster than in the decade before. And heat cost the world an estimated $1.07 trillion (around €962 billion) in lost potential income last year, all according to the annual Lancet Countdown on Health and Climate Change.

"This cannot continue," said Alan Dangour, director of climate and health at Wellcome Trust, one of the world's largest charities, which funded the Lancet report. "We must change course and put the health and well-being of people at the heart of our response to climate change — measuring success not through emissions targets or degrees Celsius but by lives saved and health outcomes improved."

The latest findings from the Lancet study show how rapidly the health risks are growing by comparing this decade to the one before the Paris Agreement was signed.

Heat is the starkest example. Climate change was responsible for 68% of heat-related deaths between 2016 and 2023, up from 55% in the previous decade. The yearly increase in climate-driven heat deaths more than doubled over the same period.

But deaths are only "the tip of the iceberg," said Ollie Jay of the University of Sydney's Heat and Health Research Center. "Except that iceberg is not melting, it's growing every year." Beneath the surface are also heatstroke, lost sleep and worsening chronic health conditions.

The economic harms are mounting too. Working in extreme heat puts people at risk of heat exhaustion, illness or injury and, ultimately, losing income and livelihoods.

Heat wiped out 573 billion potential working hours in 2025, with farmworkers bearing the brunt. Vulnerable economies in Africa and South-East Asia were hit hardest with losses reaching 5% of GDP, compared with 0.5% in the more developed economies.

"The economic toll is battering vulnerable sectors and communities the hardest," Marina Romanello, executive director of the Lancet Countdown, who is based at University College London, told journalists at a press briefing.

Water extremes are intensifying too. Satellite data show that since 2003, 43% of the world's land has been steadily drying out, while 37% has been getting wetter. Together, that is more than 80% of land shifting to extremes that threaten crops, food supplies and sanitation.

Drought can leave people without safe drinking water and raises the risk of infectious disease, while floods bring drowning, injury and contaminated water.

Heat waves and droughts pushed nearly 128 million more people into food insecurity in 2024, compared with the 1981–2010 average.

To save lives and protect human health, experts are calling for a new WHO instrument to help countries share information, coordinate responses, monitor threats and disburse funds. The United Nations and its member states would create the mechanism.

The closest existing version of this is the WHO's Public Health Emergency of International Concern mechanism (PHEIC) used during the COVID-19 pandemic and mpox outbreaks. But built with short-term emergencies in mind, it falls short of addressing a complex threat that requires sustained global action across multiple sectors, like climate change.

The world needs a broader approach, "so that we're not responding to emergency after emergency," said Romanello. "We're doing a cohesive long-term response that builds resilience, builds capacities and builds the possibilities of countries to avoid further emergencies."

Romanello told journalists that "we have more than enough proof" that such action does improve health outcomes. "Early warning systems informed by climate, have already seen a reduction in the adverse impacts of these extreme weather events," she said.

The growth in renewable energy has also partly aided a reduction in deaths from fossil fuel air pollution in half of 140 countries assessed since 2016, preventing an estimated 323,000 deaths a year.

And 56 countries now have national plans to adapt their health services for climate impacts, such as heat waves and disease outbreaks, up from just 14 in 2015.

Fossil fuels, the burning of which releases emissions fueling planetary heating as well as other pollutants, still supply more than 80% of the world's energy.

Energy-related emissions hit a record high in 2024. The world's 100 largest oil and gas companies are on track to produce 150% more oil and gas by 2040 than is compatible with limiting warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 Fahrenheit). A year ago, that figure was 138%.

Governments continue to fund fossil fuels, with net subsidies doubling since 2016 to $6.2 trillion. In 2024, 10 countries spent more on such subsidies than on their entire health systems.

Yet as fossil fuel prices rise and renewables become cheaper, these polluting technologies are becoming increasingly uneconomical, said Romanello.

Romanello likened new fossil fuel investment to "investing in Blockbuster when you have Netflix," — a bet on an old technology, referring to the once ubiquitous video rental store that failed to adapt to streaming.

Meanwhile, money to help health systems adapt to climate change is scarce. Developing countries estimate they need around $3.3 billion a year for such adaptations, which the Lancet authors say is likely an underestimate. Only about a third of that was committed in 2024. Foreign aid reductions, led by the US and followed by Germany, which made the second-largest cut, are widening the gap.

"This report is a stark warning that climate change is a health emergency," said Helen Clark, former prime minister of New Zealand and chair of the Lancet Countdown's board. "What's missing is not evidence, but the political will to act on it."
