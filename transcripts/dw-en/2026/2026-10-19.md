## Canada: Alberta votes on holding independence referendum

https://p.dw.com/p/5OPgRAlberta is a vast, resource, rich state with a population slightly smaller than Finland or New ZealandImage: Jeff McIntosh/PA Images/picture allianceAdvertisementResidents of Alberta in Canada are voting in a referendum on whether the province should begin a process that could ultimately lead to independence.

The motion does not appear likely to succeed but the vote taking place at all signifies economic and political grievances gaining some sway in the vast oil-rich region.

Separatist leaders hope that a comparatively strong showing for their cause could nevertheless pressure Prime Minister Mark Carney's government in Ottawa to pay them more heed in future.

Polls opened at 9 a.m. local time in the provincial capital Edmonton (1500 GMT/UTC) on Monday.

At least based on opinion polls, the yes/no result is in no real doubt, but as many as 1 in 3 voters might support the proposal.

Monday's referendum is the first on the notion of breaking away from Canada in a province other than majority French-speaking Quebec, which has a better-known separatist movement founded in language and cultural differences.

The vast oil-rich state is almost double the size of Germany in terms of land mass, but is much more sparsely populated with around 5 million residents. Almost half of them live in its two main cities, Edmonton and Calgary.

Alberta's provincial government is led by Danielle Smith of the United Conservative Party while Mark Carney's Liberal Party lead the national government.

Smith herself does not advocate independence and has urged voters to oppose the motion. But she has nevertheless authorized the vote, seemingly in a bid to assuage an increasingly vocal flank within her own ranks.

Independence advocates accuse the government of stifling Alberta's oil industry, for instance through environmental regulations, and also say they contribute disproportionately to the national public purse.

"Canada has an imbalanced system which feeds the central population and government at the expense of the outlying producing provinces such as Alberta," said Cory Morgan, founder of a pro-separation organization called Pathway to Independence.

Carney, who grew up in Alberta, has spent much of the past week campaigning for the "stay" campaign, appealing to national pride and urging supporters to vote to provide as resounding a result as possible — even in what might not be a close race.

The renowned economist and central banker often argued that the separatist cause might be guilty of overlooking matters more significant than dollars and drilling permits.

"Canada is not a transaction between provinces. It's not about gross and net, gives and gets," he said at an event in Red Deer on Friday.

Although polls in French-speaking Quebec suggest that enthusiasm for independence — typically a minority cause — is lower than usual during Carney's premiership, the issue is never very far from the political debate. The success of the separatist Parti Quebecois in local elections this month served as a reminder of this.

Carney is also trying to maintain national unity amid pressure from Canada's nearest neighbor and biggest trade partner.

US President Donald Trump is conducting characteristically combative trade talks with Canada that have seemed in danger of breaking down in recent months.

When at his most agitated, Trump has even called for Canada to become the "51st state" of the US. He has also tried to rename bodies of water like Lake Ontario.

## Can diplomacy prevent an Ethiopia-Eritrea war as Tigray gets a new administration?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OR1XEthiopia has installed an interim administration in Tigray tasked with holding elections and rehousing displaced peopleImage: Anadolu/picture allianceAdvertisementEthiopia's Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed on Friday announced an interim government in the country's northern Tigray region and gave it six months to prepare for regional elections and restore stability.

Abiy's announcement followed a military offensive launched in September by the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF) and six other armed groups. They were ultimately forced to retreat and lost control of the region.

"To attain the peace that has long eluded them, to secure their right to self-administration, ​and to finally enjoy the fruits of development — which have been out of reach for many years — the people of Tigray must break free from this cycle ⁠of conflict," ​Abiy said in a statement posted on X.

Shishay Amare, the new mayor of the regional capital Mekelle, said: "Our role is to create the right conditions for elections by ensuring peace and stability across Tigray," reported French news agency Agence France-Presse (AFP).

"I am confident that this will be done within six months, according to our plan," Amare said, adding that their priorities included restoring peace, helping displaced residents return home, reopening markets and ensuring humanitarian assistance reached those in need.

The fighting has revived concerns about a return to the devastating 2020–2022 Tigray war. Around 1 million people remain displaced from that conflict. The African Union has estimated that the war resulted in around 600,000 deaths from combat, famine and the collapse of healthcare.

The new administration faces unresolved disputes over political authority and territory, including western Tigray, which is claimed by both Tigray and neighboring Amhara state.

Ethiopia and Eritrea were once bitter enemies but signed a peace agreement in 2018. Eritrean forces later fought alongside Ethiopian federal troops during the Tigray war. The countries' relationship has since deteriorated.

Addis Ababa accuses Asmara of supporting Tigrayan forces and violating Ethiopian territory. Eritrea has denied the accusations.

Reports of Eritrean troop movements near the border have heightened concern, although the extent of any current deployment remains disputed.

Tsedale Lemma, journalist and publisher of the prominent Addis Standard magazine, told DW earlier this month that the danger reflects years of unresolved tensions and failures to implement the November 2022 Pretoria Agreement, which ended the previous Tigray war.

Asked whether the international community was underestimating the threat, Lemma said: "It is absolutely underestimating it." She argued that international actors had failed to ensure effective monitoring of the agreement and respond adequately as the peace process weakened.

The confrontation comes amid instability across the Horn of Africa, including Sudan's war and tensions between Ethiopia and Egypt over the Nile mega-dam project.

Last year, Ethiopia inaugurated the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD), a move that was not well received by neighboring Egypt and Sudan, both downstream countries.

Competition over access to the Red Sea adds another strategic dimension: Ethiopia became landlocked when Eritrea gained independence in 1993.

Journalist Martin Plaut, who specializes in the Horn of Africa, has warned that the region faces a crossroads. He has raised the possibility of Ethiopian forces entering Eritrea, but an invasion remains a scenario, not a confirmed decision by the Ethiopian government.

Any wider conflict could threaten regional trade and maritime security. The Red Sea and the Bab el-Mandeb Strait connect shipping routes between Europe and Asia, making the region strategically important well beyond Africa.

The United States and the United Kingdom have called on Ethiopia and Eritrea to exercise restraint and return to dialogue under their 2018 peace framework.

Speaking to reporters at Joint Base Andrews on Saturday, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio warned that the military buildup on the Ethiopia-Eritrea border could escalate into a wider conflict.

Rubio said Washington hoped lifting some sanctions would improve communication with both sides and stressed the need for a "wise and measured" response to avoid a broader regional war.

A UK government spokesperson said in an October 15 statement that: "We urgently call for de-escalation and a return to dialogue. We reiterate our support for the African Union's role in facilitating this."

For now, the recapture of towns in Tigray has changed the military balance but has not resolved the tensions surrounding the region.

Whether international pressure can prevent a wider war will depend on whether the governments involved choose dialogue over further escalation.

## Myanmar refugees find hope in Thailand&apos;s work program

https://p.dw.com/p/5OP4LThailand's work program has allowed thousands of Myanmar refugees living in camps to support themselves and their families [FILE: May 28, 2024]Image: Matt Hunt/NurPhoto/picture allianceAdvertisementAt a glance, Ray Boe doesn't stand out from the tens of thousands of other foreign workers toiling at countless construction sites that push Bangkok's soaring skyline ever higher.

Unlike most of them, though, the 31-year-old came to Thailand's capital from one of the country's nine camps for refugees who have fled decades of civil strife inMyanmar.

The camps, which hug the Thai-Myanmar border, are home to more than 100,000 people — in some cases since the 1980s, when the refugees first started arriving.

Until a year ago this month, the Thai government had refused to let them leave the camps to find work, forcing most to live hand-to-mouth on foreign donor aid.

That changed in October 2025, after foreign aid cutbacks — mostly by the United States — forced the camps to slash the food support and healthcare they could provide.

Around the same time, fighting between Thailand and Cambodia over territorial claims had sent hundreds of thousands of migrant workers from Cambodia back home, leaving behind a yawning labor gap.

To cope with the aid cuts and labor deficit, the Thai government reversed decades of policy and began issuing the refugees permits to work outside the camps, to help them meet their own needs.

Ray Boe is one of the 6,800 camp refugees the government said have entered the Thai labor force in the first year of the program.

"After the cuts by USAID and other organizations, there is no more regular support for our daily needs, like rice and other rations. And there is not enough healthcare in the camps anymore," he said.

The cuts forced him and his parents to borrow from neighbors and to pare down their already modest meals.

Since last month, he has earned 400 baht (about $12) a day pouring concrete and erecting scaffolding around what's to be a 12-story apartment building in central Bangkok. He hopes to start sending money home soon.

"It will help us pay for food and other basic needs, and for health are for my parents," Ray Boe said.

"Compared to how we were living in the camp before, it will be a big difference, having my own income and supporting my family on my own, not having to wait for other people to support us day to day," he told DW.

The program allows the refugees to work in 43 of Thailand's 76 provinces and in any sector not reserved by law for Thai citizens only.

The Ministry of Foreign Affairs said the government has improved the vocational training on offer inside the camps to better prepare refugees for work outside.

"We are hopeful that this policy would enhance opportunities and empower the [refugees] to realize their potential, as well as contribute to Thai socioeconomic development," said Praporn Waenluang, a spokesman for the ministry's information department.

"I have met a lot of camp [refugees] who have come outside and worked. They are so happy to work and earn money, a lot of money compared to what they [can] get in the camp," said Siwawong Sooktawee, Thailand project coordinator for the International Detention Coalition.

"This is good because they feel they can help themselves, they can support themselves, not depend on aid anymore," he added.

Yet he and others have said the program has gotten off to a slow start, with many flaws to work out.

They said many of the refugees who have taken part have not been given contracts by their employers, only to find the conditions rougher, the work harder, or the pay lower than what they were promised before leaving the camps. The program also lacks a clear system for lodging and settling complaints, according to observers.

"Many refugees go, come back, go, come back so many times. Some refugees, their families have to raise funds or borrow money in the refugee camps so that their father or their family member will be able to return," said K'nyaw Paw, general secretary of the Karen Women's Organization, an NGO serving the camp's ethnic Karen group.

Siwawong and K'nyaw Paw said refugees are sometimes made to repay employers through wage deductions for hiring them, including medical exams, government permits and transportation to job sites. As a result, they often start work already in debt, sometimes without prior warning.

Akwah, a father of two who goes by one name, left his camp with 20 other refugees in November 2025 for what was supposed to be a job harvesting sugarcane on a farm on the other side of the country for 250 baht per day.

When he arrived, however, he was told to cut eucalyptus trees — a much more difficult and dangerous job, he said — for only 200 baht per day. Workers had to sleep outdoors, with no electricity and only a nearby pond for water.

"We spoke to the boss, but the boss did not care. He said, 'Just do the work because you are already here.' But we said we could not do it," said Akwah. They left after three months.

The Ministry of Foreign Affairs did not reply to DW's request for comment on the program's alleged problems. The Labor and Interior ministries, which mainly oversee the program, did not reply to requests for an interview either.

Siwawong and K'nyaw Paw said refugees who take part should be guaranteed clear and detailed contracts — translated if needed into Burmese, Karen or Karenni, the main languages spoken in the camps — and an easy-to-access system for filing and settling complaints.

They also suggest that the government open more provinces up to the program and screen the job brokers coming to the camps as middlemen, to weed out the unscrupulous ones.

They said their NGOs and others are working with the government on making some of the upgrades, and that they're hopeful the program will improve with time, so that more of the refugees are able and willing to take part.

For all the program's flaws, though, they said most of the refugees are still eager to apply, even those who have run into problems.

## Rubio visits India with a trip to the world&apos;s largest oil refinery topping the agenda

https://p.dw.com/p/5OPZ4Ahead of his arrival in India, Rubio said a trade deal was 'close' Image: Kylie Cooper/REUTERSAdvertisementUS Secretary of State Marco Rubio kicks off a three-day visit to India on Monday in the finance hub of Mumbai, before heading to Jamnagar, home to a major oil refinery owned by industrial titan Reliance Industries.

Rubio's business-oriented visit comes as trade talks remain stalled amid continued ambiguity on punitive measures from the US over India's purchases of Russian oil.

Reliance is a top buyer of Venezuelan oil. The refinery can process 1.4 million barrels of crude oil a day, so it's a valuable exporter of diesel fuel for global markets. The refinery has also been a major buyer of Russian Urals crude, making it a significant part of India's energy relationship with Russia.

US President Donald Trump has put the value of the project at $300 billion (€258 billion) and presented it as a way to help reduce the US trade deficit with India. Whether the project moves forward, and on what terms, will be closely watched.

Since taking office for a second time in 2025, Trump has gone back and forth on tariff threats with India over what he considers to be unfair trade practices, along with India's continued purchases of Russian oil amid the war in Ukraine.

In February 2026, Trump announced a "trade deal" that slashed US tariffs on Indian goods, in exchange for India lowering trade barriers and halting purchases of Russian oil. Trump had claimed that Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi promised to stop buying Russian oil. However, this has never been confirmed by New Delhi.

While maritime data from Kpler, a commodities and energy-market intelligence company, showed Indian refiners briefly diversified supply away from Russia to avoid tightening sanctions, India's deep reliance on discounted Russian crude continues to be a cornerstone of its energy strategy. Russia remains India's top supplier of crude oil.

In mid-September, Trump signed the "Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act," imposing 100% tariffs on top buyers of Russian oil and gas, which includes India.

However, last week, just days before the legislation was due to go into effect, Trump signed an executive order pausing the sanctions.

Before traveling to India, Rubio was in Uzbekistan for a meeting of the "C5+1" diplomatic platform comprising the US and five Central Asian countries. In remarks on the sidelines, Rubio said trade talks with India are taking place with the sanctions waivers in mind.

"We have had to waive sanctions over the past few months, looking at the global supplies, looking at global prices, and the impact it was having on other countries," he said.

"We're trying to finalize a trade deal with India right now. We're very close to doing that," Rubio added.

In an interview with Indian broadcaster NDTV, US Ambassador to India Sergio Gor said that Jamnagar could be a valuable source of diesel for the US.

Foreign policy analyst C Raja Mohan of Jindal Global University in India told DW that Rubio skipping a visit to the Indian capital in favor of Mumbai and Jamnagar should not be considered a diplomatic snub, as the refinery has international importance amid the global energy crunch brought on by the wars in Ukraine and the Middle East.

He said Jamnagar is an "important node in global trade" because it processes Russian oil and serves as a conduit for refined Russian petroleum products reaching Western markets.

Although Trump's sanctions reprieve offers New Delhi temporary relief, the underlying threat remains. Washington retains the option of imposing punitive tariffs, leaving Russian oil as a potential source of leverage in wider trade negotiations.

Former Indian ambassador to the US, Meera Shankar, cautioned against reading too much into Rubio's decision to skip Delhi amid political criticism in India.

"The focus of his current trip is on energy, and he is visiting the Reliance refinery in Jamnagar," she told DW. "The high price of fuel, particularly diesel, has become a political liability for the Trump administration, which wants to be seen taking action on this issue."

Diesel prices have skyrocketed around the world following the Iran war and the ensuing disruption of supply from the Middle East. In waiving Russia sanctions, Trump has tried to blame Ukrainian attacks on Russian oil facilities as being the cause of high prices.

Shankar pointed to Reliance's proposed Texas refinery, noting that the state is "one of the states in play" in the upcoming midterm elections in the US.

The two governments continue to describe their ties as a comprehensive global strategic partnership spanning defence, trade, technology and energy.

Analyst Mohan argued that describing the US-India relationship as uniquely transactional was misleading.

"Every nation ultimately acts out of national self-interest, rather than pure altruism or shared values," he said.

The current friction, in his view, is part of a normal negotiation between two sovereign powers, not proof that the partnership was failing.

Besides Russian oil, another potential area of complication is the Trump administration suspending a scheme allowing US companies to sponsor skilled foreign workers.

On October 8, the Trump administration suspended new and pending applications under the US Permanent Labor Certification (PERM) program at several technology companies amid investigations into alleged visa fraud and the displacement of US workers.

The process is a key step for employers seeking permanent residency for foreign staff, including Indian professionals.

Harsh Pant, vice president for studies and foreign policy at the Delhi-based Observer Research Foundation (ORF), said Trump's own words and actions had damaged ties despite regular high-level engagement.

"Even as he calls Prime Minister Modi a good friend, he has damaged the relationship by targeting India, and his supporters have been targeting Indians," Pant told DW.

## From delivery driver to global fame: A Korean writer&apos;s journey

https://p.dw.com/p/5OIWTThe book was a massive success, securing publication in 15 languages, including English, German, Spanish and ChineseImage: ClayHouse/Michael Joseph/AceAdvertisementFor ten years, publishers rejected You Yeong-Gwang's book. Yet before it was even printed in South Korea, the once-abandoned manuscript had international publishers flocking to secure translation rights.

"I'm so grateful that the book has now been published in more than 20 countries. Even more astonishingly, it also appeared on the Der Spiegel bestseller list in Germany," he told DW while attending the 2026 Frankfurt Book Fair.

For a debut novel by an unknown author, it was a massive success, securing publication in 15 languages, including English, German, Spanish and traditional Chinese.

"The Rainfall Market" tells the story of a mysterious, hidden shop that only opens when it rains. In the shop, visitors can exchange their deepest unhappiness for a ticket to a brand-new destiny. The plot revolves around Serin, a disheartened young girl who stumbles into the marketplace run by whimsical spirits, finding a warm, comforting space.

"In fact, Serin's story is my story," author You said. "For a long time, I even wondered how wonderful it would be if I could sell my unhappiness and use the money to buy happiness."

"I had to care for my mother, who had been ill for a long time, and I had also been responsible for supporting my family financially for many years. Life felt incredibly heavy," he reflected.

"But when I was writing, I could immerse myself in the world of the story and, even if only for a short while, forget about those circumstances. That's why writing made me so happy. I truly enjoyed it."

The story draws heavily on traditional Korean folklore, serving as a quintessential "healing fiction" tale in which its young protagonist must weigh the true cost of seemingly perfect lives.

"When I was writing, I wasn't consciously trying to write healing fiction. At the time, I didn't even really know that such a label existed."

He initially planned to publish a book independently and started crowdfunding. But as the funding failed, he used all his savings and printed 200 copies, which bookstores refused to take. In a month, he only sold five copies. "I thought that's it. I really have to give up now.”

"At the time, I had undergone eye surgery and due to complications, I couldn't see, so I started working at a call center, where I could work using my voice." You said. "During the week, I worked at a call center, and on weekends I went to a computer academy. I learned how to make books on my own for a few years."

While running one of the crowdfunding campaigns, he started working as a food delivery driver. "The round trip between my home and the area where I delivered food took about four hours each day. So I wrote on my phone's memo app while traveling on the subway."

"Even when I was going through very difficult circumstances, I was able to keep writing because the moments when I was writing were so joyful. For a little while, I could forget about the difficult situation I was in," You explained.

"I gathered those pieces of writing together and launched what I intended to be my final crowdfunding campaign," he said.

"To my amazement, the campaign succeeded, and I was able to publish the book commercially for the first time," he said.

Following the crowdfunding success, the novel was acquired by Clayhouse, a prominent South Korean publishing company.

Before it was even published, he had received offers from publishers in five countries and signed international publishing contracts.

And in the first month after publication, the original South Korean language edition sold 30,000 copies across all formats, ranking as the bestselling e-book and third-bestselling physical book.

"I still find it hard to believe that all of this has happened and that I'm here at the Frankfurt Book Fair. I'm incredibly grateful and happy," said You.

Korea's newest soft power is K-literature, which sold about 1.2 million translated copies abroad in 2025 for the second consecutive year.

"When readers closed the book, I wanted them to feel a little warmth in their hearts," he said.

Although Korean literature was first known for darker, more pragmatic books about societal realities, K-comfort fiction is the child of South Korea's pressure-cooker modern society. It offers an answer to the human need for escapism and a transient antidote to the reality of a hyper-competitive education system, brutal job market and intense corporate pressure.

"When I was writing, I was going through a very difficult time myself, and I wished someone would comfort me. So, I thought, why don't I try reading something like that myself? Why don't I write something that can comfort me?" You wondered.

With burnout on the rise globally, more readers are seeking soul-soothing literature with exhausted protagonists they can relate to. Usually without a need to defeat a villain, the stories follow protagonists with a deep desire for rest, community and emotional repair.

The whimsical fiction aspect is the missing element allowing them to step away from reality. The genre originally gained traction through indie productions, online crowdfunding and endorsements from high-profile K-pop artists.

Despite the Korean context, this style of storytelling quickly went global, capturing different international audiences, including those already familiar with cozy K-dramas or those simply looking for a gentle refuge from the anxieties of the modern world.

Last year, the bestselling Korean novel abroad was "Welcome to the Hyunam-dong Bookshop," according to the Literature Translation Institute of Korea. The novel belongs to the same genre as "The Rainfall Market."

While readers embrace the genre, critics view it with literary skepticism. They point out that it relies heavily on a predictable, repetitive formula, often built around a single magical space, with simplistic prose and low-stakes conflicts that prioritize cozy vibes over deep character development.

Reviewing "The Rainfall Market" for the Korea Times, Peter Juhl, a literary critic, described healing fiction as light, feel-good stories and argued the boom reflects a deliberate effort to write global novels for international markets.

"Of course I'm happy that my book is receiving so much love and that I'm able to write. But more than anything, I hope my mother gets better and regains her health," You said after mentioning that his mother underwent surgery a week before his arrival at the book fair.

## The homeless man living in front of a London mega-mansion

https://p.dw.com/p/5N9o5Anders Fernstedt has built a home outside one of London's most expensive propertiesImage: Dan Redding/DWAdvertisementThere's a lot of stopping and staring in Knightsbridge. This part of central London is within a stone's throw of Buckingham Palace, Hyde Park and Harrods, bringing inquisitive tourists in droves.

While it may not divert quite as many eyeballs as the seat of the British monarchy, a curious collection of plants, garden umbrellas, shopping bags and children's toys filling a building's entrance causes many a curious glance.

The peculiar arrangement on a grand portico is what Anders Fernstedt, a man from Sweden who fell on hard times, has been calling home for more than three years already.

That building hosting his makeshift setup was, just a few years ago, London's most expensive house.

The mega-mansion at 2-8A Rutland Gate sold in 2020 for a then-record £210 million (€244 million, $278 million) to Hui Ka Yan, the founder of China's Evergrande group, who was sentenced to life in prison last month.

Hui never moved in and the house was registered under his wife's name. The freezing of her assets means it can't be seized by liquidators and the house is in a state of limbo.

So too was Fernstedt when he first stumbled across it. "I just thought, it would be a shelter for the night. I'm an avid camper, like an amateur botanist. I'm not afraid of nature," he told DW.

Fernstedt has never been inside the property, which appears to be in a state of disrepair. Instead, he gradually built his home in the portico, where grand columns guard the 45 rooms, indoor swimming pool and 116 windows. Year round he sleeps under the portico roof and a large umbrella which provides a hidden nook in which he's placed mattresses and bedding.

"I haven't had a bad night's sleep for as long as I can remember. And that goes for four seasons. It's not cold in the winter, and because I don't have walls, I get the breeze in the heat," he said.

"My conceit when I started was that sort of fake dream. Oh, I'm just a boy, my parents are in the house, everything's normal, I just asked them for permission to camp on the balcony."

Fernstedt was raised by his mother in Sweden before embarking on a range of careers at home and in the US, building a name for himself in the tech world.

In 2016, he moved to England where he worked as a journalist and fact checker at publications including The Economist, before work dried up around the time of the COVID pandemic.

A share in a tech company from his days in the US gave Anders a nest egg he relied on. But it couldn't last forever and he found himself living in a tent and then temporary accommodation, where he experienced violence and brushed up against people with severe addictions.

"Perhaps in this contemporary world, the biggest curiosity about my lifestyle is I'm completely off money," he said.

Fernstedt has built relationships with bakeries, cafes and florists in which he gets unused food, flowers for his home and use of power. He also makes use of a homeless charity in nearby Victoria for showers, clothing donations and socializing. But he's also made himself a broadly accepted part of one of London's most affluent neighborhoods.

"This is a very sophisticated neighborhood. I'd stayed for a few weeks and I knew everybody knew that I was here. And I thought, how can I sort of convey my presence without being present? And I thought, my constituency has to be the neighbors' children and grandchildren. Because if they plead my case at the kitchen table, my prospects improve," he said, by way of explaining the toys that line his abode.

"It is, in a way, a squirrel's nut collection," he added, when asked if he's worried about leaving his possessions out in the open. "I have so many nuts that I won't notice when something disappears."

There are some neighbors who do object to his presence, but Anders does his best to keep his home neat and his life quiet. Indeed, many others feel his presence prevents the house being a target for thieves or vandals. Overall though, he's found the community welcoming.

"Sometimes they introduce themselves asking what might I want or need. And my stock answer being: 'well, what you just gave me, a little bit of normal time, right? And neighborly banter'," he said. "Sometimes I'll be given food and very elegant Italian dinner jackets and clothes have arrived on a few occasions."

Not all homeless people in London have found such a stable lifestyle. Homeless charity Crisis estimates that there are over 200,000 people classified as homeless in England's capital, where "4,392 people were recorded sleeping rough in London between April and June, up from 4% on the same time last year. This is up 58% compared to 10 years ago."

According to government figures, there were 105,138 empty homes in London in 2025, or 1 in every 37 homes. Andy Burnham, who became British Prime Minister in July, has since vowed to offer "every rough sleeper in England a route off the streets by Christmas."

With such an enormous price tag, even after six years of being abandoned, Fernstedt knows that the house he lives at could potentially become untenable at virtually any moment.

But over the last few years, it's given him something he craved when pitching up his tent or being moved from one scary temporary residence to the next.

"I'm no longer really worried that I'll cycle around the corner and everything will be gone. Because I've been here so long, I'm free of worrying now, and if we worry too much, we won't be happy."

On October 27, Israelis will vote in an election that could oust Netanyahu from power or cement his grip on the country for the coming years. Political scientists are already calling it Israel's first AI election. Parties have been deploying AI across every aspect of campaigning — from creating outrageous videos aimed at stirring controversy to robocalling voters and unleashing bots online.

"There is a scenario where, in a strategic point shortly before the election, AI could or would be used in ways that would steer controversy that could tilt the election results," said Anat Ben-David, a media researcher leading the Ze Be AI project at the Open University of Israel.

Ben-David and her research team have been tracking all of the content posted on social media by politicians since the end of July. By mid-October, they had collected more than 44,000 posts across Facebook, Instagram, X and TikTok and found that over 14% of the material used AI in some way.

A hybrid approach was most common, with AI being deployed for a part or segments of an ad: in images, music, subtitles, logos or full-fledged deepfakes.

Some fringe parties use the greatest ratio of AI in their content. But Likud is the winner by far when it comes to sheer numbers of AI use in content analyzed by the team. Netanyahu's party is followed his left-wing rivals, the Democrats, and then Eisenkot's party, Yashar — though at far lower numbers.

Israel's Central Elections Committee issued a new rule in July mandating that all AI-generated or -manipulated content in election campaigning be labeled. Beyond that, there are few limits.

"There's no AI Act, no Digital Services Act, and the privacy law is very outdated," Ben-David said. "Then you see new [AI] uses that would be impossible to see in Europe."

Yair Golan, head of the Democrats, has been trying to capitalize on popular internet trends. ## What Earth&apos;s sixth mass extinction event means for humans

https://p.dw.com/p/5OKVuFrom tiny bees to big whales, all of the players on nature's team are essentialImage: Yonhap News/IMAGOAdvertisementFrom the tiniest microbes in the human gut to giant whales in the ocean, biodiversity is the sum of all life on the planet. It's another word for nature. And because nature is like a team, every player has a job.

When various species from trees to animals work together, they regulate weather, maintain soil health, purify water and support food production. These benefits are known as ecosystem services and they underpin human wellbeing.

"It is really an abstract concept, but we experience it absolutely every day," said Rebecca Shaw, chief scientist at conservation group WWF "And we almost never think about it when nature is functioning."

But as it declines, she and other scientists are calling on the world to really think about it. Because biodiversity is struggling as humans push ecosystems to the brink through intensive farming, burning fossil fuels, and pollution from sources like plastic and chemicals.

Over the past 52 years, monitored vertebrate populations have collapsed by an average of 73%, while freshwater ecosystems are faring worst with a drop of 85%, according to WWF's recent Living Planet report.

It tallies with other studies that show a million animal and plant species face extinction unless humanity reduces its pressure on nature.

"It is a really steep decline," said Shaw. "What it's telling us is that nature is in trouble, and it's an early warning indicator of the fabric of life that we depend on for freshwater and food and for clean air is unraveling."

Some 200 countries are gathering in Yerevan, Armenia over the next two weeks for COP17, the latest UN biodiversity summit, to advance commitments to reverse nature's unraveling by 2030. But progress towards those targets remains insufficient, with funding and implementation among the biggest challenges.

Take wetlands as an example. Some 85% of these vital ecosystems disappeared between 1700 and 2000. One study from Canada's University of Waterloo found that habitats like marshes and swamps in Southern Ontario provide C$4.2 billion (€2.63 billion) worth of water filtration services each year, removing nutrients like phosphorus and keeping drinking water sources clean.

The study found that as they disappear, water quality worsens, which massively increases the cost of treating it.

Another example is the decline in pollinators like bees and bats. More than 75% of global food crop types rely in part on animal pollination.

At the same time, soil and ecosystem degradation coupled with storms and heat waves linked to climate change are already damaging harvests. A recent UK government report stated that every critical ecosystem is on a "pathway to collapse," warning that nature's decline posed a grave threat to global food supply and geopolitical stability.

"The UK, like many other countries, relies on imports for much of the food and so is reliant on ecosystems around the world," said Louise McRae, a research fellow at the Zoological Society of London, who also contributed to WWF's Living Planet report. "This is why we need to care about global biodiversity."

Nature also provides the basis for many modern human medicines, with some 50% coming from natural compounds.

Penicillin, the first antibiotic, is produced by a mold and is estimated to have saved over 500 million lives. Before its discovery, a person could die from a simple cut. Around 70% of cancer drugs come from natural products or synthetic compounds "inspired by nature," according to the World Health Organisation (WHO).

To return to the "nature as a team" metaphor, if a player disappears, sometimes a species with similar functions in the ecosystem can jump in as a substitute. But not always, particularly if it's a "keystone species." They play a disproportionately large role in their environments, and their disappearance has significant knock-on effects.

The oyster is one such species. Wild oyster populations are facing massive declines, while 85% of their reefs have been lost worldwide. An adult oyster filters up to 50 gallons of water a day.

"No one would ever think, they did anything but sit in a shell and be slimy," said Shaw. "But you lose oysters, you can lose water filtration and clean water, you can lose the habitat for many other species that we interact with or sometimes consume."

And, said Shaw, the humble oyster protects coastal communities from storm surges. If you lose it "you lose the coastal protection."

The loss of a single species can also lead to more disease. One study suggests a decline in red foxes in North America is linked to the spread of Lyme disease, a tick-borne illness that also impacts humans.

Extinction is a natural process. But the planet has experienced five mass extinctions when a large proportion of species globally disappeared over a short geological period. The rate at which species are disappearing today, suggests Earth is experiencing a sixth event, driven largely by human activity, according to scientists.

Both McRae and Shaw called the declines extremely worrying, but said they shouldn't provoke despair.

Though she acknowledges that the underlying global trend is moving in the wrong direction and cannot be ignored, McRae says "the statistics are there to just motivate us to action rather than to cause alarm."

And the Living Planet report also highlights conservation successes, showing that nature can recover.

The number of green sea turtles, for example, had fallen significantly, but has now bounced back. This is thanks to a massive worldwide effort to protect nesting beaches and changes to fishing practices that reduce accidental capture.

Sea otters are another example. Prized for their fur, in the 1800s they were hunted to near extinction on the US Pacific Coast. When they disappeared, it meant sea urchins had no predators, leaving them free to decimate kelp forests.

That matters because as well as providing habitat for other species, these giant underwater groves protect coastlines from storm surges and absorb carbon, making them important for climate protection.

Following a ban on commercial hunting, sea otters have also rebounded, helping kelp forests recover.

"You can see that ripple effect that really had an obvious and a measurable impact. But critically, it can be reversed," said McRae, adding that biodiversity loss and climate change are intertwined and exacerbate each other but have joint solutions.

"We're learning how to turn things around at very small scales," said Shaw. "Now the real challenge is to turn things around at very big scale so we can see it in the global indicators."
