## Russian birth tourism to Brazil increases

https://p.dw.com/p/5MmkiAll women have access to public healthcare in a Brazil, regardless of visa or residency status Image: Felipe Dana/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementYulia and Dmitri (whose names have been changed for reasons of safety) flew from Russia to Brazil this summer so that their child could be born there. Yulia was 32 weeks pregnant at the time of travel.

Her daughter was born at a private clinic in Santos, a city in southern Brazil. Anybody born in the country has an automatic right to Brazilian nationality. The family is currently preparing all the paperwork.

"We've achieved our minimum goal," said Dmitri. "With a Brazilian passport, our daughter will be able to travel without a visa to almost any country in the world. She won't have to apply for an expensive and time-consuming Schengen visa. We want her to be able to decide for herself where she studies, works or lives."

He said that a colleague of his had traveled to Brazil in early August with his pregnant wife for the same reasons.

Yulia and Dmitri said that they had spent the equivalent of about €10,000 (about $11,500) during the first six weeks of their stay in Brazil, which included the costs for several days of treatment for their daughter in intensive care. Dmitri quit his job at a bank for the trip and hopes to find work again after the family returns to their home in the Russian capital Moscow.

However, they are only planning to return once all the documents for their daughter are ready and that could take months. Other Russian families have decided to stay in Brazil permanently.

According to organizations and lawyers that provide support to Russians in Brazil, there has been an increase in immigration from Russia, particularly since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, after which visa requirements for Russian nationals entering certain countries were tightened.

Birth tourism to Brazil has also increased since Argentina, which used to be one of the main destinations for people wanting their children to be born in a country that automatically grants citizenship to newborns, tightened its regulations. People who want their children to obtain Argentinian citizenship now have to have resided in Argentina for at least two years. Since 2025, the access of people who do not have a permanent residence permit to free healthcare has also been restricted.

According to the Russian news outlet RBC, more than 7,000 Russians applied for a residence permit in Argentina in 2023. By mid-2025, the number had decreased to about 1,800.

Meanwhile, almost 500 Brazilian passports were issued to Russians in 2024 and more than 600 the following year. More than half of the new citizens were women.

The parents of a child born in Brazil can apply for a residence permit, which they can obtain within a year, under certain circumstances.

"The events of 2022 certainly played a role, but we had already toyed with the idea of giving birth abroad," Yana told DW. "A lot can happen in Russia. I always wanted a second passport and to give birth to my child in another country. When I became pregnant unexpectedly, all we had to do was decide which country made sense."

Yana gave birth to a boy at a public hospital in Florianopolis, the capital of the southern state of Santa Catarina, and the family stayed in Brazil for about three years.

"It was important to get passports for us and our child. I really enjoyed living there, and I learned Portuguese," Yana said, adding that she and her husband have lived as "digital nomads" in various countries over the years.

Even though they no longer live in Brazil, they want their son to have a connection to his birth country: "We read Portuguese books and talk a lot about Brazil. I want him to be able to travel without a visa and remain open to the world."

One of the largest Russian communities in Brazil lives in Florianopolis, and it's growing. According to the local health authorities, 119 Russian women took advantage of prenatal consultations in the first six months of this year. In all of 2025, it was 136 Russian women.

A lawyer who preferred to remain anonymous told DW that Brazil neither expressly prohibited nor permitted foreign nationals to enter the country for the purpose of giving birth. Often pregnant women do not disclose their reason for traveling to Brazil at border control. And once they are in the country they have access to free public hospitals and medical facilities regardless of their immigration status.

As Russian birth tourism to Brazil increases, a whole industry has emerged. Often, recent Russian immigrants to Brazil act as intermediaries and help others find housing or medical care. They provide assistance with complicated administrative procedures or the language. Depending on the level of care, the cost can reach into the thousands of dollars.

However, providers of such services, lawyers, and volunteers report stricter controls by Brazilian authorities, particularly in cities with larger Russian communities, such as Florianopolis. They say authorities are requesting more documents than in the past.

"The police are conducting more thorough checks to verify whether people actually live at the addresses they provide," one lawyer said, explaining that in the past, false addresses had been given by people hoping to obtain residence permits more quickly.

But DW did not find any evidence to suggest that Russians were being denied entry to Brazil en masse.

In late July, the Russian Telegram channel Baza reported that 10 pregnant women had been "deported" from Russia. The information was later picked up by various media outlets. However, the Brazilian police denied the veracity of the report to DW, explaining that decisions to deny somebody entry to the country were always made on a case-by-case basis, and that pregnancy played no role in the process.

DW was only able to find one documented case of a pregnant Russian woman being initially refused entry into Brazil. She was allowed to enter the country after a lawyer intervened. According to the court records, the Brazilian border authorities had doubts as to how the Russian national would be financing her stay in Brazil.

## A war-fatigued and anxious Russia picks a new parliament

https://p.dw.com/p/5MmmuThe upcoming Duma elections are hard to miss when you walk down Moscow's streetsImage: Ramil Sitdikov/REUTERSAdvertisementRussians are increasingly anxious. Nearly half of those surveyed by state pollster FOM say a sense of alarm is the prevailing mood among the people around them.

According to data by independent pollster ExtremeScan, 60% of Russians believe that the war against Ukraine has had negative consequences for their day-to-day lives.

"Anxiety and war fatigue are driven by increasing uncertainty," Margarita Zavadskaya, a sociologist and senior research fellow at the Finnish Institute of International Affairs in Helsinki, told DW.

She says this year has seen a shift in public opinion. Rising prices, internet crackdowns, fuel shortages caused by Ukrainian strikes on oil refineries and rumors of another mobilization drive are making it harder for Russians to plan for the future. Some are even leaving the country, afraid they could be drafted after the elections.

Sociologist and the founder of ExtremeScan Elena Koneva said people were starting to ask the question: "How much does this war cost me and my family? And am I ready to pay this price? "

From September 18 to 20, Russians are voting for a new parliament (the State Duma) for the first time since 2022. It's the first parliamentary election to take place in Russia during the Ukraine war. And although there are unlikely to be any surprises in the official results, the Kremlin will be closely monitoring turnout.

"The Kremlin is confident it can fabricate whatever figures it needs. The question is how high of a price it will have to pay," said Zavadskaya.

Although elections in authoritarian systems like that of Russian President Vladimir Putin have little to do with democratic change, they still serve an important purpose. They are a reality check on whether the population remains loyal. And for Putin, that is especially important right now.

"They are crucial for him because they demonstrate that the majority supports the main party, the party of war, and that he has legitimacy for further escalation and the continuation of this war," Russian opposition politician in exile Ilya Yashin told DW.

In the fifth year of the war in Ukraine, there is little sign the Kremlin is willing to end the conflict. United Russia, a party de facto led by Putin, is campaigning on a platform that lists victory in the war among its priorities. Its candidate list is headed by people presented as veterans of the war against Ukraine.

"The silent majority just wants the war to end," opposition politician Yulia Galyamina, who still lives in Russia, said in a recent interview with online media-outlet Bereg. Most of these people still support Putin, but they are becoming increasingly anti-war, she said.

That mood is also reflected in polling. Six in 10 Russians favor peace negotiations with Ukraine, according to independent pollster Levada Center. Recent Kremlin hints at a new round of peace talks in October may be a tactic to calm the electorate, Zavadskaya said.

The liberal opposition party is the only registered political force opposing the war in Ukraine.

Although its rating was below 5% in July, the party has seen a surge in popularity since then. Many people, especially younger Russians, are joining Yabloko and volunteering.

According to the independent pollster ExtremeScan, 18% of Russians would have voted for Yabloko. But they will not be able to: In a recent court decision, Yabloko was barred from running in the parliamentary elections.

"This support for Yabloko is, in fact, a demonstration of people's attitude toward the war," Koneva told DW.

Now, with Yabloko out of the race, are there still ways for Russians to express that attitude?

Yulia Navalnaya, the widow of Alexei Navalny, and opposition politician in exile Maxim Katz are urging supporters to back any party except United Russia in an effort to reduce the ruling party's result in the election.

Yashin says neither choice matters. What matters, he says, is showing up at polling stations at noon on Sept. 20.

"These queues will be a visible expression of public discontent," he said. "No need for flags, banners, or symbols. Everyone will know: the queues at noon are made up of people who do not want the war to continue."

DW Russian asked its readers anonymously whether they plan to vote in the upcoming election. Most of those who responded said they would follow Navalnaya's and Katz's advice.

"Elections have long become a farce and a political spectacle. But I will vote because I want to show that I have a position," one user said.

Others decided to stay away from the elections. One respondent wrote: "I don't want to legitimize Putin's regime."

## Alexander Ovechkin backs Putin party in campaign video

On Monday he took his first few strides back on the ice in the United States as he starts gearing up for a new National Hockey League (NHL) season with the Washington Capitals, his 22nd in the world's best league.

Before leaving his homeland, where he spent his summer break, he did make his contribution to the election campaign, however, appearing in a video promoting the ruling United Russia party, which steadfastly supports President Vladimir Putin.

In the video, which has been widely shared on social media, Ovechkin doesn't speak but can be clearly seen in the third row of a group of supporters behind Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov, who is heading the United Russia list for the parliamentary vote.

Ovechkin's appearance has faced criticism, including from Ukrainian skeleton pilot Vladyslav Heraskevych, who was disqualified from the 2026 Olympic Games for insisting on wearing a helmet depicting athletes killed in Russia's war in Ukraine.

This is certainly not the first time that Ovechkin has expressed his support for Putin, with whom he shares his profile photo on his Instagram account.

And, at times, Ovechkin's public support has appeared even more active than in the current United Russia video. Back in 2017, Ovechkin used his Instagram account to announce the creation of "PutinTeam" a group to help support Putin in the 2018 presidential campaign.

"I have never hidden my feelings about our president; I have always openly supported him. I am certain there are many of us who support Vladimir Putin! So let's unite and show the world a strong, united Russia!" he wrote.

"Being part of such a team is a source of pride for me; it feels just like pulling on the Russian national team jersey, knowing the whole country is rooting for you."

The creation of PutinTeam came three years after Russia had invaded and seized the Ukrainian territory Crimea in 2014, a move that was met with widespread condemnation in the international community. Later that year, Ovechkin appeared to express support for the operation, posting a photo of himself on Instagram holding up a sign with the message: "#savechildrenfromfascism."

"Our grandfathers and grandmothers witnessed all the horrors of fascism! We will not let it happen in our time," he wrote.

Putin has painted the Ukrainian authorities as "neo-Nazi" or fascist, in particular when justifying Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, launched in February 2022. What Putin calls a "special military operation" raised questions in the minds of many about how this might affect Ovechkin's view of the man he had always so publicly supported.

On February 25, 2022, a day after Russia launched the invasion, a reporter covering the Washington Capitals asked Ovechkin if he still supported Putin.

"Well, he's my president," Ovechkin replied. "But how I said, I'm not in politics, I'm an athlete."

"Please, no more war. It doesn't matter who's in the war — Russia, Ukraine, different countries," he said.

"Alex Ovechkin is a Russian citizen who ‌lives in Moscow with his family during the offseason and will ‌live ​there following his retirement," team spokesperson Sergey Kocharov said in the statement.

"This summer, while in Russia, he was asked by his country to participate in a video campaign. As the longest-tenured athlete in Washington, D.C., and a pillar of the community, his focus and ours is on the season ahead and the ⁠continued impact ​he makes on the ice and in our ​city."

When Ovechkin was made available to reporters two days later, his message was essentially the same.

"About the video; I'm Russian. Once I finish playing hockey I am probably going to live there, my kids going to live there, my family. So I'm for peace and love and I hope everthing is going to be okay. My home is Russia," he said.

Asked by a reporter how he came to be involved in the campaign video, Ovechkin replied: "They asked me to do this and I said yes. Simple as that."

Whatever you may think of his political views, Ovechkin is without a doubt one of the best players to ever lace on a pair of skates.

Now, back on the ice in Arlington, Virginia, ahead of the Washington Capitals' training camp, "Great8" is focused on getting his aging body (he turned 41 on Thursday) ready to compete for what would be a second Stanley Cup for both him and the Caps.

For much of the offseason, Ovechkin had left open whether he would return for another year. After all, he has achieved just about everything you can in the NHL, having broken Wayne Gretzky's career goal-scoring record two seasons ago. Going into the 2025-26 campaign he has 929 goals in 1,573 regular season games. In July though, after a conversation with his wife, he decided to come back for one more year in Washington – and he hasn't ruled out extending his career beyond that.

"I'm going to be 41. I've had a lot of pressure on my shoulders over the years," he said. "I'm going to focus on the game and helping my team to win," he told reporters after his first skate. "I'm still a good player and can still help the team to win."

His numbers support that last assertion, having scored 32 goals in 2025-26. Only two other players have ever scored more over the age of 40.

"I can't wait for the season," he said. "On paper, you can see our team is one of the best teams but now we have to work for the Stanley Cup."

The bookies disagree, however, with some putting the Capitals way down at 18 (of 32) on their list of Stanley Cup candidates for 2027.

On the other hand, the prospect of United Russia's winning this weekend's parliamentary elections, the first since the launch of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, appears a safe bet.

## Judges: As AfD ascends, Germany must bolster justice system

https://p.dw.com/p/5MnHRGerman judges want to make sure state governments cannot abuse prosecution powers to influence criminal casesImage: Jennifer Brückner/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementThe official association of German judges, the Deutscher Richterbund (DRB), has called on the federal government to tighten up the law to stop state governments from abusing prosecution powers to potentially influence criminal cases.

The prospect that the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) could take over the government in Saxony-Anhalt, which appears highly likely after the party's resounding election win in early September, has raised fears that "extremists in government" could steer "criminal investigations in one direction or another through directives," according to the DRB.

The AfD, sections of which are considered a threat to the German constitutional democratic order by domestic intelligence agencies, is set to take control of a state government for the first time ever in Saxony-Anhalt. This has sparked concern in the judicial system that the party could, for instance, push prosecutors away from pursuing cases against right-wing extremists.

"The top priority now is to prevent the misuse of public prosecutors' offices in Germany for partisan political purposes," DRB executive director Sven Rebehn said in a statement emailed to DW. "Currently, the law allows state justice ministries to interfere even in individual criminal proceedings. While there is a political tradition of self-restraint when issuing directives to prosecutors, this tradition can be abandoned at any time and offers no effective protection."

In Germany, unlike in many other countries, state prosecutors, along with the police, are part of the executive branch of government. This puts them under the authority of the state or federal Interior Ministries, and subject to parliamentary oversight. This dependence is seen by many constitutional lawyers as a democratic principle, as it means that prosecutors' actions can be checked by a democratically elected body. But others see it as a problem, as it opens the possibility of political influence.

German Interior Ministries, both at the federal level and in the 16 states, have the power to issue directives to prosecutors in individual cases. For instance, if some evidence has been supplied by an intelligence agency, the Interior Ministry can issue a directive to the prosecutor to keep that fact out of a court case, as disclosing it could compromise the work of the agency.

The German domestic intelligence agency, the Verfassungsschutz, which reports to the Interior Ministry, has long been a focus of the AfD's ire, as members of the party consider it politicized. The agency is charged with keeping tabs on extremist tendencies in the country. By the same token, German Justice Ministries can make a ruling over how prosecutors interpret a certain law, or how investigations are to be pursued.

One such directive was made in 2015, when the then federal justice minister, Heiko Maas, told the federal prosecutor to withdraw a report that found that state secrets had been compromised in a particular case. Maas denied that this had been the directive, and federal prosecutor Harald Range was dismissed in the ensuing power struggle.

As things stand, there is still no way to check the ministry's directives, which some see as a problem. Berlin's state prosecutor, Margarete Koppers, also has strong opinions on this point, and sees the danger as acute: "The rule of law is at risk when its rules are politically abused," a spokesperson for Koppers told DW by email. "A look at European countries that have, at various times, been under authoritarian-populist leadership — and where the structures of the rule of law were subsequently eroded — shows that this is not an unrealistic scenario." When the far-right Law and Justice (PiS) party took over in Poland, it triggered a constitutional crisis by trying to appoint judges to the constitutional court. The government of Viktor Orban in Hungary also tried to intervene in the judicial system.

Both the DRB and Koppers believe that removing the ministries' right to take directives is long overdue, but others in the judicial field are not so sure.

"I can understand the fear that a sword like this in the hands of the wrong people makes the executive branch a danger," said Gül Pinar, a criminal lawyer based in Hamburg who in 2020 offered expert testimony in the Bundestag on the issue.

Pinar said reform was necessary, but mainly because ministries' directives are not documented. "The people who are affected by the criminal procedures don't know that certain actions taken by the prosecutors stem from a directive, from the government, or the federal prosecutors, or the Interior Ministry," she told DW.

If the directives were documented, Pinar said, it would be possible for lawyers to examine whether they were legally sound, or whether they overstepped their authority. "We have to insert new mechanisms to check what is happening," she said.

"But the solution is not to make the state prosecutors independent," Pinar said. Hardly anyone in the judiciary, she said, doubts that the right to issue instructions stems from the principle of democracy. But she added that there were many voices, particularly amongst judges and public prosecutors, who advocate for an independent public prosecution service.

The current federal government has not shown any inclination to change the law. In a statement to the Legal Tribune Online, the Federal Justice Ministry said that the current laws provided plenty of safeguards: Ministries are prohibited from issuing "unlawful" directives, and prosecutors from following them.

In fact, according to the ministry, keeping prosecutors in the executive branch is important, because it means that they are more accountable and subject to parliamentary oversight.

The relatively unique position of Germany's state prosecutors has come up at the European level before: In 2019, the European Court of Justice warned that European arrest warrants could be compromised by political influence because German state prosecutors were not independent enough: European arrest warrants are supposed to be issued by state prosecutors, but, in Germany, only judges are allowed issue them.

Pinar said allowing state prosecutors to issue arrest warrants was "not compatible with our legal system." In fact, she said, the German system safeguards more civil freedoms. "In Germany, withdrawing someone's freedom is always under the authority of the judge," she said. "I think it would better if other countries adapted their systems to ours, so that the executive branch can decide on the freedom, but was under a judge's authority."

## As global crises grow, Germany cuts development aid

https://p.dw.com/p/5MYVINGOs took to the street outside the chancellery to demonstrate against cutting development aidImage: Giulia Saudelli/DWAdvertisementGerman Development Minister Reem Alabali Radovan opened her speech in the lower house of parliament, the Bundestag, with a litany of catastrophes and suffering: floods in Nepal, heat waves, forests engulfed in flames, rivers running dry, and crop failures caused by drought.

"Millions of people are facing hunger," said Radovan, of the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD). "And one thing is clear: The climate crisis is threatening the entire world. No country can tackle it alone."

Radovan opened the debate on the federal government's proposed 2027 budget for her ministry with a stark assessment. The budget is set to shrink for the fifth consecutive year, falling from just over €10 billion to just under €9.5 billion ($10.9 billion). In 2022, it stood at nearly €14 billion, meaning that the ministry's budget has been reduced by roughly one-third over the past half decade.

How does this sharp decline square with the dramatic picture painted by Germany's development minister? She pointed to a strategy she unveiled in January, when she declared that "development policy will become an even more important pillar of German security, alongside diplomacy and defense."

Aid organizations reacted skeptically to this policy shift when it was first announced, and their concerns have deepened in light of the latest funding cuts. "The renewed reductions in funding for international cooperation are further exacerbating an already critical situation," said Michael Herbst of VENRO, the umbrella organization representing around 150 development and humanitarian NGOs.

Herbst criticized the stagnant budget for humanitarian aid administered by the German Foreign Office. While it exceeded €3 billion in 2022, it has remained at just €1 billion since 2025. In light of worsening global crises, VENRO is calling for a change of course. "Next year, we will need to invest more in combating hunger because the conflict involving Iran and the dry summer are expected to trigger a sharp decline in agricultural yields," Herbst said, explaining the organization's demand for substantially higher funding.

The impact of the cuts is already clearly visible, according to VENRO. A survey of its member organizations found that the number of funded projects is declining significantly. "Our sample of 34 organizations shows that, among these organizations alone, more than ten million fewer people are being reached with assistance."

"And many local partner organizations, which are indispensable to effective development cooperation and humanitarian assistance, are no longer able to operate," Herbst said. He added that the full impact of these far-reaching cuts is unlikely to become apparent for another three to four years.

The federal government's belt-tightening across virtually all ministries is a consequence of the Zeitenwende announced by 2022 Chancellor Olaf Scholz(SPD) in response to Russia's invasion of Ukraine. Since then, Germany has embarked on a major military buildup, increasing its defense budget year after year, while nearly all other ministries have had to make do with significantly less funding.

Herbst, a member of VENRO's executive board, is calling on the Bundestag to exempt both development cooperation and humanitarian assistance from Germany's "debt brake." "After all, exceptions have already been made for the modernization of the Bundeswehr and for military aid to Ukraine," he said. Herbst said an exemption would enable Germany to once again assume greater responsibility on the international stage.

Jamila Schäfer, a member of parliament from the opposition Green Party, made a similar argument during the Bundestag debate on the Development Ministry's budget. Climate action and development cooperation, she argued, are not acts of charity but are firmly in Germany's own interest.

"Germany must invest in its defense capabilities, but security is not created through investments in the defense budget alone," Schäfer said. "Those who strengthen military security while cutting support for international stability have a blind spot in their security policy."

Nicolas Zippelius, a lawmaker from the governing center-right conservative alliance, the Christian Democratic Union and Christian Social Union (CDU/CSU), sees the ongoing cuts to development spending differently. In his view, every ministry must contribute to consolidating the federal budget. "Less money does not necessarily mean a smaller impact. Instead, it should encourage us to focus more clearly on priorities and critically review existing structures."

Zippelius dismisses demands to exempt the Development Ministry from Germany's debt brake. "The key question is whether every euro spent is directed to where it can make the greatest difference." The Christian Democrat argues that success in development cooperation, as in other areas of policy, should be judged not by the size of the budget but by the impact it achieves.

Jan Sebastian Friedrich-Rust, who heads the humanitarian and development aid organizationAction Against Hunger, has a very different view. "The cuts planned for the 2027 budget are short-sighted and dangerous. As a result, up to 22.6 million people worldwide could die by 2030," he said in a written statement to DW.

Cuts to development cooperation and humanitarian aid are also fiscally short-sighted, Friedrich-Rust said. "Hunger, crises and instability generate long-term costs that far exceed the cost of prevention. Those who cut spending today are merely postponing the bill and ultimately making it much more expensive," he warned.

To underscore its concerns, the Action Against Hunger alliance delivered a petition to the leaders of the governing coalition of SPD and CDU/CSU parliamentary groups on the eve of the Bundestag's budget debate. About 100,000 people added their names to the appeal, titled "Don't Balance the Budget at the Expense of Solidarity: Strengthen International Aid!"

## Klopp wants Germany&apos;s football team to &apos;reclaim the flag&apos;

https://p.dw.com/p/5MlX0Jürgen Klopp engaged with a number of topics at his squad announcement press conference, and was not afraid to discuss politicsImage: Hannes P. Albert/AFPAdvertisementJürgen Klopp used his first squad announcement as Germany's new head coach to deliver a stirring speech in favor of what he described as "positive patriotism."

"I want us to always wave it in a way that makes no one think: 'What's wrong with you?'" he said. "But, rather, that people simply get the feeling that you're happy and understand how lucky you are to have been born, raised or moved to this wonderful country."

This was an apparent reference to the recent state election in Saxony-Anhalt, which the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) won in a landslide victory.

Klopp said of the election "that some people probably thought it was OK, while many others thought: 'Good heavens, what's going on there?"

The former Borussia Dortmund and Liverpool head coach was quick to say he is not a political expert and that he doesn't want to talk about politics at every press conference, but that he doesn't want to "leave national pride to the wrong people."

Rather than leaving that up to misinterpretation, something Klopp said was possible, he turned toward the concept of "positive patriotism."

"I can be proud to be German and still be open-minded, diverse, kind and friendly," he said. "I actually think we're a wonderful country."

The 59-year-old added that, just as he had selected the current squad by looking at what the country had to offer in terms of football talent rather than what it was lacking, it might help to do the same when looking at the country as a whole.

This echoed a similar sentiment of former Germany head coach Julian Nagelsmann, who spoke emotionally after Germany's Euro 2024 exit.

"Being together must become more important again. We must not be consumed with envy, always paint everything in a negative light and not begrudge our neighbors anything," Nagelsmann said.

"I believe that if everyone starts within their own small circle — on their street, helping their neighbors when something comes up [...] [we can] support one another, integrate everyone, welcome them, and help them feel at home, and bring people together to work with us toward a better future."

Klopp was quick to turn to the power of football, while also recognizing its place in wider society.

"It's more important that we have doctors. It's more important that we have nurses. It's more important that we have a great, great many people in a wide variety of professions," he said, before adding that football can "foster a sense of community that lets us truly feel the power of sports."

Klopp cited the recent successes of Alexander Zverev, the world champion field hockey team, the home World Cup in women's basketball and the success of athletes at this summer's European Athletics Championships as further positive examples of the power of sport.

"That's our mission. We have to create the right atmosphere — I know that. But to do that, we need people who are willing to embrace it," he said.

Klopp's delivery was a textbook example of how to create alignment across his messaging. His squad, made up of 44 players including 11 new faces, is a reflection of the diverse society that Klopp proudly referenced.

His engagement with the media was also strategically smart. After laying down clear boundaries in his first press conference that included him saying he would quit if coverage strayed into the personal, Klopp this time opted to seek to include the media as part of the process while also subtly reminding them of what he sees as their role.

When explaining his choice to name such a large squad with so many new and young players, Klopp said he wasn't going to tell members of the media how to do their job when it comes to assessing players, but said that he knew how he would do it. In between the lines, this was an indirect appeal to the media to choose coverage that would foster the development of this new era of players rather than hinder it by being overly critical or negative.

## Jews once lived lived here: When houses tell stories

https://p.dw.com/p/5MaOjA 1930 photograph of Salomo Birnbaum (standing) and his family, who had to leave their home in Germany and flee to London Image: David Birnbaum/Nathan & Solomon Birnbaum Archives, TorontoAdvertisementOver the years, many people have passed through this fourth-floor apartment in an old Berlin building. Here, at Rosenheimer Straße 40, they have sat on the sofa and gathered around the kitchen table. They have looked out the window and let their minds wander.

Marie Rolshoven has lived here with her family since 2007. "The feeling of crossing that threshold, the same one the former residents crossed for the last time, stays with you," she says. She knows that Jews once lived here, and none of them survived the Nazi era.

After the National Socialists seized power in January 1933, it quickly became clear that Jews were considered second-class citizens under Nazi rule. Even the right to remain in their homes could no longer be taken for granted.

"A racially conscious fellow citizen cannot be expected to share a household with a Jew," the Nazi party newspaper Völkischer Beobachter wrote on September 18, 1938, claiming that landlords were therefore entitled to terminate leases without notice. On April 30, 1939, the law took effect. Jewish tenants and property owners were forced to leave their homes and move into buildings designated by the authorities, referred to in Nazi jargon as "Judenhäuser"(Jewish houses).

Several people were forcibly housed in Henny and Carl Möller's apartment at Rosenheimer Straße 40. In the end, there were nine of them. In the fall of 1941, Nazi authorities began systematically deporting Jews to ghettos and camps in Eastern Europe.

Propaganda Minister Joseph Goebbels wrote in his diary: "The main thing is that the capital of the Reich be cleansed of Jews; and I will neither rest nor relent until this goal is fully achieved." The residents of Rosenheimer Straße were among those who were subsequently deported.

Marie Rolshoven aims to honor the fate of these individuals. In 2016, she, along with her late mother and a friend, founded the initiative Denk Mal Am Ort (a wordplay meaning both "Think about it at the site" and "Memorial at the Site") inspired by the Amsterdam project Open Jewish Homes.

The idea is to invite people to the very location where everyday life once occurred. "You feel a strong connection to these individuals through the actual site," says Rolshoven. "It's different from visiting a memorial."

Two floors below, her neighbors had a similar experience. Denk Mal Am Ort found out that the Jewish Katzenellenbogen family had previously rented the apartment.

The family escaped Nazi Germany in 1939, just in time. "In 2018, 92-year-old Joel Ludwig Katzenellenbogen was invited back here, accompanied by his daughter, son-in-law, and grandson,” Rolshoven tells DW.

The Denk Mal Am Ort initiative discovered him through Facebook. Rolshoven recounts how touched the visitors were when the former resident walked in and remarked, "Yes, I remember it clearly. My brother's bed used to be right here. He was 10 when we had to leave the country."

Joel Ludwig Katzenellenbogen has since passed away, along with many other eyewitnesses. This highlights the importance of intergenerational remembrance, according to Rolshoven. She has already welcomed a descendant of a former resident into her home.

Claudia Samter's mother managed to escape from Berlin to Argentina, but her aunt was not as fortunate. Seeking assistance in tracing her family's history, Claudia contacted Denk Mal Am Ort. It turned out that her aunt had once been housed in Rolshoven's apartment.

Claudia Samter has already visited twice. "After all, there's no grave in a cemetery for her to visit," says Rolshoven. "We sat at the kitchen table to talk about life today and tomorrow, and we laughed a lot and cried, too. It felt good to give this space a new purpose once again."

Denk Mal Am Ort has so far organized 90 commemorative events in eight cities this year. Many young descendants attended, according to Rolshoven. "Knowing that they had stood in this stairwell and touched the same doorknob helped them."

19-year-old Adam Ethan Klein from Switzerland also wanted to learn more about his ancestors' history. "I always knew there was a story of escape, but it was never a significant topic at home,” he says.

"They'd say, ‘Yes, your great-grandparents left Germany in 1938, just in time. Luckily, otherwise you wouldn't be here today'."

Klein says his decision to dig deeper was prompted by the spread ofantisemitism, as well as the widespread slogan "Never again is now." (Editor's note: This slogan serves as a call to actively confront antisemitism, hatred, and threats to democracy today.)

The whole family was aware of a suitcase belonging to his maternal great-grandfather in the attic. However, no one ever looked inside until Adam finally opened the suitcase and discovered dozens of documents. He also found a letter from the Hanover District Court dated June 12, 1933, addressed to his great-grandfather, attorney Max Haas.

The letter stated: "As of today, following the decree issued by the Minister of Justice, you have been removed from the list of licensed attorneys."

Klein was profoundly affected by the letter: "With this single sentence, it became unmistakably clear to the families that they were not quite what they had aspired to be for generations: an average German family. From that moment on, they were above all one thing: Jews."

For Klein, this was a troubling realization. So was the news that someone had stabbed an ultra-Orthodox Jew to death in Zurich while shouting antisemitic slogans. The attack occurred not far from the synagogue where he had shortly before celebrated a cousin's bar mitzvah. (Editor's note: In Judaism, a bar mitzvah is a ceremony that marks a young person's coming of age in religious matters.)

"For the first time in my life, I felt that unsettling sensation of not quite belonging. Yet Zurich is my home, the city where I've lived carefree as a Jewish Swiss from birth," Adam would later write.

"That was definitely new, uncharted territory for me. I had always thought that such things don't happen in Switzerland. Maybe that was a bit naive of me," he admits.

The subject would not let him go. Adam continued his research, determined to write his high school graduation thesis about his relatives. In the spring of 2024, he set off with his parents, sister, grandmother, uncles, aunts and several cousins to visit the places where their ancestors had lived.

At some point during their journey, they found themselves in Stuttgart, standing in front of a five-story building at Hölderlinstraße 55, when a man on a bike emerged from the courtyard and approached them.

"So, are you taking a guided tour of the city, or are you planning to move here?" he asked jokingly. The man was Thomas Koch, who had spent time researching the former residents of his house. When he heard the answer, "No, we're just checking out where our ancestors lived," he was thrilled.

Koch had already discovered that the former owners of the house, the Merzbacher brothers, sold it in 1936 for 75,000 Reichsmarks. From that amount, they had to pay 70,000 Reichsmarks in wealth taxes and the Reich Emigration Tax. The remaining sum was just enough to cover the ship fares to Brazil and the United States.

From 1937 to 1938, a family named Hirschheimer, who worked in the plywood trade, lived on the ground floor before they were forcibly relocated to a "Judenhaus." Their trail went cold in 1939, as Koch found no further records. So, he asked, "Are you the Hirschheimer family?" They were indeed.

Koch describes the encounter as magical. Adolf and Auguste Hirschheimer were Adam's great-great-grandparents, and their daughter, Ilse, was married to Max Haas.

## Saint, sinner or feminist icon: How Mary Magdalene&apos;s image evolved through time

https://p.dw.com/p/5MhxM'Mary Magdalene Mourns at the Body of Christ' by Arnold Böcklin (1867): just one of many portrayals currently on view at the Städel Museum in FrankfurtImage: Staedel MuseumAdvertisementApostle, prostitute, penitent and confidante of Jesus — just a few of the roles assigned to Mary Magdalene since the early days of Christianity.

Artists from the Renaissance to the Baroque period, including Titian, El Greco and Georges de la Tour, depicted her in the 16th and 17th centuries as a repentant penitent; Rembrandt portrayed her as a witness to Christ's resurrection; and Max Beckmann, a representative of the New Objectivity movement, depicted her as a sinner in 1917.

"Mary Magdalene is probably the most multifaceted and enigmatic figure in the Bible, and also one of the most enigmatic in European cultural history," said Bastian Eclercy, curator of the exhibition "Mary Magdalene: Sin. Pray. Love" at the Städel Museum in Frankfurt. "She appears countless times in art history."

Very little is known about Mary Magdalene from the Bible, yet she plays an important role in the New Testament.

The Gospels recount that Jesus delivered her from seven demons, and that she accompanied Jesus and witnessed his crucifixion and burial. On Easter morning, she discovered the empty tomb and became the first witness to the Resurrection. She then proclaimed the message of the risen Christ to Jesus' disciples.

"She is then referred to as the Apostula Apostulorum, that is, the Apostle of the Apostles," explained Eclercy. "This gives her a very central role in the history of salvation."

Around 600 AD, Pope Gregory the Great permanently distorted the image of Mary Magdalene by merging her with two other women mentioned in the Bible: an unnamed woman, the "sinner" mentioned in the Gospel of Luke who washed Christ's feet, and Mary of Bethany (who also washed his feet). The Pope claimed that the seven demons cast out of Mary Magdalene by Jesus represented the seven deadly sins. His sermon played a decisive role in shaping Mary Magdalene's reputation as a repentant prostitute — something for which there is absolutely no evidence in the Bible.

In the High Middle Ages, a legend emerged to the effect that Mary Magdalene, together with Lazarus and a group of companions, had been cast adrift in a boat on the Mediterranean; with divine help, she is said to have reached the coast of Provence in France, where she first preached the Gospel and then lived in a cave for some 30 years, doing penance.

Even today, two French towns claim to hold her remains: Vezelay in Burgundy and Saint-Maximin-la-Sainte-Baume in Provence.

In the 16th century, finally, the Reformation and Renaissance humanism brought about a change in thinking and opposition to the conflation of the three women into one.

However, it was not until the Second Vatican Council (1962 to 1965), which adopted far-reaching reforms to the Roman Catholic liturgy, that the identification of Mary Magdalene with the anonymous sinner was officially revoked.

"This very broad range of roles, which have been attributed to her throughout history and which are in a certain state of tension with one another, has evidently held a particular fascination for artists of all eras and genres time and again, whilst also presenting them with a challenge," said Eclercy.

With the rediscovery of the Gospel of Mary in modern times, the image of Mary Magdalene got something of a makeover.

This text, not included in the biblical canon, portrays her as a particularly close confidante of Jesus and has given fresh impetus to discussions about her role.

Today, she is regarded as one of the most influential women of early Christianity and a key figure in feminist theology.

"In recent decades in particular, she has thus become a role model for women — that is, for female observers — but also, and especially, for contemporary female artists, who see in her the qualities of a woman who lives on her own terms," said Eclercy.

Lady Gaga portrayed Mary Magdalene in her 2011 video for "Judas" — "the most exciting artistic moment of my career," as she wrote on Twitter (now X) at the time.

The most recent portrait in the Frankfurt exhibition — which features more than 100 depictions of Mary Magdalene — is by the Spanish artist Nieves Gonzalez and was completed in 2025: "La Sfida" (The Challenge) deconstructs virtually all the traditional attributes ascribed to Mary Magdalene by artists of earlier centuries.

Where she once gazed penitently towards the heavens, the painting depicts the female apostle looking down at the viewer with a self-assured gaze. Where her hair would otherwise have hung loose — alluding to the figure's sinful past — here it lies carefully brushed around her neck like a scarf. And where much bare skin would normally be visible, her body is now wrapped in a thick red down coat. In her hands, the disciple holds an equally (blood-) red skull — for centuries a symbol of transience — a gesture that clearly expresses her power.

Mary Magdalene has now also been officially recognized as an equal within the Catholic Church: In 2016, Pope Francis elevated her liturgical celebration on July 22 from a Memorial to a Feast, thereby aligning her legal and ritual status with that of male apostles within the faith. It's yet another chapter in the illustrious life story of Mary Magdalene, and unlikely to be the last.

The exhibition "Mary Magdalene: Sin. Pray. Love." runs at the Städel Museum in Frankfurt from September 17 through January 17, 2027.

## How climate change worsened Nepal&apos;s deadly mountain collapse

https://p.dw.com/p/5MhsgA drone view of flood damage along the Trishuli River in Nuwakot district, Nepal.Image: Rajesh Kumar Singh/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementNearly 1,400 people have died after a mountain slope collapsed and flash floods swept through the valleys of Nepal and Tibet. More than 5,000 people remain missing.

The collapse was initially thought to have been caused by an earthquake. Now, glaciologists and climate scientists have determined that a complex series of factors led to the disaster, with climate change laying the groundwork several times over: Warming temperatures receded the glacier, thawed permafrost and increased meltwater, destabilizing the slope until it gave way on August 26.

"This was not a single trigger event," Walter Immerzeel, a mountain hydrologist at Utrecht University in the Netherlands, told journalists on a press call. "The failure occurred at an elevation of about 5150 meters (about 16,896 feet) and there were several processes acting together over time scales of years to decades, rather than one factor acting alone."

The floods began upstream with a large rock avalanche on the north face of Langtang Lirung. That caused a section of the glacier to collapse at the same time, sending roughly 110 million cubic meters of rock and glacier ice, the size of 44,000 Olympic swimming pools, hurtling downward. That type of rock avalanche is rare, occurring at this size only once every 1,000-10,000 years.

The material fell about 1,400 meters (4,593 feet) from the mountain to the valley floor, picking up debris, water, ice, rock and sediment as it descended. The impact released as much energy as a magnitude 5.2 earthquake. As it crashed into the valley, it also tore up buried glacier ice, adding up to 30 million more cubic meters of debris and water to the flow.

Within about seven minutes, the flood reached Nepal and China's Rasuwagadhi border, 20 kilometers (about 12 miles) downstream, moving at an average speed of 170 kilometers per hour (about 106 miles per hour). The flood continued downstream, traveling 200 kilometers in under seven hours.

It was a complex combination of conditions, exacerbated by climate change, that allowed the slope to collapse in the first place. That's according to researchers with World Weather Attribution, an international collaboration of scientists that looks at the role of climate change in extreme weather and disaster events.

Rising temperatures, due to the burning of fossil fuels, have reshaped this stretch of the Himalayas for decades.

Near the failure site, the glacier's edge had retreated just 75 meters between 1964 and 2000. But between 2010 and 2026, it dramatically accelerated, retreating another 373 meters. That stripped away the ice supporting the rock wall above it.

Permafrost — or permanently frozen ground within the rock — had also started to thaw. That took place between 4,500 and 5,500 meters of elevation, a critical band that's most sensitive to warming. As it thawed, the mountain walls became more and more unstable.

In the immediate run-up to the collapse, conditions were extreme. July and August overall were the warmest on record in the area, with climate change adding about 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 degrees Fahrenheit) to the region. On August 24 and 25, temperatures ran roughly 2.5 to 2.7 degrees Celsius above the 10-year average. A day before the catastrophe unfolded, the maximum temperature was approximately 7 degrees Celsius above the reference mean.

Researchers also believe a 2015 earthquake played a role. That magnitude-7.8 quake triggered an earlier avalanche at Langtang Lirung, which may have weakened the rock mass in the years since, priming the slope for failure.

Researchers also tried to find out whether this was a disaster the region could have prepared for and adapted to. They determined it was uniquely hard to detect, as Nepal's early warning systems are designed for forecastable rainfall-driven floods, not quickly developing, cascading events like this one.

The flood outran Nepal's early warning systems and destroyed several monitoring gauges in its wake. The first mass SMS warning was sent about 38 minutes after the initial collapse, but the flood had already reached the border 7 minutes in.

"When warming destabilizes the roof of the world, no amount of local adaptation can fully shield vulnerable people downstream from this scale of destruction," Friederike Otto, professor of climate science at Imperial College London, said.

"Without much faster action to transition to zero fossil fuel emissions we will inevitably see more disasters of this scale occurring in the years to come."
