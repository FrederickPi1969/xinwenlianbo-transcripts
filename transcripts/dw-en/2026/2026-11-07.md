## Journalism and AI: Who owns the news?

https://p.dw.com/p/5PBhOPrefer DW on GoogleImage: Matteo Della Torre/NurPhoto/picture allianceAdvertisementMillions of people now use AI chatbots as a source of news to find quick answers to questions about current events.

A 2026 survey by the Reuters Institute for the Study of Journalism, which questioned more than 90,000 people worldwide, found that 10% use AI chatbots for news on a weekly basis, a figure that is significantly higher among younger audiences.

Explaining what is happening in the world, why it matters, and what it means has traditionally been the work of journalism. Media organizations send reporters into the field, commission investigations and conduct research, producing stories that help audiences understand global events. But as more people turn to AI tools for information, the relationship between those producing journalism and those distributing it is changing.

"In the past, search engines like Google indexed our websites and directed users to their articles, generating traffic in return," Julia Becker, publisher of Funke Media Group, told a panel at the Global Media Forum (GMF) in Berlin. "But now that exchange has broken down."

"AI companies draw on our quality reporting to generate answers, yet the reporting itself often remains largely invisible by the end of this process," said DW Director General Barbara Massing in a keynote address at the forum.

Their remarks reflect a worry that is growing across the media industry. Publicly available news is copied and summarized by AI chatbots, while news organizations are becoming less and less able to rely on the money and visibility that used to come from distributing that work.

There has been growing concern about how copyrighted material is collected, used, and reproduced by artificial intelligence, and whether the companies that build those systems should pay the people and organizations that created the material.

Media houses are now pressing "Big Tech" companies to ask permission before using their work and to pay for it.

In October, German broadcasters ARD and RTL, along with the country's Federal Association of Digital Publishers and Newspaper Publishers (BDZV) and the Association of Private Media (VAUNET), called for stronger copyright protections for media companies.

In the US, lawsuits are adding pressure. The New York Times has sued internet company Brave Softwar for allegedly using its content without permission and getting around its paywall.

Meanwhile, legal institutions in the EU, including the European Court of Justice (ECJ) are examining whether AI-generated answers can themselves infringe copyright.

In February, a coalition of media organizations, including the Guardian, the BBC, and the Financial Times, called for rules that would give publishers more control over how AI systems copy and reproduce their content.

The problem goes beyond whether AI companies may use journalism. It also concerns how publishers and journalists can get paid when their work becomes part of the answers AI systems generate.

"AI systems shouldn't just use quality journalism. They should also pay for it," Massing said.

Professor Martin Senftleben, who directs the Institute for Information Law at the University of Amsterdam, separates two ways AI companies use journalistic material: Training their models and generating responses to individual user questions.

Payment for training is a more complicated legal question, he said. The more urgent issue is what happens when an AI system uses journalism to answer a question in real time.

He argued that European copyright law may already offer a way to address part of the problem. Under existing EU law, publishers and journalists may be able to claim payment collectively, without waiting for a new legal framework.

"What are we waiting for? Germany is a large member state. This should be introduced in Germany," he told the forum.

One option, Senftleben suggested, is a legally required payment system in which every AI response that draws on journalism triggers a payment. A collecting organization would gather the money and distribute it among publishers and journalists, in the same way that music royalties are collected and distributed.

Once such a system exists, publishers and authors could use its rates as a starting point when negotiating individual licensing deals with AI companies.

Lutz Mache, senior manager of government affairs and public policy at Google, told the GMF panel that Google is willing to negotiate agreements with German media companies and to discuss new AI products with them.

Google has also started testing other ways to pay publishers for their contributions to AI-generated answers. Its "AI Contribution" pilot, which is being tested with selected publishers, aims to reward content that plays a significant role in answers produced by products such as Gemini and AI Overviews. But the program is still an early experiment, not a general payment system open to all publishers.

Microsoft, Anthropic, and OpenAI have recently said they support clearer, standardized signals that let website owners state whether their content may be used to train AI models.

Jörg Heidrich, a lawyer who specializes in technology law and serves as AI commissioner at the German media company Heise, warned against adding new rules without considering their effects on the wider tech sector.

Europe has already fallen behind in the global AI race, he argued. More regulation, he warned, could make it harder for European companies to compete with major providers based elsewhere.

Heidrich also pointed to smaller players, including startups, independent developers, and research institutions. They depend on access to data to build and train AI systems. He argued that the debate over copyright and payment risks overlooking these groups, and that new restrictions could hurt their work.

## Son of Iran&apos;s shah receives &apos;Freedom Prize&apos; in Frankfurt

https://p.dw.com/p/5PBuPPrefer DW on GooglePahlavi received the 'Freedom Prize' in Frankfurt's historic St Paul's Church from an institution linked to the pro-business Free Democrats (FDP)Image: Hannes P. Albert/AFPAdvertisementIranian opposition leader Reza Pahlavi on Saturday received a "Freedom Prize" in Frankfurt in the central German state of Hesse.

Some 18,000 people rallied to show support for Reza Pahlavi, according to a police statement.

The event was held at St Paul's Church. The building hosted the Germany's first-ever elected parliament in 1848-1849, known as the Frankfurt Assembly.

Pahlavi is the son of the last shah of Iran, Mohammed Reza Pahlavi, an autocrat who fled into exile after being deposed in a 1979 revolution.

The 1979 revolution was followed by the transformation of Iran into an Islamic republic and the consolidation of power around the Shiite Muslim clergy.

Pahlavi was handed the prize by the Friedrich Naumann Foundation, which is tied to the pro-business Free Democratic Party (FDP).

Pahlavi said that Iran "is a country not waiting to be taught democracy, this is a country being prevented from practicing it."

"A people ready to die for the right to vote is willing to exercise the right to vote," he said.

"You could not have chosen a people who better understand the meaning of the word 'freedom,'" he said.

He also stressed that he was receiving the prize only as a "trustee" until a democratically-elected government comes to power in Iran.

"I hold it only temporarily," he said. "One day I will place it in the hands of its rightful owners, not on foreign soil but in the capital, Tehran."

The speech was accompanied by chants of "Long live the shah, long live Iran!" from the crowd.

Pahlavi has in the past offered to lead a transitional government in the event of a democratic transition in the West Asian country and has said he does not aim to use this proposed position to reinstate the Iranian monarchy under his father's dynasty, instead planning to leave the decision to a later referendum.

While Pahlavi has emerged as a figurehead for factions within the anti-government protest movement, others have criticized him for his vocal support for Israel and have accused him of failing to distance himself from his father's autocratic rule.

Around 200 prisoners held in Iran signed a letter protesting Pahlavi's receipt of the prize, Sahar Sanaie, spokeswoman for the Society for German-Iranians in Hesse, was cited by the AFP news agency as saying.

The Frankfurt branches of the center-left Social Democrats (SPD) and the environmentalist Greens boycotted the event and said he could not be considered a legitimate representative of the Iranian people.

Meanwhile, the socialist Left Party argued that the church could not by used as a venue for the event due to Frankfurt city statutes that prevent the use of places of worship for political gatherings.

## After victory in the midterms, what next for US Democrats?

https://p.dw.com/p/5PBj3Prefer DW on GoogleDemocrats across the US, as here in Des Moines, Iowa, celebrated spectacular victories in the midterms on November 3Image: Vincent Alban/REUTERSAdvertisementA "blue wave" has rolled across the United States. Some are even calling it a "blue tsunami." The Democrats scored a decisive victory in the midterm elections on November 3, wresting the majority from President Donald Trump's Republicans in both the US House of Representatives and the Senate. They also won gubernatorial elections in former Republican strongholds such as Texas.

Pictures from Tuesday evening show Democrat politicians and supporters across the country celebrating, hugging, even weeping with joy. However, as the White House reminded Americans in a post on X the day after the elections: "Trump is still your president."

Trump has the power to veto any proposed legislation Congress puts before him, and his veto can only be overruled by a two-thirds majority. Also, in the past he has, on occasion, simply ignored the Congress's decision.

Patrick Malone, professor at the public administration and policy department at American University in Washington DC, told DW that a presidential veto is not necessarily the end of the matter — even though the Democrats don't have a two-thirds majority in either Congressional chamber.

"There are Republicans, especially on the House side, who are not going to want to tie themselves that closely to Trump anymore," says Malone. "The Republicans that survived had a very strong message sent to them. It would be illogical and foolish for them to point to a neighboring district [that turned blue] and say, 'Well, that can't happen here.'"

For that reason, Malone predicts that Republicans and Democrats may come together to overrule a Trump veto. "It could happen very easily."

A question at the front of many people's minds is: Might the Democrats make another attempt at impeachment? One possible basis for this would be that Trump started the war with Iran without congressional approval. The US Constitution says only Congress has the power to declare war. But as commander-in-chief, the president also has a degree of military authority. How far this extends without Congressional approval is legally disputed.

From January 2027, Democrats will have the simple majority required to bring charges against him in the House of Representatives. They will not, however, be able to muster the two-thirds majority in the Senate that would be needed for a conviction and Trump's removal from office. Some Democrats say it is nonetheless imperative that the impeachment process be initiated, as a way of keeping the president in check.

Others remain skeptical. "If we make Donald Trump the center of our focus, we will lose this moment in history," the Democratic senator Cory Booker said. "We need to deal with the American people's problems."

This does not mean that Democrats won't try to launch investigations into Trump, or into members of his family or government. Quite the reverse. Malone believes the Democrats in Congress take their control function very seriously and will definitely initiate investigations. These, he says, serve a purpose beyond the documented outcome: "Another thing about Congressional investigations: They have a much stronger impact on public opinion than on legislation. They catch people's attention."

While Trump will most likely get to keep his job, Pete Hegseth might be about to lose his. Observers assume the Democrats will demand a different secretary of defense. "There's a lot of speculation about what's going to happen to Pete Hegseth," says Brandon Conradis, a former DW journalist who is now an editor at Bloomberg Government in Washington DC. Whether Trump will actually fire Hegseth is a decision for the president. All the Democrats can do is exert political pressure.

From January onward, the Democrats will have a majority in Congress, which is where decisions are made on how government funds are spent. Conradis says one possibility for the Democrats to get their way would be to link the financing of Trump's pet projects to specific conditions. "One thing Democrats might do is say, 'Look, if you want more funding for these conflicts, Pete Hegseth needs to go.'"

As secretary of defense, Hegseth is being held at least partially responsible for the US's relative lack of success in the war with Iran. This could also be a reason for Trump to fire him.

After their success in the midterms, the Democrats have been enjoying a brief moment of unity. But Malone says it won't last. "The Democratic Party was unified for maybe 24, 36 hours. Now they're not really going to unify for a bit."

Unlike Germany, the US does not have governing coalitions at the federal level. There are, however, various political groupings within the Democratic Party itself. On the left wing of the political spectrum there are the progressive Democrats, including, among others, the prominent young representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez. "[Progressives] have very aggressive policy ideas, whether it be environment or social programs," comments Malone.

In the middle are the mainstream liberal Democrats, who see themselves as politically pragmatic and good at compromising. But there are also moderates. "This is an important group on the Democratic side," Malone says. "There were a lot of Democrats [in the midterms] who won elections in former red districts or really, really conservative blue districts. They won't be able to go back to their districts and say, 'Look, AOC wants this.'"

It will be no easy task for Democrats in Congress to agree on one clear direction. But many believe that it's vital that they do. "They're going to have to figure out a positive governing agenda that they can all agree on," says Malone. Just opposing Trump won't be enough for the Democrats to sustain their current success, the pundit argues.

## New Zealand election too close to call with over 95% of votes counted

https://p.dw.com/p/5PBEOPrefer DW on GoogleIncumbent Luxon said his National Party has a 'pathway' to forming a governmentImage: David Rowland/REUTERSAdvertisementThe two main parties were effectively deadlocked in New Zealand's close-fought general elections on Saturday, with the rising cost of living and general economic problems the main issues at stake.

Results with 95% votes counted showed the ruling center-right National Party and the opposition Labour Party neck-and-neck with just under 30% of the vote.

The National Party held a slim lead of about 3,500 votes more than Labour late on Saturday night.

Incumbent Prime Minister Cristopher Luxon, who heads the National Party, said that his party "is once again the largest party in parliament, and we have a pathway to forming the next government."

The National Party currently governs in coalition with the pro-business ACT and the right-wing populist New Zealand First. According to preliminary results, the three parties are set to net a total 67 seats in parliament, amounting to a wafer-thin majority.

Labour leader Chris Hipkins said that New Zealand's politicians would need to find "common ground" after the results showed neither his party nor the ruling National Party were likely to form a governing majority on their own.

"New Zealand has spoken. It may take us a little bit longer to figure out exactly what it is that you've said," Hipkins told supporters.

"Our next parliament is going to require all of us to compromise, to work together, and to find common ground."

Special votes, including those cast from people overseas, are to be counted over the next three weeks. The votes typically benefit left-leaning parties and have shifted preliminary election results over the last three elections.

If the right-wing bloc fails to hold on to its slim majority, the centrist Opportunity Party could act as a kingmaker. The party is projected to get seven seats, putting it past the 5% threshold to enter parliament for the first time since it was founded in 2017.

New Zealand has a mixed-member proportional system that requires voters to tick two options: a party and a local electoral candidate.

Electorate winners earn a seat in parliament, which typically has 120 seats, unless a so-called "overhang" leads to more lawmakers being returned to parliament, along with the highest-ranked members of the party based on their national party vote.

Preliminary results on Saturday showed that parliament is set to expand to 130 seats because of the overhang, making it the largest parliament in New Zealand history.

The vote took against a backdrop of high prices for basic goods and the highest unemployment in more than a decade.

The voting took place under New Zealand's strict laws banning media reporting and political campaigning on the final day.

New Zealanders have, however, been able to cast their votes since October 26, with almost 1.7 million of the country's 3.8 million eligible voters already casting their ballots by Thursday, according to the electoral commission.

Russian President Vladimir Putin has also weighed in on the issue, accusing countries opposing Russia's return to international sport of violating the rights of his country's athletes.

The IIHF Council had originally called two days of meetings, on Monday and Tuesday, to try to reach a consensus on how to respond to the CAS decision — before extending discussions for a third day, on Thursday. By Friday, its members had evidently still not reached a consensus.

This comes as the Women's Ice Hockey World Championship, which is not affected by the CAS decision, opened in Denmark — and with the clock ticking toward the start of the World Juniors in Alberta on December 26.

## Amid protests, France&apos;s rising debt costs threaten stability

https://p.dw.com/p/5P6hiPrefer DW on GoogleThe French government is facing protests over the rising cost of living, and is also grappling with mounting debt costsImage: ReutersAdvertisementDeeply indebted, politically trapped in a standoff between extremes and led by a government without a parliamentary majority: These are the defining features of France's current predicament.

If, in response to nationwide protests, the government plans to provide €4 billion ($4.48 billion) in low-interest loans to renovate schools by 2030 and allocate a further €1 billion to universities by 2029, it is promising money the state simply does not have.

France's public debt stands at around 120% of gross domestic product (GDP). By comparison, Germany's debt-to-GDP ratio is roughly 65%, despite its €600 billion investment package for infrastructure and defense.

Germany still enjoys the highest possible AAA credit rating. France, by contrast, is rated AA, A+ or Aa3, depending on the credit agency. While Germany's outlook is consistently rated as stable, several agencies have assigned France a negative outlook.

As a result, the French government must pay higher interest rates on its borrowing than Berlin. The risk premium over German government bonds, known as the spread, is now at its highest level since the financial crisis. France pays nearly 1.6 percentage points more in interest than Germany on 10-year bonds.

The spread acts as a kind of "fever gauge" for bond markets, explained Friedrich Heinemann, an economist at the Leibniz Center for European Economic Research (ZEW) in Mannheim.

"This fever gauge then forces a change of course. If the spread rises too sharply, France's financing conditions come under pressure, and anyone in France who needs a loan has to pay higher interest rates. Banks then get into trouble, and the crisis escalates further," Heinemann told DW.

"This could then slide toward a financial crisis, which would finally send a clear signal to everyone: Things cannot continue like this unless you are prepared to risk a genuine economic collapse," he said.

Landry Charrier, a researcher at the University of Bonn's Center for Advanced Security, Strategic and Integration Studies, however, does not expect France's debt-fueled spending spree to end anytime soon.

"French people are aware of the country's high public debt. But debt remains an abstract concept if its consequences are barely felt in everyday life. According to INSEE [the French national statistics agency — Editor's note], 56% of the population receive more in government benefits and services than they pay in taxes and social contributions. The majority therefore have a tangible interest in maintaining the status quo," Charrier told DW.

For more than half a century, France has regularly spent more than it collects in tax revenue. The last time it balanced its national budget was in 1974.

France last paid a similarly high-risk premium at the end of 2011, during the eurozone debt crisis. What is particularly striking is how quickly it has risen: Since June 2026, the spread over German government bonds has more than doubled.

The main driver is not so much the general level of interest rates. Rather, it's the growing risk premium investors demand in response to high debt levels, political instability, large budget deficits and the recent unrest.

The higher the interest rates, the larger the share of the national budget that must be devoted to debt servicing alone. France is expected to pay more than €79 billion in interest on its total public debt in 2026. That figure is projected to exceed €91 billion in 2027 and could reach between €125 billion and €132 billion by 2030.

These figures include the costs incurred by social security funds and local authorities. The central government's interest bill alone is currently estimated at €62.6 billion by the Agence France Tresor, a French Treasury agency.

In the coming years, France will have to refinance large amounts of outstanding bonds that were issued when interest rates were at historic lows. At the same time, the country's substantial budget deficit means it must take on additional debt.

The trend of rising interest costs is also affecting Germany. The ZEW's Heinemann has calculated that German interest payments are likely to increase sharply by the early 2030s.

"For the federal government alone, which has so far paid around €30 billion, the figure will triple within a few years as both debt and interest rates rise. And it won't stop there — it will exceed €90 billion. Reaching €100 billion in annual interest payments in the federal budget is then no longer far off," warned the economist.

In France, however, economic realities seem to have little impact on political debate. Both the far-right National Rally (RN) and the far-left France Unbowed (LFI) are campaigning on costly promises.

RN leader Marine Le Pen, for example, wants to reinstate the retirement age of 62 while promising €140 billion in savings. Jean-Luc Melenchon, LFI's leader, has even suggested that government bonds held by the French central bank should be "thrown into the fire."

For Heinemann, this is "a good example of radical parties on both the right and the left simply telling economic fairy tales."

Their common message, he said, is that "there is a way out of this mess that requires neither fiscal consolidation nor painful cuts."

Charrier is convinced that many French people have yet to grasp the seriousness of the situation.

"The euro has made it easier for France to access cheap financing and has long softened the pressure to adjust," he pointed out.

"In the past, currency devaluations [of the French franc — Editor's note] made economic imbalances immediately felt through declining purchasing power and rising prices. Today, that warning signal is missing. This has created the illusion that necessary adjustments can be postponed indefinitely," Charrier said.

Hundreds of employees of Ueno and other companies in its parent conglomerate protested outside the Labor Ministry Thursday, demanding unpaid wages and severance.

Retirees, depositors, students and workers had marched through Asuncion a day earlier, demanding Pena's impeachment and holding signs saying "Give us back our money."

Authorities detained eight senior executives, including founder Miguel Vazquez and bank president Juan Manuel Gustale. Prosecutors also charged 27 bank officials and business associates with offenses including breach of trust and attempted fraud.

Lawyers for several detainees said they needed time to review the allegations before commenting.

Pena had sought to reassure customers in a video message Wednesday, pledging legislation "that will return to every saver what they are entitled to as quickly as possible."

The crisis also forced him to cancel his attendance at the Ibero-American summit in Madrid.
