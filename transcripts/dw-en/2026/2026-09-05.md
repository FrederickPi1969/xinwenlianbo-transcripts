## Germany: From emigration to immigration destination

https://p.dw.com/p/5Kev7A statue in the port of Bremerhaven commemorates the millions of Germans who emigrated abroadImage: S. Ziese/blickwinkel/picture allianceAdvertisement"Why should [they] be suffered to swarm into our Settlements, and by herding together establish their Language and Manners to the Exclusion of ours? [...] [They] will shortly be so numerous [...] and will never adopt our Language or Customs."

These defamatory comments about refugees and asylum-seekers sound as if they might have been made today by the right-wing populist Alternative for Germany party (AfD). Yet in this instance, the people vilified and accused of refusing to integrate are Germans — and the harsh judgement was made in the 18th century by none other than Benjamin Franklin, one of the Founding Fathers of the United States.

The pursuit of happiness and a better life are mentioned in the American Declaration of Independence, which Franklin helped write. Over the centuries, they have been the motivation for countless people to turn their backs on their respective homelands.

The Germans who emigrated to America in Franklin's lifetime were actually fairly limited in number. By 1820, only about 150,000 had successfully made the journey. Often, they were persuaded by compatriots who told tales of their tremendous success in their new homeland and celebrated the prosperity they had supposedly attained in a very short space of time. What most people back home didn't know was that these recruiters received a commission for every migrant.

"The crossing on a sailing ship cost about what a tradesman would earn in a year," said Simone Blaschka of the German Emigration Center in Bremerhaven. "To be able to afford that, families had to sell all their worldly goods."

They would have had no idea what awaited them on board the ship. During the crossing, which took several weeks, they were packed together below deck in a very confined space, plagued by bedbugs, fleas and rats. Many fell sick with dysentery, typhoid, smallpox or scurvy.

In his book "Journey to Pennsylvania in the Year 1750," the schoolmaster and organist Gottlieb Mittelberger described what he had experienced on the voyage: "Many people whimper, sigh and cry piteously for their homes; most of them get home-sick. Many hundred people necessarily die and perish in such misery, and must be cast into the sea."

On arrival in America, Mittelberger wrote, the majority of his fellow passengers were effectively auctioned off. These were people without means who were granted free passage after undertaking to work for years as bonded laborers in exchange. In the US, their contracts were sold off. Mittelberger saw families torn apart and parents forced to sell their children.

As these early migrants were traveling to America, there was simultaneously a big wave of emigration from Germany to the east. People followed the call of the Russian czarina, Catherine the Great, herself a German princess by birth.

On July 22, 1763, she issued a manifesto inviting people to come to Russia, with the aim of cultivating uninhabited regions for arable farming. She promised the new arrivals free transport to their chosen place of residence, land, also free of charge, the first year tax-free, schooling, exemption from military service, and a special political status that would allow them to maintain the German language and, for the most part, govern themselves.

However, in the 19th century the American continent once again became the dream destination. "The German population almost doubled in the 19th century, so you can imagine what the job market was like," said Blaschka from the German Emigration Center. The situation was exacerbated by failed harvests and famines.

"People didn't see any future for themselves or their children, and decided to emigrate," she said. In this way, around 6 million Germans were drawn to the New World.

"Between the beginning of the 19th century and the World War I, there were probably around 60 million Europeans who left their homes," said Jochen Oltmer, a professor at the Institute for Migration Research and Intercultural Studies in Osnabrück. Then, in the mid-20th century, this trend reversed and Europe went from being a continent of emigration to one of immigration.

In the 1950s and '60s, the future looked rosy for postwar West Germany and its "economic miracle." From 1956 onward, so-called "guest worker trains" arrived in stations like Stuttgart, Dortmund and Cologne, carrying Italians, Greeks, Spaniards, Portuguese, Yugoslavs and Turks, attracted by the promise of well-paid jobs in the new Federal Republic.

By the time the ban on recruiting foreign labor came into force in 1973, around 14 million people had come to West Germany as "guest workers." Around 3 million stayed permanently.

Yet Germany still did not consider itself an immigration country. "For a very long time, we were dealing with the mindset: It doesn't matter why people came to the Federal Republic; they don't belong, because in any case they'll leave the country again and disappear," said Oltmer.

In 2025, according to Germany's Federal Statistical Office, more than 30% of Germany's total population had a migrant background. Yet many third- and fourth-generation citizens still don't feel they belong, which has to do with the perspective of the society in which they live.

These days, Germany is keen to attract skilled workers, but refugees are only granted asylum if they are politically persecuted in their homeland, or are fleeing war.

As a migration researcher, Oltmer is constantly examining the reasons why some migrants are accepted and others are not. This question was especially relevant in 2015, when refugees came to Germany in very large numbers.

"The attitude was: 'OK, you people from Syria, in light of the Syrian civil war, you are entitled to protection.' But at the same time this society says of people from Afghanistan, 'In your case, we're a bit more sceptical,'" he said.

Yet asylum accounts for barely 10% of immigration to Germany, Oltmer explained. The majority of immigrants are not people fleeing their country. "This means that when it comes to scandalization and the creation of certain negative images, we are seeing a massive distortion of the debate." And the descendants of the German emigrants Benjamin Franklin once reviled as "Palatine boors" have long since perfectly integrated into American society.

In Germany, however, integration is taking much longer. "Many people have no possibility of regularizing their status and obtaining a stable residence permit, even though everyone involved knows they can stay, and will stay," said Oltmer. He believes people urgently need to sit down and clarify how they want German society to look in future.

## Why many Syrians feel Assad&apos;s death sentence isn&apos;t enough

https://p.dw.com/p/5LPqNDespite terrible memories of death during the war, including that of her brother, Huda Khayti now often returns to her hometown of DoumaImage: privatAdvertisementHuda Khayti often finds herself returning to the place where she lost almost everything. Khayti, a Syrian women's rights activist, survived a 2013 chemical weapons attack on the east Ghouta region by forces of the former Syrian dictator Bashar Assad.

Her brother was killed by the bombs of the Assad regime and its allies. Her women's center was destroyed along with her home and in 2018, she had to leave her hometown of Douma — a major city in Ghouta — and flee to Idlib, a rebel stronghold in northern Syria.

Today, Khayti regularly visits her hometown, which is about 10 kilometers (about 6 miles) northeast of the country's capital, Damascus. Since the Assad regime was overthrown in late 2024, Khayti has traveled regularly between Idlib and Douma, where she's even working on opening a new women's center.

"Ghouta means so much to me emotionally," she told DW. But every time she returns there, she is also confronted with remnants of the civil war. "When I'm traveling through the country I see traces of war everywhere, so many houses and so much infrastructure destroyed. So much suffering. So much trauma."

Almost two years since the overthrow of the Assad regime, Syria has started a process to examine the crimes of the regime in court. In mid-August a Damascus court sentenced Bashar Assad and other high-ranking members of his regime to death in absentia. Assad is currently living in Russia, where he fled after his government was overthrown. Many senior officials are also there or hiding elsewhere.

Among other things, Assad was found guilty of premeditated murder, torture and depriving individuals of their liberty. The court said these were war crimes and crimes against humanity.

"Of course, this conviction of Assad is a good thing," Khayti said. "Even if it's only on paper. Bashar Assad may have been convicted but he just continues to live his life in Russia — like so many other Syrian criminals who fled to Russia."

The court's conviction had a big impact in Syria anyway. "The verdict had a lot of echoes by people, they were so happy," said Lina Chawaf, a Syrian journalist, media expert and researcher at Harvard University's Center for Middle Eastern Studies. "Many media celebrated the verdict on the statement day as a victory and consider it as part of transitional justice."

Still, it remains unclear whether there will be any actual punishment following the conviction. Although Russia and Syria agreed on an extradition treaty in 2023, observers say it's unlikely Russia would send Assad, a long-time ally, back to Syria. Additionally, according to the treaty, if the death penalty is likely, that could prevent extradition too.

In August, Syria's Deputy Justice Minister Mustafa al-Qassem said the country had established a commission to cooperate with Interpol and that cooperation with other countries in extraditing fugitive suspects would speed up legal proceedings.

Rana Sheikh-Ali, founder of Daret Salam (Peace Circuit in English), which uses technology to build peace and empower women, believes the Assad trial in August should be seen as largely symbolic. From a human rights perspective, she opposes the death penalty, but at the same time she says many locals saw the death sentences as just.

But the prosecution of high-ranking regime members is only one aspect of the transitional justice process.

Transitional justice isn't just about criminal trials. Other aspects include searching for missing people, investigating crimes, deciding on reparations and formulating institutional reforms. With all that, the focus should be on victims and survivors.

Numbers supplied by the Syrian Network for Human Rights indicate just how big the task is. In an article, the advocacy network reports that over 234,805 civilians were killed during the civil war, at least 202,000 of them by the Assad regime and its allies.

In addition, there are also at least 181,312 cases of arbitrary arrest and enforced disappearance to investigate as well as 45,339 deaths resulting from torture. It's thought that the actual numbers are likely far higher. The network has also documented 217 chemical weapons attacks by the Assad regime.

Women's rights activist Khayti was just a few kilometers from the epicenter of one of those attacks in eastern Ghouta in 2013. "I remember it like it was yesterday," she recounted. "I can't forget the screams of the victims. I kept thinking we were next. I felt that scratchiness in my throat, that burning in my eyes."

For a system of transitional justice that is truly victim-centered, it's not enough to just have survivors act as witnesses in court. "Victims must also participate in every detail related to the transitional justice process," she explained.

Additionally, those who were impacted by the crimes must be able to participate voluntarily and be protected as they do, Sheikh-Ali added. They should be able to talk about their experiences and should also be heard on topics like compensation, financial support and public recognition.

For survivors like Khayti, this means not just making Assad and a few top regime officials the primary targets of justice.

"I also want to see convictions for individuals who wrote reports that led to people ending up in the Saydnaya torture prison," Khayti said. "These individuals are just as guilty as the torturers themselves."

"Convictions in absentia against Assad and other officials send an important signal after decades of impunity," said Sophie Bischoff, managing director of Adopt a Revolution, a German-Syrian human rights organization that supports civil society initiatives. "But for survivors like Huda Khayti, these sentences remain symbolic as long as those convicted are not actually held accountable — and it's also still not transparent exactly who is to be held accountable and on what legal basis, and who would be granted immunity."

Bischoff also noted that the new Syrian state has yet to fully and comprehensively investigate crimes against humanity and war crimes committed in Syria.

"So far it's primarily been Syrian civil society organizations and human rights activists who have been researching and documenting these cases for years," she told DW. "Systematic state documentation of violent crimes committed during the Assad regime era is only just beginning."

The new Syrian government has created two commissions to work on issues of transitional justice. In late August, one of these — the National Commission for Missing Persons — launched a platform called Athar, where families of the missing can submit information.

The other organization, the National Commission for Transitional Justice, was established in May 2025 but has so far dealt only with crimes committed by the Assad regime. It has not dealt with crimes committed by militias fighting the war or the extremist group known as the "Islamic State".

"This falls far short," said Bischoff. "For a comprehensive reckoning and a new beginning in a Syria for all, the war crimes of all warring parties must be included."

For Khayti, the requirement to place victims at the center of the process of transitional justice also has another difficult aspect. Her testimony is being heard, but at the same time she is forced to return to the original trauma.

"It's difficult for me to talk about it and I often feel like I'm reliving it," Khayti explained.

Sheikh-Ali said centering the victims means those impacted play a meaningful role in the process. But as they do so, she added, they should also be protected.

It's also important to remember these events, Khayti added. In Douma, she is working on ways to preserve recollections of the war. "We need to inform younger people about it so that the memory doesn't fade," she said.

## What would an AfD government in Saxony-Anhalt really mean?

https://p.dw.com/p/5KvfvUlrich Siegmund, top candidate for the far-right AfD in Saxony-Anhalt, is confident he will get the mandate to form a governmentImage: Annegret Hilse/REUTERSAdvertisementThe Alternative for Germany (AfD) could bring in massive changes should it form a government in Saxony-Anhalt. The far-right party, currently way ahead in opinion polls, is hoping to form a single-party government and have its lead candidate, Ulrich Siegmund, serve as state premier.

The Saxony-Anhalt chapter of the AfD is classified by the domestic intelligence agency as "confirmed far-right."

In its draft government program, the AfD has promised "deportation from minute one!" if it wins the election in Saxony-Anhalt. The stated goal, according to the party's election program, is simply to deport as many immigrants who have no residency permits as possible.

The party has also pledged much stricter asylum rules at all levels: It wants more detention pending deportation, mandatory work for asylum-seekers, and intends to cut all state funding for integration programs, which it refers to as an "asylum industry."

Furthermore, it wants to exclude children and adolescents who do not have permanent residency in Germany from attending regular schools. On top of that, a "Remigration Task Force" is to be established within the state government to coordinate the state's asylum policy.

Exactly how many of these measures an AfD state government would be legally able to implement remains unclear. Creating special classes for refugees could, for example, be a violation of international and European law.

And the question of who can and can't be deported falls under the jurisdiction of the federal government, not that of an individual state.

The AfD opposes immigration to Germany. In its government platform, the party calls for a "180-degree turnaround in migration policy," and its stated goal is to reduce the number of immigrants in Saxony-Anhalt.

However, the region has been suffering for years from a shortage of skilled workers, particularly in nursing and care for the elderly. The AfD's plan to end this shortage is to encourage Germans who live abroad to come back.

To this end, it plans to launch a "return program," including a proposed "return bonus" to help find housing and daycare spots for children. It is unclear how many returnees the AfD anticipates and how much money it has budgeted for the "return program."

Saxony-Anhalt has been severely affected by depopulation for over 30 years. No other German state population has shrunk more since reunification in 1990 — from around 2.9 million people then to 2.1 million today.

Economic researchers like Reint Gropp, president of the Halle Institute for Economic Research, have warned that an AfD government is likely to exacerbate the skilled worker shortage.

"Highly qualified professionals, in particular, have options," he told Die Welt newspaper on Tuesday. "If they feel they are unwelcome or can expect hostility, they will choose other regions."

The 16 state governments have virtually no authority over Germany's foreign and defense policy, though they can influence political debates via the Bundesrat, the chamber of parliament that represents the states. In that context, an AfD state premier would carry considerable political weight.

For example, since the start of Russia's full-scale war against Ukraine in 2022, Germany has largely halted oil imports, but the AfD's election platform calls for a rapprochement with Russia and an end to sanctions.

The call is largely symbolic, as Saxony-Anhalt is bound by federal sanctions, but an AfD state premier could set a new tone in the Bundesrat.

The AfD also wants to expand Russian language instruction in schools and specifically recruit native Russian speakers for this purpose.

## UN approves world map reflecting true size of Africa

https://p.dw.com/p/5Le4rUnlike a globe, a two-dimensional map can distort the actual sizes of the continents or their shapes and anglesImage: ingimage/IMAGOAdvertisementA yearslong push to change the default view of the Earth came to a vote at the United Nations General Assembly on Friday with 164 countries in favor of a new map, the United States against it and six abstentions.

Known as the Equal Earth cartographic projection, the map "offers a more accurate representation of the sizes of continental landmasses and to this end is a relevant option for improving the accuracy of the cartographic representation of the world," according to the resolution, which was spearheaded by several African countries.

Togo's foreign minister, Robert Dussey, told the UN maps "shape our understanding of the world."

"They guide education, nourish the imagination, and influence collective perceptions," he said. "They are, therefore, never neutral, when they present a distorted image of our planet, they risk perpetuating inaccurate representation that are passed down through generation."

The resolution pushes for countries to adopt the Equal Earth projection rather than the traditional Mercator projection, which was created in 1569 by Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator.

The Mercator projection accurately depicts the shapes and angles of landmasses, necessary for shipping, but distorts their relative sizes: landmasses near the Earth's poles appear larger while those near the equator look smaller. This makes Greenland appear about as big as Africa, despite the African continent being about 14 times larger.

Togo's Dussey said his country aims to replace the maps used in its schools by the end of the year.

The Mercator projection's use is a result of colonization, Dussey said, adding, "The world is changing. Africa also is changing."

France, which co-sponsored the resolution, said on Friday that it would also abandon the Mercator projection.

In that projection, "the United States, Russia and China take on a disproportionate visual prominence compared to Africa, Latin America or southeast Asia," while "Greenland appears almost as big as Africa," French Foreign Minister Jean-Noel Barrot said in statement.

"Changing maps obviously doesn't change the world," he acknowledged. "But correcting a distorted representation is already an act of truth."

In support of the new projection, online campaign group #CorrectTheMap said, "You could fit the United States, China, India, Japan, Mexico and much of Europe into Africa and still have land to spare."

The UN General Assembly vote is not legally binding and "in no way challenges the use of Mercator projection for maritime and aerial navigation, for which it remains fully suited," the ministry said in a statement.

As the only country to vote against the resolution, the United States said the Equal Earth projection pushed an "ideological agenda" and distracted attention from "genuine problems of international peace, prosperity or good relations."

"Instead of focusing on genuine problems... this body is debating map projects from the 16th century and their role in promoting reparations and cognitive justice," US representative Yaryna Ferencevych said ahead of the vote.

Estonia, Georgia, Lithuania, Morocco, Serbia and Ukraine abstained from voting on the resolution.
