09/13/2026September 13, 2026Asylum approval rates climb as applications sinkAsylum applications have gone down dramatically in GermanyImage: Joko/IMAGOThe number of new asylum applications in Germany has fallen sharply but the rate of approvals has risen again, according to the latest official figures from the Federal Office for Migration and Refugees (BAMF).

Altogether 39,646 people put in first-time applications for asylum in Germany in the first half of 2026, a steep drop from the 72,818 applications filed in the same period last year.

However, 47.4% of the applications in the first half of this year were approved, as compared with 37.5% of applications in 2025 as a whole.

Families applying for asylum had particular success, accounting for 45.6% of the positive decisions.

The approval rate for people from Afghanistan — the main country of origin for asylum applicants in the first of the year — was 89.3%, with protection granted to Afghan women in nearly 95% of cases.

This can be attributed to a European Court of Justice decision in 2024 that women from Afghanistan are generally entitled to protection because they are at risk of gender-based persecution under the Taliban regime.

## A Romani artist in Hungary reclaims his identity

https://p.dw.com/p/5MVpiRoma artist Norbert Olah in the Bura Galeria in BudapestImage: Tobias Zuttmann/DWAdvertisementThe painting on the canvas is of a man playing chess with a robot. The color on his face is still damp and glistening. Norbert Olah was working into the early hours: The Bura Galeria in Budapest has been his studio all summer. He finds it easiest to concentrate at night, when the Hungarian capital gradually falls silent.

Olah has taped foil and cardboard over the tiled floor at the back of the gallery. Pots of brushes, tubes of paint, pencil sketches, and, over and over again, pictures that include the blue-and-green Roma flag line the room.

Norbert Olah is an artist. He's also Romani. For a long time, he deliberately left this part of his identity out of his art. Not because his ethnicity wasn't important to him, but because he didn't want to be labeled a "Romani artist."

"There are so many prejudices about Roma culture and Roma art," Olah says. "The art scene regards it as naïve, unprofessional, unsophisticated, and kitsch."

But the older Olah got, the harder he found it to ignore his own story in his art. Especially in a country in which being Romani is not just an ethnicity but a political reality.

The first time Viktor Orban's Fidesz party won an absolute majority in the Hungarian parliamentary elections of 2010, Olah had just finished school and was starting his art course at the Hungarian University of Fine Arts in Budapest. At a time of life when the world ought to be opening up, Olah felt more and more barriers were being erected.

Olah was directly affected by Hungary's political reorientation, as both an artist and a Romani. Politics co-opted the cultural scene and made it toe the party line. Roma became the target of hateful rhetoric, including from the prime minister himself. Orban repeatedly called attention to himself with racist comments about Roma.

For the 16 years Orban was in power, Hungary's Roma population, estimated to be around 700,000 people, was caught between official promises of integration and persistent marginalization. Orban's government introduced a state employment program that hired predominantly Romani workers. But instead of helping them gain a foothold in the job market, many Romani found themselves trapped in insecure, poorly paid, temporary jobs. The government did little to implement real change. Romani in Hungary are still disproportionately affected by poverty and social exclusion.

In 2021, during Hungary's biggest independent contemporary art event, the OFF-Biennale Budapest, Olah and his father laid a brick wall directly in front of the entrance to the building that had housed the Roma Parliament, a Roma cultural center with an art collection, archive, library, and events program. The Orban government evicted it in 2016, saying the building was structurally dangerous and promising to repurpose it as a national Roma center. It has stood empty ever since.

The title of Olah's work was "The Romani Artist's Fears." Words like "integration," "assimilation," and "segregation" were chiseled into the bricks. A few weeks later, Olah staged a performance in which he took a sledgehammer to his artwork and smashed it till it collapsed.

In an accompanying text, Olah wrote publicly for the first time about the fears, prejudices, and expectations he encounters as an artist with Roma roots. The text is full of questions. Should a Romani artist's work focus on topics relevant to Roma? What is the relationship between ethnicity and aesthetics? Would people only contact an artist with Roma roots if they were specifically looking for a Romani artist, or would they do so if they needed a Hungarian artist, too?

"Of course we're always looking for answers. But sometimes it's more interesting to look at the question itself," says Olah, picking a piece of crepe masking tape off the floor. "If it feels uncomfortable, it's a good question."

And so Olah has created a new label for Romani artists. The Bura Galeria, established by Romani activists in 2021 in Budapest's District 8, has become the place for it: A platform for contemporary Roma art.

"We're not asking to be accepted by the art scene," said Olah. "We're opening the door and inviting others in."

Identity and exclusion, power and the misuse of power — these became central themes for Olah's artistic work. He had no lack of inspiration during the Orban years.

Although Fidesz's policies were hardly favorable to Roma, Orban's party could, for a long time, count on Roma votes — for example, because local employment programs enabled local politicians to pressure socially underprivileged voters. There were also many documented cases of vote-buying. Fidesz was so confident that the majority of Roma would continue to vote for it that it openly fueled the antiziganism still widespread in Hungarian society.

Shortly before the election in April 2026, Fidesz's Janos Lazar, who was minister for transport at the time, spoke out against labor migration with the argument that Hungary still had "domestic reserves," like the Roma, who could "clean the crap out of overflowing toilets" on Intercity trains. The scene appears in Olah's art: A fake election poster depicts a man in a balaclava sitting on a golden toilet, brandishing a toilet brush, with the slogan underneath: "Gypsy Criminals for President."

Gypsy Criminals is also the name of the artists' collective Olah founded in 2024, along with a friend of his and his miniature pinscher. The term comes from Hungarian crime statistics, and has been used since the 2000s by right-wing politicians to imply that all Roma are criminals. The name is ironic, like many aspects of Olah's work.

Analyses by the foundation Roma for Democracy show that many Roma did not vote for Orban's Fidesz party in the April 2026 parliamentary election. Orban's successor, Peter Magyar, adopted a different tone in relation to Roma as part of his election campaign. He met with members of the Roma minority, and his election manifesto contained a specific paragraph about equal opportunities for Roma. Four of the Tisza party's nine new members of parliament are Romani. One was even elected a deputy speaker of parliament.

Since the election, there has been cautious optimism that the precarious situation of many Roma in Hungary could finally change. On the other hand, nothing has fundamentally changed in the four months since the election, apart from the tone when talking about the ethnic minority. "What I'm seeing right now are mostly symbolic gestures," says Olah.

Some of his friends in the more liberal art scene are growing impatient. They would like to see Magyar take a much more liberal course, but Olah is a realist.

"He's a conservative politician," he says. "We elected him to put an end to the Orban system. He didn't promise any more than that." But the election in April was an opportunity for a fresh political start, he adds. "I think in the long run we will live in a normal society again, led by a normal government, that will do some things well and others not so well. As an artist, it will be my job to criticize that."

Olah's work also considers the future. The first pictures for his next project are already up on the wall in his studio. It's Roma futurism: A Romani couple strolling on Mars; a blue-and-green Roma flag on the moon; a weightless astronaut with a traditional Cegled water jug, used by Romani musicians as a percussion instrument. Olah is also planning a two-meter-high space station in the shape of a cartwheel, a symbol of the Romani people.

For Olah, space travel is also a metaphor for social advancement — the desire of people and communities to overcome the barriers they face as a result of their origins, or poverty, or life in a minority. Again, he is asking questions. What if a society were fundamentally recreated from the beginning? If aliens encountered Roma, would they do so without prejudice? What remains of an identity if everything about it changes?

Olah shreds the masking tape between his fingers. More unanswered questions. But that's exactly what makes them so interesting.

## Sweden election: Far right eyes power for 1st time

https://p.dw.com/p/5MWBDAndersson's leftist bloc has a narrow lead going into the tight raceImage: Denis Balibouse/REUTERSAdvertisementSweden is holding a general election on Sunday that will either result in the center-left opposition returning to power after four years or see the far right enter government for the first time.

Opinion polls in the run-up to the election have been predicting an easy win for the left-wing alliance over the right-wing group led by incumbent Ulf Kristersson, which includes the far-right party Sweden Democrats.

But the four-party opposition alliance, led by former Prime Minister Magdalena Andersson, the head of the center-left Social Democrats, has seen that lead narrow as election day has neared.

"We all knew it was going to be a very close election; I've said that all along," Andersson, the first woman to serve as Swedish Prime Minister in 2021-2022, told a rally in the Swedish capital Saturday.

"Now it's up to voters: do they want a government that will be totally dominated by the Sweden Democrats, or do they want a government led by me?"

The Social Democrats have governed in Sweden for most of the last century and remain the country's largest party.

Kristersson's campaign has focused on his government's record of reducing violent and organized crime and clamping down on immigration.

A victory at the ballot box on Sunday would suggest voters approve of that approach, supporters of the right-wing bloc say. Critics have denounced the agenda as undermining civil liberties and unfairly targeting immigrants.

"We can unify parties, voters and issues into joint action. That's why ​we get things ‌done," Kristersson said this week in parliament.

Unlike in Germany, where most parties have ruled out working with the far-right Alternative for Germany as part of the political "firewall", Kristersson has promised the Sweden Democrats key government posts if his alliance wins.

The leader of the far-right party, Jimmie Akesson, told a campaign rally in Stockholm on Saturday that the Sweden Democrats "have already won," as the party eyes power for the first time since entering parliament in 2010.

Akesson is expected to be given the role of migration minister if the right-wing alliance wins on Sunday.

Eight million people are eligible to vote in a country of 10.6 million people. Turnout regularly exceeds 80% in Sweden.

## France: Attack ruled out in train derailment

https://p.dw.com/p/5MUIXAn 18-year-old woman was seriously hurt in the accidentImage: Guillaume Saligot/AFPAdvertisementA train derailed on Friday evening between the cities of Rouen and Caen in Normandy, northwest France, authorities said.

The public prosecutor of Rouen, Sebastian Gallois, said Saturday evening that there was "no indication of a malicious act carried out with the deliberate intention of derailing the train, and certainly not of an attack," according to reports in the French media.

Gallois' remarks came after some politicians had suggested earlier on Saturday that the incident might have been the result of sabotage or other criminal activity.

At least 44 people were injured in the accident, which occurred near the municipalities of Cleon and Tourville-la-Riviere at around 7:30 p.m. local time (18:30 UTC), the Seine-Maritime prefect said in a statement.

One of ​the injured, a young woman, was in a critical state and was evacuated by helicopter to Rouen, the local authority added. Her condition has reportedly improved in the hours since.

The train was carrying around 180 passengers when it derailed, national rail company SNCF and the prefect said.

"The priority is to care for the passengers and then to restore safe operating conditions," SNCF Reseau said.

A piece of rail that might have been involved has been found on the track, according to police reports.

## Anthropic boss Dario Amodei calls for AI slowdown

https://p.dw.com/p/5MVwYAmodei founded Anthropic with his sister Daniela in 2021 [FILE: 2025]Image: Fabrice Coffrini/AFPAdvertisementThe CEO of artificial intelligence (AI) safety and research company Anthropic, Dario Amodei, has called on AI firms to slow the technology's development.

His remarks on Saturday come amid mounting concerns over the risk of "superintelligent" computer software systems.

In a blog post, Amodei said he was concerned that a so-called swarm of autonomous software systems, or AI agents, could be capable of taking over the "entire internet" within six to 12 months, potentially causing billions of dollars' worth of damage.

"We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models. Progress will still seem fast, and we must make wise use of the time we gain," Amodei wrote.

Amodei's comments come in the wake of AI researcher Jacob Coxon, who joined Anthropic from rival firm OpenAI, leaving the sector in protest at the US companies "gambling with our lives" in the race to come up with AI models that are capable of self-improvement.

The Anthropic chief called for a coordinated, industry-wide pause, but said he would not wait for his firm's rivals to act.

As a first step, he said he would permanently allow independent observers into Anthropic to assess the safety of its models during the training phase.

Sam Altman, the chief of ChatGPT developer and Anthropic rival, OpenAI, as well as Elon Musk, who owns xAI, said they agreed with Amodei's assessment.

"I agree with Dario that we need to pace the frontier," Altman wrote on X, adding that OpenAI "will do the same."

In early June, Anthropic had called for AI development to be slowed or suspended amid safety concerns over AI agents.

More than 1,000 employees at AI companies, including Amodei, called on the US government to help slow the pace of development of automated AI.

Then in July, OpenAI revealed that during testing, AI agents had independently broken out of a secured environment, connected to the internet and infiltrated Hugging Face, a site used to store and develop code, as it tried to find answers to a task it had been assigned.

The AI models had exploited software vulnerabilities and coordinated among themselves during the incident, Amodei explained.

"A swarm of agents essentially acted as a fanatically devoted collective, conducting cybersecurity attacks on targets they were not asked to attack and that were unrelated to the task at hand," he said in his blog.

Amodei said he was concerned that an AI swarm with more capabilities would have caused "catastrophic damage."

"Not building the technology deprives humanity of benefits or simply places AI in the hands of authoritarian powers, while building it too fast is reckless. We have sought a middle way," he said.
