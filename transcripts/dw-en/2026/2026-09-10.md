## Digital data leeches: trading on our profiles

https://p.dw.com/p/5MOgEApps collect our personal information — and often pass it on to third partiesImage: Jeremy Piper/REUTERSAdvertisementEveryone has secrets. Things they wouldn't tell just anyone – except, perhaps, their dating app. What sort of partner they're looking for. Where they go to look for them. Their sexual preferences, maybe, or details about their health.

This information is meant to help the app find suitable matches. Selling the details on to other companies is not in the users' interest.

But this is what allegedly happened with Grindr, a dating app explicitly aimed at the LGBTQ+ community. Around 12,000 app users in Britain sued the company, which they said had shared highly sensitive personal information with advertisers — even, in some cases, a user's HIV status.

The lawsuit, filed in 2024, was settled earlier this week. Grindr has agreed to pay the claimants a total of 26 million pounds (€30 million; $35 million) – but stresses that this is not an admission of liability. Grindr continues to dispute the allegations, there has been no legal judgement.

This latest case is just one of many that raise the bigger question of what actually happens to the information we entrust to our apps on a daily basis.

For users, an app is primarily just a helpful product. But they are often supported by cloud services, analysis tools, advertising networks and other service providers. They save and transfer data, link and analyse it — often contrary to our interest.

Jan Penfrat, a digital policy expert, says that many of the big tech companies want to "make it as difficult as possible for users and regulatory authorities to understand what data is gathered on our devices by which players, and for what purpose."

Penfrat works for European Digital Rights (EDRi), a European network of NGOs and experts that campaigns to defend fundamental rights and freedoms in the digital realm.

We all know what it's like: An app, a messenger service, for example, is quickly installed, and when it asks if it has permission to use personal data, users will often just click "yes."

But the devil is in the detail. When you install WhatsApp, for example, you give it access to all the contacts in your smartphone. The phone numbers are transferred to the server — including those of people who don't use WhatsApp themselves, and who have not given permission for their contact details to be passed on.

"This means a car or clothes manufacturer can say: I'd like to address these target groups, and I want my advertising to appear in their Google search, Google Mail, or any of the hundreds of millions of third-party websites with integrated Google advertising," Penfrat explains.

Google is just one of many companies that offer data profiles. Two types of data feed into these profiles. One is the data that every user voluntarily discloses when they state something in their profile, or upload private photos.

But in addition, many big tech companies have for years now made assumptions based on the data they receive.

"If they have access to my location data and know where I usually am at night, they can assume that this is my home address," Penfrat says.

"If, for example, it's in a very expensive part of town, they can immediately make assumptions about my income. If they see: Oh, he has geolocation switched on and he regularly goes to a gay club, then he's probably a member of the LGBTQ community. And all this goes straight into the profile."

The EU already has one of the strictest data protection regulations in the world. The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) aims to protect people's personal data and privacy.

Other EU digital laws such as the Digital Markets Act or the Digital Services Act are intended to prevent big tech companies misusing their power over businesses and users. US businesses also have to abide by these laws if they offer their services to people in Europe, or observe their behavior.

The EU Commission has imposed numerous fines as punishment for violations of these regulations. In 2025, it fined Apple €500 million euros ($580 million). Meta, the company that operates Facebook, was fined another €200 million ($232 million).

Google had to pay €890 million ($1.03 billion) in fines this year. That sounds like a lot — but a look at the net profit of the Google corporation Alphabet puts it into perspective. In 2025, Alphabet's net profit was around €117 billion ($136 billion).

"So these are sums that a company like Google or Amazon will make in just a few days," says Penfrat. "They just include it in their budget."

As a result, it is not that easy for the EU to implement truly effective controls. The tech giants have global infrastructures, huge financial resources, and complex business models. European agencies first have to get past these.

Added to this is Europe's dependency on cloud services, operating systems, platforms and increasingly also AI infrastructure that, at least to date, have been provided in large part by US companies.

Penfrat criticizes Brussels for lacking the political will needed to take firmer action against these companies, and to better equip European data protection authorities.

## 25 years after 9/11: Al-Qaeda weakened but not gone

https://p.dw.com/p/5MKAz25 years after 9/11, al-Qaida has lost visibility. But regional offshots remain strongImage: APAdvertisementThe extremist preacher Abdullah al-Muhaysini no longer has to worry about US sanctions. In late August 2026, the US Department of the Treasury removed the former jihadist from the Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons list, or SDN List. It includes individuals and organizations whose assets are frozen in the US and with whom US citizens are generally not allowed to do business.

After Syria's civil war broke out in 2011, al-Muhaysini recruited fighters and helped establish a governing structure shaped by the Nusra Front, which emerged from al-Qaeda.

His removal from the list is linked to the new US government policy on Syria: Washington also removed the Nusra Front and Hayat Tahrir al-Sham (HTS) from the SDN List.

25 years after the September 11 attacks, this is a remarkable snapshot. Al-Qaeda  — which means "the base" in Arabic — has neither disappeared nor remained the same organization: Its core has weakened, but its regional branches have remained strong across parts of Africa and Asia, observers agree.

"This is largely due to the successful counterterrorism efforts following 2001," Lars Berger, professor of terrorism studies at the German Federal University of Administrative Sciences, told DW.

The military campaign in Afghanistan, along with cooperation between intelligence agencies and police, has significantly weakened the group.

"From this perspective, the September 11 attacks were a major strategic failure for al-Qaeda," Berger said.

The hoped-for radicalization of the Arab world and the overthrow of its regimes failed to materialize. About ten years after the attacks in New York and Washington, al-Qaeda's most famous figure, Osama bin Laden, was killed in May 2011.

"The militia sought out areas where it could establish a permanent foothold, for example in parts of Africa or Yemen," Berger said, adding that "countries with weak state structures, overwhelmed security forces and unresolved local conflicts are particularly favorable."

"Today, this is particularly true of Africa," Rüdiger Lohlker, professor emeritus of Islamic studies at the University of Vienna, notes.

In the Sahel, Jama'at Nusrat al-Islam wal-Muslimin, or JNIM, has emerged as a powerful al-Qaeda offshoot, he said.

An analysis by the think tank Critical Threats Project uses Niger as an example to show how JNIM and the Islamic State's Sahel Province (ISSP) compete for territory, fighters and resources. In June 2026, JNIM claimed responsibility for an attack on the international airport in Niger's capital, Niamey.

In Somalia, al-Shabab — a group closely linked to al-Qaeda — is strong, and in Yemen, al-Qaeda is present in the Arabian Peninsula (AQAP). Afghanistan also remains a safe haven for the terrorist organization.

However, the success of these offshoots cannot be explained solely by jihadist ideology, Lohlker added.

Rather, it often coincides with a kind of "economy of violence," he said, noting that "violence including kidnappings, smuggling and looting has long been an economic factor for terrorism."

He also said that there are veritable "markets for violence:" Where money can be made through violence, jihadist organizations can also recruit and retain followers.

"We fundamentally need to move away from a Eurocentric perspective," Lohlker further points out.

In his view, al-Qaeda and other jihadist groups have always been far more present outside Europe and the US than it has appeared from a Western perspective.

However, over the years, the offshoots' strategies have undergone a change. According to Lars Berger, there has been a "strategic shift" away from direct attacks on the "distant enemy" and toward the establishment of local structures.

"We are now seeing an attempt to proceed in an alternative and gradual manner, namely instead of spectacular acts of violence ordered from the highest levels, there are smaller-scale actions that are more grassroots in nature," he said.

In the Sahel, this entails forming local alliances, gaining influence in communities, and controlling territories. The Critical Threats Project describes this development as "localization."

Al-Qaeda is thus surviving precisely through this differentiation. JNIM capitalizes on ethnic and social conflicts in the Sahel, while al-Shabaab exploits conflicts within Somali clan structures. The global jihadist goal remains as an ideological framework, but the actual fighting takes place on the ground.

In the competition for public attention, however, al-Qaeda faces a superior rival: the so-called "Islamic State," or IS.

"Ultimately, younger jihadists tend to be more drawn to the 'Islamic State,'" terrorism expert Behnam Heidenreuter-Said, head of the research center for Strategic Police Research at the Hamburg Police Academy, told DW.

IS has carried out "more spectacular" and media-effective terrorist attacks, he said, highlighting that "al-Qaeda has taken a more long-term approach."

Heidenreuter-Said, who published a comprehensive history of al-Qaeda in 2018, added that "IS wanted to make a splash and demonstrate successes as quickly as possible in order to grow explosively."

However, IS's expansion was followed by its collapse, he said. Al-Qaeda, on the other hand, continues to exist, albeit under changed circumstances.

The terrorist group meanwhile faces the question of whether it can replace its aging fighters. Although this is conceivable, al-Qaeda as a brand is visibly fading, Heidenreuter-Said observed. In Europe in particular, militant jihadism has become less attractive. However, jihadist ideas have not disappeared, al-Qaeda has simply lost its former ability to mobilize large numbers of young people, he said.

It is hard to say just how dangerous the organization actually still is, Behnam Heidenreuter-Said points out.

## Germany sets its sights on Russia&apos;s shadow fleet

https://p.dw.com/p/5MMQlRoyal Marine Commandos detained a sanctioned tanker, the Smyrtos, in the English Channel in June 2026Image: LPhot Hutchins/Royal Navy/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementOn September 1, when the German government publicly blamed Russia for the failed drone attack on Leipzig Airport, it announced a series of retaliatory measures. Among them was a tougher crackdown on the so-called Russian shadow fleet. The term refers to often aging, poorly maintained and inadequately insured vessels, mostly oil tankers, with opaque ownership structures, which Russia uses in an attempt to circumvent sanctions.

But detaining such vessels is far from straightforward, Julian Pawlak, a maritime security expert at the German Institute for International and Security Affairs (SWP) in Berlin, told DW. International maritime law, he noted, is fundamentally designed to protect the freedom of commercial shipping.

"The transport of these goods on the high seas is not, in itself, illegal," Pawlak explains. "What complicates matters is that parts of this shadow fleet are linked to so-called hybrid activities, including acts of sabotage, espionage, and possibly drone operations and related disruptions."

Germany has not done enough to address the issue so far, argues Sara Nanni, a security policy spokesperson for the Green Party.

In a recent interview with DW, she complained: "Russia is financing both its war against Ukraine and its aggressive behavior toward Europe through its shadow fleet." Action must be taken against it, she said. "That is the only thing that could persuade Russia to return to the negotiating table."

She accuses the German government of failing to take action: "Other countries are doing it in the Baltic Sea, but Germany isn't. I keep hearing the interior minister say that it is technically or legally difficult. But I believe the decision not to do it is a political one. And I think it is wrong."

By "other countries," she is referring, for example, to Finland. In late 2024, the tanker "Eagle S" damaged the Estlink 2 power cable in the Gulf of Finland by dragging an anchor across the seabed. Finnish border guards subsequently detained the vessel.

Although a court later dismissed charges of aggravated criminal damage, Sönke Marahrens, a security expert at Kiel University, praised the Finnish response in an interview with DW: "They sent a clear message to the other side: We know what you're doing, and we're stopping you. And you could see that after this clear warning, the other side got the message and changed its behavior."

In addition to Finland, the United Kingdom, France and Sweden have also adopted a tougher approach toward suspicious vessels. Within its territorial waters, the Swedish Coast Guard specifically targets tankers and cargo ships suspected of belonging to the shadow fleet for inspection. Vessels are routinely detained if they are found to be carrying forged documents, lack adequate insurance, pose a risk to maritime safety or violate environmental regulations.

It is easy to assume that the German government's restraint is aimed at avoiding an escalation with Russia. That impression is reinforced by the fact that the Alternative for Germany (AfD), which is considered right-wing extremist in parts, won the state election in the eastern state of Saxony-Anhalt on a platform that, despite the war in Ukraine, advocates closer relations between Germany and Russia. According to opinion polls, concerns about the prospect of war in Europe were also a decisive factor for many voters. Russia's foreign minister, Sergei Lavrov, even described Germany's accusations against Moscow as "the beginning of a real war."

Johannes Peters of the Institute for Security Policy at Kiel University, however, believes such fears are unfounded.

"Other European countries have taken a more robust approach, and despite those concerns, and indeed despite Russian rhetoric, very little has actually happened," Peters said. "Russia has not escalated the situation. Instead, it has largely steered clear of confrontation. In particular, we have seen that laden tankers, which are extremely valuable, are now avoiding Swedish waters because Sweden has stepped up efforts to stop and inspect vessels."

After the attempted attack on Leipzig Airport, Chancellor Friedrich Merz said: "The federal government will make those responsible for hybrid attacks pay a price. They will pay for it."

Peters believes: "If you use strong rhetoric, sooner or later you have to back it up with action. The chancellor has put the government under pressure to follow through."

Security expert Pawlak, however, cautions that inspecting suspicious Russian vessels is not without risk.

"Of course, any such operation has to take into account the possibility that Russian warships may be escorting these vessels or, as has increasingly been the case, that armed crews with ties to Russian mercenary groups may be on board," Pawlak said. "Those are risks that must be factored into the equation."

Estonia experienced those risks firsthand in the case of the oil tanker "Jaguar." In May 2025, the vessel's captain ignored instructions from the Estonian authorities while passing through the country's coastal waters. Estonian officials attempted to stop and inspect the suspicious tanker. Russia responded by scrambling a fighter jet, prompting Estonia to stand down and allow the vessel to continue on its way.

"It is important to consider the region in which these activities are taking place," Pawlak says. "In the Baltic Sea, Russia is naturally much closer geographically and therefore has other options at its disposal, including the deployment of military units in support." Farther afield, however, for example in the English Channel between the United Kingdom and France, that becomes more difficult: "Russia cannot escort or protect every shadow fleet vessel."

While maritime security specialist Johannes Peters firmly supports taking action against shadow-fleet vessels where justified, he cautions against harboring illusions.

"We are not going to be able to shut down this shadow-fleet system through inspections alone," he said. "Rather, the aim is to saddle Russia's shadow fleet with risks and uncertainties and to increase the costs of operating it."

He offers a simple example: "If only one ship a day can make the journey instead of three, that translates into losses of hundreds of millions of euros a day. If a voyage takes not x days but x plus four, that, too, cuts into Russia's earnings. And revenue from oil exports is vital to Russia's war economy."

## Cyberattack in Berlin: 1.4 million files on the dark web

https://p.dw.com/p/5MOxJAfter a failed extortion attempt, hackers published 1.4 million records from the Berlin administrationImage: picture alliance/dpaAdvertisementIt was a story that almost went unnoticed amid all the clamor surrounding the closely watched state election in Saxony-Anhalt on September 6. This highly alarming story is one of many recent reports that have detailed attacks on infrastructure or drone attacks on airports carried out by hostile foreign powers. Incidents like this one in the German capital highlight just how vulnerable Germany's data networks are.

So what exactly happened? Since early September, employee files, official correspondence, pay stubs and even scanned ID documents have been appearing on the dark web. The hacker group Rhysida stole the data and then tried to extort the Berlin city government. They demanded a ransom of 30 bitcoins, which is equivalent to about €2 million ($2.3 million), or else they would publish the sensitive data.

But last Friday, Berlin's Governing Mayor Kai Wegner stated: "The State of Berlin will not give in to blackmail."

The data was subsequently published on the dark web, the part of the internet accessible only to tech-savvy users, where a lot of illegal activity takes place. In total, the data set consists of approximately 1.4 million records.

Two departments of the city government serving approximately four million residents have been compromised: the Department of Public Works and the Department of Transportation. This time, the attack wasn't carried out by foreign intelligence agencies, but by criminals.

The hackers initially gained access to protected data between August 7 and 14 without being detected. Rhysida, a cybercrime group believed to originate from eastern Europe, has been attacking public institutions around the world since 2023, claiming responsibility for nearly 280 attacks, primarily in the US.

The hackers were able to gain access when a Berlin Transportation Authority employee replied to a phishing email, a message disguised to appear genuine and benign. Opening one of the attachments inadvertently grants the attackers access to the victim's network.

In Berlin, the state government's secure fiber-optic network connects approximately 600 locations, ranging from municipal offices to senate departments.

The list of sectors that experts believe could be compromised illustrates the real gravity of the data breach. In addition to the personal data of employees and citizens, this includes information on combi-heat and power plants, fuel storage facilities, emergency power supply systems as well as prisons and water treatment plants.

In an interview with the Frankfurter Allgemeine newspaper, Jochim Selzer of the Chaos Computer Club said that the publication could have serious consequences for the people affected — even if the data only appeared on the hard-to-access dark web: "The more you know about a person, the better you can impersonate them and the better you can figure out what you need to know about them in order to, say, place an order on their behalf."

And what about the political authorities responsible? The Berlin state government has instructed its employees to change their passwords. That, however, sparked outrage from Thorsten Schleheider, the vice-chairman of Berlin's police union.

He said that Berlin's data has been inadequately protected for years and that many employees have not received sufficient training: "It is unthinkable that highly sensitive data was compromised for days, and the only action taken appears to be instructing employees to change their passwords."

To address the data breach, the Berlin Senate has set up a coordination office involving all Berlin administrative agencies. It has also established contact with federal agencies such as the Federal Office for Information Security (BSI), based in Bonn.

Mayor Kai Wegner said, "We will inform, advise and support those employees and Berliners who were affected as quickly as possible."

The Berlin authorities were, however, able to provide reassurance regarding at least one issue of concern. They announced that the September 20 election for the Berlin State Parliament is not at risk.

State Election Commissioner Stephan Bröchler told the Bild tabloid: "As things stand, all systems and processes necessary for the election remain unaffected — including election preparations, the actual election day itself and the steps leading up to the release of the preliminary results."

Chancellor Friedrich Merz (CDU) was at least indirectly affected by the data theft. The stolen information also includes details about the controversial expansion of the Chancellery in the heart of Berlin.

https://p.dw.com/p/5L7ESAround 2,800 elephants live in Pendjari in Benin, one of 24 national parks managed by African Parks on the continentImage: Katrin Gänsler/DWAdvertisementPrince Harry has long been a supporter of African wildlife and nature. Harry's role was to mainly open doors for African Parks, van Beemen says. His fame attracted media attention and helped the NGO gain access to potential donors. But his notorious falling-out with his family increasingly failed to improve the organization's coffers and image.

According to its own figures, African Parks manages 24 protected areas in 13 African countries, covering more than 20 million hectares. By 2030, the organization aims to manage as many as 30 parks.

Its business model is known as delegated management: African governments hand over the long-term management of protected areas to the group.

The land formally remains state-owned. But the organization takes on wide-ranging responsibilities, including conservation, infrastructure, tourism and anti-poaching operations.

For African governments, this model offers a major advantage: African Parks brings international funding, expertise and personnel to protected areas that many states would otherwise struggle to manage on their own.

In order to achieve its objectives on the ground, African Parks employs a host of armed rangers across the continent, who are allowed to use indelicate methods if and when needed; in some cases, its influence even extends to managing border controls.

"They take over the state's monopoly on the use of force within the park," says van Beemen, adding that some critics of the NGO have gone as far as describing African Parks as a "state within a state."

It is in this context that mounting allegations of human rights abuses have been recorded in recent years, with conservation efforts repeatedly coming into conflict with local communities.

The worst allegations of rights abuses emerged in 2023 at Odzala-Kokoua National Park in the Republic of Congo.

Members of the indigenous Baka community reported violence, torture and sexual abuse at the hands of African Parks' rangers.

The NGO subsequently commissioned the London-based firm Omnia Strategy to investigate the allegations, resulting in African Parks eventually acknowledging certain instances of human rights abuses two years later — though the full investigation report was never made public.

According to van Beemen, the report was not even shown to the government of the Republic of Congo, despite African Parks operating there under an agreement with the government. To this day, it remains unclear what exactly happened in Odzala-Kokoua, and how many people were affected.

African Parks has promised reforms while conceding past failures in its own systems and processes; still, it has remained highly intransparent about the details involved in the entire scandal, says van Beemen.

The Dutch journalist has meanwhile published a book on the whole affair, titled "Beasts and Men." The volume won several awards in the Netherlands and has attracted international attention and acclaim.

However, African Parks fundamentally rejects van Beemen's portrayal of the organization, saying his book contains "hundreds of factual inaccuracies" as well as "a similar number of deeply misleading statements."

Regarding the human rights abuse allegations, African Parks says some of the cases described by van Beemen had occurred outside the NGOs control.

At the same time, it stressed in a statement that it has a zero-tolerance policy toward abuse, and that there is training in place on human rights standards as well as a comprehensive complaints mechanism for transgressions.

But as part of his research, van Beemen also spoke to several former rangers and people affected by conservation operations in several African countries, where he says he repeatedly encountered a fundamental problem perpetuated by African Parks: the loose definition of what constitutes poaching.

According to the accounts he collected, the NGO also treats some locals who hunt animals for their own consumption as poachers, rather than using that term exclusively to describe professional poachers who kill rhinos or elephants for international criminal networks.

Van Beemen said that during his research in Zambia, former rangers told him about the brutal treatment the people taken into custody on such grounds can face.

The history of African Parks is also controversial. The organization was founded by Dutch businessman Paul Fentener van Vlissingen, who — according to van Beemen — reportedly held Africans in low regard.

Among other things, he refused to accept the international economic sanctions against the South African apartheid system during the 1980s.

A former close associate of the founder told the Dutch journalist that at the top echolons of the organization, Africans were allegedly considered incapable of protecting their own natural resources because they lacked the money and management skills to do so.

In its early years, African Parks was headquartered in the Netherlands where according to van Beemen, employees would routinely look at maps of Africa and plan where the organization could take over additional parks.

## Are electric vehicles really bad for the environment?

https://p.dw.com/p/5MN0SThe electricity used to charge EVs can contribute to carbon emissions, if it comes from burning fossil fuels like coal and gasImage: Gero Rueter/DWAdvertisement"The EU's car lie" — Germany's Bild tabloid newspaper didn't mince words when it reported on what it called the European Union's "dangerous" claim that electric vehicles were carbon neutral.

Markus Söder, the conservative state premier of Bavaria — home to major automotive brands like Audi, BMWand  Mercedes-Benz — was equally direct. Writing on social media, he called for an end to "the EU's phaseout of internal combustion engines" set for 2035. Stepping away from the phaseout, he said, would now be backed by "science."

Söder and Bild were reacting to a new report by the Technical University of Munich (TUM), which outlined what it considered to be a major flaw in the EU's climate strategy: the fact that its vehicle emissions regulation only focuses on CO2 emissions from exhaust gases and that battery-electric vehicles are considered "climate-neutral."

The report stated that battery-electric vehicles reduce carbon emissions by only "41% on average" over combustion engine vehicles, with the EU regulation failing to take into account emissions generated by other factors like manufacturing and electricity mix.

Gas-powered vehicles were at a disadvantage, the report concluded, suggesting emissions should be counted over a vehicle's entire life cycle, from the materials used to build a car to the fuel that makes it run. Credit could be given for the use of green steel, for instance, or recovering raw materials through recycling.

It highlighted several major car manufacturers that support this approach, including BMW, Audi and Volkswagen — all major institutional donors at TUM.

Georg Bieker, who researches the environmental impact of electric and gas-powered cars at the US-based International Council on Clean Transportation (ICCT) think tank, said TUM's analysis didn't offer any new insights. He, and other climate and mobility experts, pointed out that the importance of life-cycle assessments was already well known.

Bieker co-authored a 2025 study examining the global warming potential of passenger cars sold in the EU which took into account their entire life cycle, from the production of the vehicle to its fuel consumption and maintenance. It found that the emissions generated by battery-electric vehicles are on average 73% lower than gasoline cars, and up to 78% when using only renewable electricity. Similar studies, including from the EU and the International Energy Agency, came up with emissions reductions of between 60%-66%. The Germany Environment Ministry said modern vehicles could even achieve reductions of up to 80%.

Hybrid vehicles, meanwhile, which combine a combustion engine with an electric motor and battery charged by the car's brakes and an internal generator, generate fewer emissions than traditional cars, but still don't match fully electric vehicles.

Bieker said the full EV advantage will only grow as production and operation becomes less carbon intensive. Even today, he added, production emissions for gasoline internal combustion vehicles only account for around 13% of the total climate impact; most of the emissions come from the tailpipe.

Bieker confirmed batteries were still one of the main environmental challenges facing electric cars. The mining of key resources like cobalt and lithium has been linked to habitat destruction and water depletion; mining 1 ton of lithium, enough for around 125 EVs, requires some 2 million liters of water.

Bieker's ICCT study found that while battery-electric vehicles had an estimated 40% higher production emissions than gas-powered vehicles due to battery production, these additional emissions were offset after driving about 17,000 kilometers (10,500 miles), or in one or two years. And, Bieker pointed out, most of those batteries could eventually be recycled — unlike the gasoline burned in a combustion engine.

"We see a trend of emissions decreasing for battery production," Bieker told DW, pointing out that technology was still being developed. The production of lithium-iron phosphate (LFP) batteries, for example, generates fewer emissions than the nickel manganese cobalt variety. LFPs are increasingly becoming the battery of choice for the industry — they're generally safer, last longer and are cheaper to manufacture.

Bieker said batteries were also increasingly being produced in Europe in larger, more efficient factories, which also helps reduce emissions generated by importing them from abroad. Though Europe still ranks second behind China when it comes to battery cell manufacturing, the European Commission has committed to boosting the sector with financial support, including a recent €1.5 billion in interest-free loans.

The other major factor contributing to EV emissions is the electricity mix — how the power charging the batteries is actually generated. While electric cars don't produce emissions during driving, they can generate them indirectly when charged using electricity produced by burning fossil fuels like coal and gas.

But the rapid expansion of renewable energy in recent years — especially solar, including a growing popularity of home installations — is increasingly tipping the scale in favor of EVs.

"Europe's electricity grid is currently more than 70% decarbonized and expected to be almost fully decarbonized by 2040," said Gabrielle Clark, a policy officer at European trade federation Eurelectric focusing on energy, climate and sustainability. "As the production of green steel and battery recycling capacity also ramps up as expected, this will only strengthen the case even more."

Clark said it wasn't necessary to replace the EU's current tailpipe emissions regulation, which plans to phase out combustion engines entirely by 2035, with more complex regulations and green accounting rules.

"Whether assessed through [life-cycle assessment] or tailpipe emissions, [battery-electric vehicles] are always the preferred technology for carbon emission reductions in the road transport sector," she told DW in an email. https://p.dw.com/p/5MMigAs Myanmar's newly minted head of state, Min Aung Hlaing has been hosted by the leaders of India, China, Laos, Thailand, Russia and now Vietnam — six countries in five monthsImage: Aung Shine Oo/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementMyanmar coup leader Min Aung Hlaing wrapped up a three-day state visit on Sunday to Vietnam, where the Southeast Asian neighbors vowed to boost defense and security ties.

There was nothing unusual about the trip itself, except that it marked the ex-general's sixth official trip abroad since assuming Myanmar's presidency in April, when he was voted in by a parliament stacked with loyalists following military-run elections in December and January that were widely considered a charade.

As Myanmar's newly minted head of state, Min Aung Hlaing has been hosted by the leaders of India, China, Laos, Thailand, Russia and now Vietnam — six countries in five months. He'll be heading to Cambodia on Friday to make it seven.

"Since the December-January elections and Min Aung Hlaing's transition to president after giving up his role as commander-in-chief, he's really put a renewed focus on diplomatic partnership and trying to shore up the government's allies in the region,” said Hunter Marston, head of the Southeast Asia Program at the Lowy Institute, a Sydney-based think tank.

"Even if they're relatively short trips to regional friends and allies, I think it shows that there is an acceleration of Naypyitaw's strategy to diversify its partnerships and to normalize relations bilaterally,” he added, referring to Myanmar's capital.

Marston and other analysts told DW that Myanmar is slowly returning to the international stage, aiming to shed its pariah status despite the mountain of war crimes allegations leveled against the regime — some against Min Aung Hlaing himself.

Since Min Aung Hlaing's military toppled the democratically elected government of Nobel laureate Aung San Suu Kyi in 2021, civil war has killed some 100,000 people and displaced over 3 million.

Last month, the United Nations said human rights conditions in the country had sunk to a new low, as the military keeps up a campaign of forced recruitment, arson and often indiscriminate air strikes on contested and resistance-held areas.

Myanmar's regime has made little progress on the plan so far and rejected repeated requests for ASEAN's special envoys to the country to meet with Aung San Suu Kyi, who was arrested the morning of the coup and has been held in custody since.

Last month, the country's newly installed parliament passed a motion flatly rejecting the bloc's peace plan, and its Foreign Ministry said ASEAN's special envoys were no longer needed.

Under the circumstances, Min Aung Hlaing and his ministers are unlikely to be attending ASEAN's senior leaders summits "any time soon,” said Pinitbhand Paribatra, an associate professor of international relations at Thailand's Thammasat University.

Marston and Marciel, though, say there's a good chance ASEAN will welcome Myanmar fully back into the fold once Thailand assumes the bloc's rotating, one-year chairmanship in 2028.

With significant business and security interests in Myanmar and a shared border stretching over 2,400 kilometers, Thailand has been keen to placate the country's regime and to urge the rest of ASEAN to do the same.

Thailand's premier and foreign minister "have been very clear that they want to pursue calibrated engagement [with Myanmar] and that they have already seen sufficient progress to incentivize further engagement," said Marston.

Beyond China, Russia and Southeast Asia, Marston and Pinitbhand say Myanmar may also find countries to reengage with among Russia's authoritarian allies in Central Asia and elsewhere. Min Aung Hlaing made visits to Belarus even before he was president.

Among democratic countries, Pinitbhand said Japan was "more than ready" to restore normal relations with Myanmar.

"I don't think that Min Aung Hlaing will be traveling to Japan any time soon. But … there might be some other attempts from Japan to, for example, resume the relationship, because the Japanese private sector already has lots of investments and everything inside the country," he said.

Marciel pointed to both Japan and South Korea as countries that might start normalizing their relations with Myanmar in the next year or so.

As for countries in the West, the analysts say they see little to no chance of them welcoming Min Aung Hlaing and his regime back onto the world stage or of lifting the heavy sanctions they have placed on them.

Marciel also said that Myanmar was low on the Trump administration's list of priorities. But with human rights also low on that list, he added, there was a chance it would warm to the regime for the right commercial or economic opportunities.

On the whole, though, the former ambassador says the West won't be embracing Min Aung Hlaing and his regime for now.

"I don't see any movement to ease sanctions among the Western countries that maintain them, as they all see the recent elections as a sham and also do not give much priority to Myanmar matters in their foreign policy," he said. "Things could change, but that is how it looks for now."

The work is believed to have been made in England nearly 1,000 years ago before finding its way to Bayeux, now located in the French region of Normandy.

Two previous attempts to loan the Bayeux Tapestry to the UK were unsuccessful. Once was in 1953 for the coronation of Queen Elizabeth II, and again in 1966 for the 900th anniversary of the Battle of Hastings, which is depicted on the tapestry.

The Bayeux Tapestry is a 70-meter long artwork, which is thought to be incomplete, depicting the Norman conquest of England in 1066.

Norman forces led by William the Conqueror were able to defeat and dethrone King Harold Godwinson in a campaign culminating in victory at the Battle of Hastings. The tapestry famously depicts Harold being shot in the eye with an arrow, as he is said to have died — admittedly in what modern historians believe is likely a myth or artistic exaggeration.

This conquest was a crucial contributing moment in the establishment of what would become England and later the British Empire and the UK. It was the last of several successful foreign invasions of England in a millennium, and turned out to be the last one for a millennium.

The conquest also added the final core ingredient, French, to the linguistic melting pot that produced the modern English language.

The embroidered cloth, which has brief Latin titles captioning its 58 scenes, is widely accepted to have been made in England as a tribute to William after his victory. It was then transported to his family home in Normandy.

"For too long, the EU has kept treating Serbia as a normal candidate country," Engjellushe Morina of the European Council on Foreign Relations told DW, despite Belgrade's continued backsliding on democracy and the rule of law.
