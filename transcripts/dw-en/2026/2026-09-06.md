## Ebola: Why children are last to be treated in medical crises

https://p.dw.com/p/5LgXjChildren have been badly affected in the current Ebola epidemic, and treatment options for them remain limitedImage: Gradel Muyisa Mumbere/REUTERSAdvertisementThe current Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has been devastating. Since the outbreak began in mid-May, more than 3,000 people have died.

Almost half of people infected with Ebola are expected to die, and the mortality rate is particularly high for young children. According to a commentary by Doctors Without Borders recently published in medical journal The Lancet, the mortality rate for children under the age of 5 is over 60%.

But often, it's because of a lack of medication and information on how to use it with young patients, rather than then disease itself.

"Children must be centered in Ebola treatment and prevention research," the aid organization wrote in early September.

At the heart of the debate is the EBO-PEP study, which began mid-July. The study looks at an experimental antiviral drug called obeldesivir, which is being studied for its ability to protect people exposed to the Bundibugyo Ebola virus, the strain responsible for the current deadly outbreak.

But children weighing less than 30 kilograms (about 66 pounds) and around 12 years old or younger, were not included in the original study. But that could change soon with protocols to include children weighing over 20 kilograms.

Children weighing less than 20 kilograms — that is, roughly 6 years old or younger — won't be included in the treatment. There is an alternative treatment for them: Another antiviral drug, remdesivir, given intravenously. But as Doctors Without Borders argued, a 10-day course of infusions is difficult to administer during an outbreak, especially for young children.

According to Neal Russell, a pediatric doctor based in London who consults for Doctors Without Borders and who was also the lead author of the Lancet commentary, young children are at particularly high risk from Ebola. This could be due to their weakened immune systems, underlying health conditions, or the fact that treating them for Ebola is especially challenging in an outbreak.

For example, children are often harder to diagnose than adults because early symptoms resemble other illnesses, and young children often find it difficult to describe their symptoms accurately. Other issues include the fact that children cannot always be accompanied into an Ebola treatment center by their parents or caregivers, something that is especially important in their recovery. Younger Ebola patients also require closer monitoring and specially trained staff.

In May, an American doctor was treated at Berlin's Charite hospital after contracting the Bundibugyo Ebola virus while caring for patients. His wife and their four children were also considered at high risk. The medical team in Germany decided to treat the family prophylactically with an experimental antibody drug called MBP134.

"Of course, a careful risk benefit assessment had to be carried out," said Han Ngoc Le, a doctor at the hospital who was involved in the treatments.

The available data about the drug's impact on children was very limited. But at the same time, the children were at high risk of catching Ebola after close contact with their sick father.

In the end, all the family remained healthy and no apparent side effects were seen. It's unclear whether the experimental treatment prevented infection in the children. Such a small sample can't be mapped onto the larger population either.

But the test was valuable nonetheless. "The mere fact that the antibody was so well tolerated even in 1-year-olds and children under 12 is a valuable finding upon which future studies can build," Le said.

Le explained that, from a medical perspective, children cannot simply be thought of as "small adults."

Their metabolisms function differently, they react differently to medications, their pharmacokinetics — what a body does with a drug — are simply different. The same sort of cautions also apply to pregnant women.

That's why dosages of medication can't simply be transferred from adults to children. It's also why any new medications require separate studies so that specific dosages can be determined and potentially special formulations can be developed. Children might require kid-friendly syrups instead of adult-size tablets, for example.

"All of this creates extra costs," said Marc Biot, access coordinator for Europe at Doctors Without Borders, who works to ensure people in need can get affordable, good-quality medical products. He said research institutions and pharmaceutical companies often see investing extra in pediatric studies as a lower priority.

"It is faster and it can be cheaper if you only focus on adults," said Biot — which is why treating children and pregnant women usually only becomes part of the research at a later stage of drug development. "That is our plea — a plea not only to the scientists, to the pharmaceutical companies, but also to those who fund and allow trials to happen — to allow that to happen on the same speed for adults as for children and pregnant women."

Biot said it's sad to see that pressure is needed to make it happen. "Whenever you start a clinical trial, you also should include pregnant woman or children," he argued.

None of this is new. In 2020, the Global Accelerator for Pediatric Formulations network was founded — supported by the World Health Organization(WHO) — to accelerate the development and availability of child-friendly medicine worldwide.

This isn't an issue limited to Ebola. Russell pointed to the 2024 mpox epidemic in the Democratic Republic of Congo, where children made up a large proportion of those infected or killed.

Although a vaccine existed for mpox, it wasn't made for children. The WHO advised it be used "off label" for minors, but they tended to get it a lot later than adults anyway. Experts say they've observed similar patterns with other diseases including HIV, tuberculosis, and during the COVID-19 pandemic.

"When you're doing a research study, which is not in an emergency, [it makes sense] to protect children and pregnant women from any potential harm of an investigational product," said Russell. "But when you're in an emergency, the balance between protecting those people from any potential harm from research, and protecting them from the very fatal disease, changes."

Berlin-based doctor Le believes children and pregnant women should be considered right from the beginning of any new clinical trials. "They shouldn't be excluded from participation just like that," she said. "Instead, sub-studies or parallel studies should be set up from the outset."

Russell calls for a similar approach. He wants to see all people benefit equally from scientific progress during a health crisis — especially in a health emergency, when the most vulnerable should not be the last to benefit from that progress.

"No matter who you are, you should have the same opportunity to have access to these medical products," he said.

## Hungary begins hunt for corrupt regime&apos;s billions

https://p.dw.com/p/5LfmSIn his election campaign, Peter Magyar promised to recover government funds that were stolen or diverted by the Orban administrationImage: Attila Kisbenedek/AFPAdvertisementAsset recovery — it sounds bureaucratic but behind this phrase is one of the most important campaign promises made by Hungary's new government.

Hungary's new prime minister, Peter Magyar, and his Tisza party want to retrieve funds and assets that went — in some dubious ways — to the family of ousted Hungarian leader Viktor Orban or his allies, various oligarchs, or frontmen. The new government wants those funds back for state use, and it also plans to hold the beneficiaries accountable under law.

Billions of euros were stolen or diverted and, if social media is to be believed, many Hungarians want to see the individuals responsible behind bars. Magyar has talked of a sum of around €60 billion ($70 billion), though other estimates suggest the real amount could be three times that much.

Observers say the success or failure of the asset recovery will almost certainly influence the future of Magyar and the Tisza party.

The huge sums of money are linked to images of decadent luxury under Orban. The former Hungarian prime minister's family, party members, and friendly oligarchs lived on luxury estates, flew in luxury jets, vacationed on luxury yachts, and repeatedly won billions worth of public contracts.

Orban-affiliated entrepreneurs, artists, academics, and journalists were also generously supported with public funds for years. Often their main qualification was loyalty to the former prime minister.

Those lifestyles contrasted with the lives led by Hungarians in poverty, the image of neglected public infrastructure, poor conditions in schools and hospitals, and the exodus of many young Hungarians from the country, as well as a huge deficit in the state budget, some 7.5% of Hungary's national income. These contrasts explain why there is such a popular desire for justice.

It's been almost four months since the new Magyar government took office and anti-corruption actions have officially started.

At the end of July, the new government established the National Asset Recovery Authority, or NVVH — a sort of "super institution" with very broad powers and independence.

Hungary hasn't had an authority like this in its modern history. The way state assets were privatized in the 1990s after communist rule was problematic, as it often was elsewhere in the former Soviet bloc. When Orban first came to power in 2010, there was a half-hearted attempt to address the corruption of the previous government under Ferenc Gyurcsany, but it came to nothing.

The NVVH will investigate where public funds flowed and how assets like real estate were used. Although no specific date has been set, the investigations will primarily focus on the Orban government's time in power, so dating back to 2010. The authority is also meant to make sure this cannot happen again, and will look into how public funds and assets are to be used in future.

The NVVH has both policing and prosecutorial powers, so it can investigate cases and bring charges. The authority is accountable to Hungary's parliament.

Late last week, parliament appointed Anna Unger, 46, a political scientist at Eotvos Lorand University in Budapest, to head the new anti-corruption and asset recovery body. Unger is an expert on political systems, democratic institutions, and party financing and was chosen out of almost 100 other applicants in a process with several stages.

Some were surprised at Unger's appointment, as she wasn't really a public figure. Her research has included political corruption but she's not a recognized expert on the topic, nor is she a lawyer. Observers had expected a lawyer or an anti-corruption expert to get the job, somebody like Miklos Ligeti, head of legal affairs at Transparency International Hungary since 2012.

Ligeti also applied for the job but is reportedly being considered for a position as a deputy director. Unger will have four deputies with different duties and responsibilities. Katalin Tasnady, a well-known prosecutor with around two decades of criminal law experience, was made the NVVH's deputy chief of investigations.

Government representatives said Unger had presented a comprehensive political vision for the agency's work. During her parliamentary hearing, she said that under Orban, Hungary saw more than just what one might call classic corruption. An almost feudal system had prevailed, she argued. That was why in addition to restitution and asset recovery, the system had to be thoroughly investigated and exposed so that it would never happen again, Unger said.

Unger caused the biggest upset when she said it would be folly to expect legally binding verdicts in any major corruption cases during the NVVH's first six-year term.

Robert Puzser, a popular commentator and personality on social media, called Unger's statement "devastating." Voters had given Magyar "unprecedented power to hold the country's plunderers to account," he said on Facebook, and if Hungarians had to wait more than six years, it could cause the collapse of this new government.

The new prime minister himself reacted angrily to critics who said the NVVH should have had a lawyer at its head.

"Nobody promised people they could vote for the leader of the NVVH as if it was a [reality TV] casting show," he wrote on Facebook. Plenty of experts thought Unger was absolutely suitable for the job.

In a podcast on the 24.hu media outlet, political science Gabor Torok surmised that the way that the NVVH had been established indicated that it would be a "strong political institution" that aimed to come to terms with the recent past.

Torok said the public expects the Tisza party to thoroughly reckon with the old system, and the new government will only be able to maintain the public's trust if it doesn't look like the "culprits of the previous order" are getting away with their past crimes, he said.

Nonetheless, the vote has become a major challenge for the federal government coalition of the conservative Union — of the Christian Democrats and their Bavarian sister party the Christian Social Union (CDU/CSU) — and the center-left Social Democrats (SPD). In the last few months, there were even rumors in Berlin that German Chancellor Friedrich Merz of the CDU could step down following this vote — or that he would be forced to by his own party.

This is because the in-part right-wing extremist Alternative for Germany (AfD) party leads the polls in Saxony-Anhalt, far ahead of the centrist parties governing at the federal level, the CDU and SPD. Even an absolute majority for the right-wing populists cannot be ruled out. If that came to pass, the election would have serious consequences for the federal government in Berlin.

Political scientist Carsten Koschmieder from the Free University in Berlin does not believe Merz will give up.

"I do not have the impression that he is a person who would resign," Koschmieder told DW. "Rather, he is very glad to be chancellor and has long worked toward that." In addition, there is currently no ready successor to Merz in the CDU and CSU, he added.

An absolute majority for the AfD could, however, plunge the governing coalition in Berlin into crisis, Koschmieder said. It would also intensify pressure regarding the correct way to deal with the AfD, which is in parts right-wing extremist.

Before he was elected chancellor in May 2025, Merz set the goal of halving support for the AfD, in part by adopting a tough migration policy. The fact that this has not succeeded, and instead the CDU has lost support, has yielded him much criticism within his party. His Union of the CDU and CSU has categorically ruled out working with the AfD, as have all other established German political parties.

It's also possible that the CDU in Saxony-Anhalt ends up forming a minority government with the SPD and the environmentalist Greens. But even such a three-way coalition is unlikely to have a majority in parliament.

This would mean it has to be tolerated, ironically, by the socialist Left Party. Ironic, because the CDU also wants to avoid any cooperation with the Left, as the chancellor reiterated in an interview with regional newspaper the Mitteldeutsche Zeitung: "Both the AfD and Left Party have such fundamental differences with the Union that cooperation is out of the question," Merz said on Tuesday.

Here, Koschmieder said, a double problem arises for Merz: Many local-level CDU politicians, especially in eastern Germany, consider cooperation with the AfD quite possible, and have categorically rejected working with the Left Party.

But many other conservative politicians fear the opposite: that engaging with the right-wing populists would massively damage the CDU/CSU. "There are many conservative democrats in the CDU who say: We have nothing to do with the AfD. They want to abolish our democracy; they support Russia's President Putin and oppose Western integration and European unity," Koschmieder explained.

In other words: It is mainly for the chancellor's party that the election result in Saxony-Anhalt could become a crucial test. Merz has already identified this issue, and has made recent calls for calm.

"Outsiders are trying to drive a wedge into the Union. The result of a state parliament election does not endanger the stability of the federal government," he told the Mitteldeutsche Zeitung.

The chancellor seems resolved to carry on with his government, even amid growing dissatisfaction and rising pressure. This year, he still plans to bring his controversial reforms of the old age pension system through parliament, which have been heavily criticized by members of his own party on the campaign trail in Saxony-Anhalt.

But that hasn't discouraged Merz. "The pension system is no longer sustainable because of demographics, and we have known this for years," he said.

Political scientist Koschmieder also thinks many CDU politicians at the state level will continue their criticism of the pension reform plans, but he added: "I do not think they will completely fail, but there will be amendments in some places."

Pressure from his own party, problems with upcoming reforms — all of this could be in store for the chancellor. And that's not all: Interior policy and security experts at the federal and state levels have warned of high risks if a possible AfD government in Saxony-Anhalt has access to sensitive data — when it comes to counterintelligence, for example.

Georg Maier, the SPD interior minister in the eastern state of Thuringia, told German media outlet RND: "If an AfD politician becomes the interior minister of Saxony-Anhalt, that would be in my view a glaring security risk for Germany."

But also here, in northeastern Germany, the AfD is the strongest polling party, at around 35%.

## German company successfully launches rocket to space

https://p.dw.com/p/5LghZThe Spectrum rocket made history as the first commercial launch to reach orbit from continental EuropeImage: Isar Aerospace/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementA Spectrum space rocket built by German company Isar Aerospace was launched into space from the Norwegian spaceport at Andoya.

"We're in orbit! And just made European history: This is the first time a privately developed rocket reached orbit from continental Europe," the company wrote on X.

"Germany has spaceflight capabilities," said the country's Ministry for Research, Technology and Space on X, praising a "milestone that impressively demonstrates our country's innovative strength and technical expertise".

This was the German company's second attempt to launch the Spectrum rocket into space. The first attempt, which took place 18 months ago, was unsuccessful.

The Spectrum rocket is designed for small and medium-sized payloads. It carried five smal satellites and an in-flight technological experiment, the company said.

According to Isar Aerospace, the launch was for "both the vehicle's qualification mission and its first flight carrying payload."

The move is believed to bring Europe closer to being able to offer satellite flights from its own territory. Several countries voiced interest in the growing market of commercial space missions, including Britain and Sweden.

In March, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz visited the launch site of Isar, a Bavaria-based company, showing support for its efforts and for European autonomy at a time when the world order is changing under US President Donald Trump.

The company said that while the US launched 198 rockets last year, Europe's tally was just eight. Isar's goal is to produce some 40 rockets a year.

Although the rocket is not yet ready for mass production, Isar has already received orders through 2028.

The Spectrum rocket is intended to send both civilian and military satellites into low Earth orbit, meaning a few hundred kilometers above the Earth's surface.

The industry includes Elon Musk's SpaceX and ArianeGroup, a French joint venture between Airbus and Safran that operates a spaceport in French Guiana, located in South America.

For the sake of developing new rockets and extend its testing and launch infrastructure, Isar said last week it received €200 million ($232 million) from the European Space Agency (ESA).
