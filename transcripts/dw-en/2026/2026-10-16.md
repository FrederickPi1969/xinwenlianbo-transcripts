European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and European Council President Antonio Costa both took the fore in calling on allies to continue their support of Kyiv as it faces increased Russian attacks and detrimental US foreign policy decisions.

"This is no time to withdraw support to Ukraine," said Costa as he stood next to the Ukrainian president. "This is no time to relieve Russia's sanctions. Now it is time to increase our support, to increase our pressure."

"One ​thing is absolutely clear," pledged von der Leyen, "we will ​not lift any sanctions unless Russia ⁠stops ​this ​war."

The EU leaders' remarks come just one week after Ukraine and its European allies were blindsided by US President Donald Trump's unilateral decision to lift sanctions on Russian oil exports and purchase diesel from the Kremlin after a phone call with Russian President Vladimir Putin.

On Wednesday, a US official said Washington was also considering easing sanctions on Russia's shadow fleet — unflagged tankers that turn off transponders to transport and transfer illicit oil while circumventing international export sanctions.

After undermining years of Western sanctions against Russia over its illegal invasion of Ukraine, Trump is now seeking to increase pressure on Ukraine — calling for Zelenskyy to be replaced as its leader and blasting Kyiv's targeting of Russian energy infrastructure as a "disgrace."

Upon arrival at Friday's Brussels summit, Zelenskyy said, "We want to end this war as quick as possible. We don't see now signals from the Russian side that ​they are ready for this, to end this war, that's why we have to prepare for the winter."

During his speech at the summit, Zelenskyy asked European Union leaders to provide an additional €70 billion ($79 billion) in funding to Kyiv next year to help it continue fighting Russia.

"The war will cost €155 billion next year. And we need at least €70 billion euros more from you — for defense, for weapons, and for our resilience. This is a realistic amount. And I ask you to find this support," the Ukrainian president said.

When asked after his meeting with EU leaders if Trump was scapegoating him for troubles in the US, Zelensky gave a knowing smile and stayed mum. "This is my answer," the Ukrainian president said, as journalists broke into laughter at a press conference in Brussels.

Trump last week justified his calls for Zelenskyy to be removed from office by claiming the Ukrainian leader did not want to stop the war — which Russia launched on February 24, 2022 — as well as attempting to divert anger over high global fuel prices away from his own war in Iran, which triggered the closure of the Strait of Hormuz and sent global oil and gas prices through the roof.

"All he's doing is making the world oil price higher," Trump told far-right US media outlet Newsmax on Thursday. Asked about Ukraine resuming attacks on Russian energy infrastructure, Trump said, "Well, I think that's a disgrace."

Earlier this week, Kyiv reported restrictions on its use of Elon Musk's Starshield system, Starlink's military-grade satellite service which Ukraine relies on for its battlefield communications, mid-range strikes on Russian-occupied territories and interceptor drones.

## The EU wants an AI revolution. But on whose terms?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OIhkMicrosoft's vice chair and president, Brad Smith, addresses delegatesImage: Paul Faith/AFPAdvertisementStep-dancers spun across the circular stage in the middle of a dark hall, entrancing an audience of policymakers and tech bosses. Not simply another bureaucratic conference, the International AI Summit, hosted in Dublin this week, aspired to something more spectacular — a grand announcement that Europe is ready for its artificial intelligence (AI) revolution.

Among the more than 1,500 expected guests were Google DeepMind co-founder and chair Demis Hassabis and Microsoft president Brad Smith, in addition to high-ranking European Union and Irish officials. The event was a key part of Ireland's term as president of the EU Council, but a lack of critical voices to counterbalance the AI enthusiasm on display has drawn accusations of tech boosterism.

The summit was "almost like a party political broadcast on behalf of AI," Barry O'Sullivan, an AI professor at University College Cork, told DW.

The overarching theme was how to use AI to get Europe's sluggish economy moving, boost innovation and catch up with the US and China.

On panels packed with senior executives, the potential benefits were made clear: detecting early signs of cancer, improving warnings of extreme weather, and optimising power distribution to save energy. Turning those promises into reliable services, however, still requires testing and validation.

Ireland's interest in these issues doesn't come purely from goodwill towards its fellow EU member states. The country hosts the European operations of several of the world's largest technology companies, and relies heavily on the taxation of multinational firms. Foreign-owned multinationals paid €28.8 billion in corporation tax in 2025, accounting for 87% of the total, excluding exceptional Apple tax proceeds. That includes pharmaceuticals and other sectors, not just technology.

This means Dublin has a clear interest in shaping European rules while keeping investors on board. But Europe's financial firepower remains far behind America's: Private AI investment reached $286 billion (about €253 billion) in the US in 2025, compared with $21 billion (€18.6 billion) in Europe, according to Stanford University's AI Index.

But does Europe even need to compete on the same terms? O'Sullivan thinks it should focus on its own strengths, including industrial robotics and specialised applications: "We have a unique kind of AI opportunity in Europe, and that's what we should back."

Using foreign models could help European businesses move faster. But it also leaves them dependent on suppliers elsewhere, which can change prices, restrict access and decide which capabilities to release.

EU representatives at the summit were quick to underline that rules were now in place to reap AI's benefits while mitigating its risks.

Michael McGrath, the Irish commissioner responsible for democracy, justice, the rule of law and consumer protection, spoke of a balance between AI's potential and the "appropriate guardrails" required to ensure trust.

The AI Act bans certain practices and imposes stronger requirements on sensitive uses. Its Omnibus amendments, in force since July 2026, aim to simplify compliance, though have als delyed some safeguards. Rules covering areas including employment and education now apply from December 2027; those for AI embedded in regulated products from August 2028.

The Commission has said that companies need time to adjust to new standards and guidance. Critics, however, believe protections being delayed under pressure to compete.

For O'Sullivan, regulation and innovation are not contradictory. "The idea that you can't have regulation and innovation is just a nonsense," he said, pointing to pharmaceuticals and medical devices.

For protesters outside, assurances made by politicians and tech firms ring hollow. Paul Murphy, an Irish lawmaker from the socialist People Before Profit party, argued that even though Europe's regulations are strong than those in the US, the bar remains low. With the latest amendments, he sees a move towards deregulation.

Instead, he wants civil society, trade unions and politicians to discuss how the technology should develop and how limited resources like water and electricity should be used.

Inside, union representative Fionnuala Ní Bhrógáin, one of the few critical voices on stage, questioned who would benefit from higher productivity. Optimization, she said, can put higher pressure on workers.

Murphy, too, fears an increased pressure to work faster, with less autonomy and more supervision. Whether an hour saved on emails becomes time to think, or leads to more assignments, depends on employers' decisions and workers' bargaining power.

O'Sullivan, on the other hand, said that AI removing mundane tasks gives employees "more time to think, and to be more creative in their jobs."

In Ireland, the question of resources is particularly pressing. Data centres used 23% of the country's metered electricity in 2025. That includes cloud storage and other services, not just AI. Murphy argued that their expansion raises household bills and undermines climate goals.

Renewable energy could help meet demand. But additional clean electricity must also replace fossil fuels, while housing and other industries need grid capacity too.

"The sustainability of AI is a huge problem," O'Sullivan agreed, calling for the sector to reduce its overall energy footprint.

Back inside the hall, even industry leaders acknowledged the need for boundaries. Hassabis, the chief scientist of Alphabet, called recent AI hacking incidents "early warning shots" and advocated international oversight. Microsoft president Smith said: "We need law. We need regulation."

Their warnings follow concrete failures. During cybersecurity evaluations in July, OpenAI models bypassed isolation controls, hacked the infrastructure surrounding the test, broke containment, and attacked a real company, Hugging Face, which hosts AI models and datasets. Had similar behavior occurred inside a hospital, power grid, or other critical system, the results could have been much worse. Anthropic separately disclosed unintended actions by its AI on real websites, including a ​false homicide tip to the Philadelphia police, despite instructions not to create accounts or submit anything destructive.

Speakers on and off the stage agreed: The incidents show just how difficult it can be to keep increasingly autonomous systems under control. Developers' assurances alone cannot guarantee safety. Nor can regulation eliminate every risk. But for O'Sullivan and Murphy, it remains essential.

Europe wants to shape AI without compromising on the benefits its promises.This balancing act is a difficult one, involving choices about investment, resources and the distribution of gains. Even a spectacular opening performance cannot dance around those questions.

The plan aims to cut CO2 emissions and support the development and construction of electric cars in Europe, the statement added.

"Our shared ambition is clear: to successfully transform our automotive industry while maintaining our climate ambition and ensuring that the cars of the future are produced in Europe," the countries said.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz welcomed the agreement, describing it as a "good day for car drivers and for jobs in Germany, France and Europe."

## India&apos;s young workers have a new colleague: AI

https://p.dw.com/p/5OJyYThe use of AI is widespread among younger Indians, with a survey finding that 93% of Gen Z respondents and 95% of millennial respondents used AI in their day-to-day work Image: PUNIT PARANJPE/AFP/Getty ImagesAdvertisementAcross India, young workers are increasingly turning to artificial intelligence (AI) to speed up routine tasks, whether researching news stories, translating copy, analyzing business data or sorting legal documents.

Journalist Farah Kazmi, 28, who works for a news portal in New Delhi, used AI while reporting on rules governing stray animals and feeding them in gated communities. She asked an AI model to find the Supreme Court's latest guidelines, earlier court rulings and recent dog-bite cases.

"Since it can take quite a lot of time to search Google for specific articles about court rulings, previous judgments or recent dog-bite cases, I asked AI to find them for me," Kazmi told DW.

It gave her a starting point for relevant articles and cases, which she then read and cross-checked herself.

Kazmi also uses AI to translate English copy into Hindi for the Hindi news team, and Hindi quotes into English. But translations need careful checking, Kazmi said, pointing out that in one case, AI interpreted the name "Suraj" as "sun," changing the sentence's meaning.

"Before using AI, I mostly relied on Google Translate. It was more literal and did not always understand the context. AI gives a much better and more context-based translation, although it still needs to be checked and corrected," she said.

AI has not fundamentally changed journalism, which still involves talking to people and reporting from the field, Kazmi said.

"Ultimately, I see AI as a tool that helps with research and saves time," she said, "but the actual verification and reporting still has to be done by the journalist."

Yajatt Misra, 28, business strategy and international markets manager at Kanoria Chemicals and Industries, uses AI to analyze data, summarize large volumes of information, prepare first drafts and structure ideas.

It also helps him turn financial and business information from different sources into management updates and presentations.

"Things like summarizing information, preparing first drafts, analyzing data and putting together presentations take less time now," Misra told DW.

Misra said the challenge was knowing when to trust the output. AI can miss business context or get details wrong, so its numbers, logic and conclusions need checking.

"There is less need to spend time on repetitive tasks and more focus on analysis, problem-solving and providing useful insights to the business," Misra said.

Rakshanda Deka, a 30-year-old lawyer, uses AI to sort large bundles of client documents chronologically or by theme and produce a first list of important dates. Some systems link information to the original documents so lawyers can check the source.

AI can also help prepare standard applications and petitions using a lawyer's own format and style.

"If used sensibly, and without outsourcing the substantive legal thinking, AI is basically like having a sincere fresher or junior colleague," Deka told DW.

"AI often gives you false information dressed up very confidently as fact," Deka said. "So it is imperative that you double-check everything."

Deka is concerned that some young lawyers may use AI as a substitute for their own thinking. "A lot of the youngest lawyers are beginning to use AI as a substitute for themselves," she said.

The use of AI is widespread among younger Indians, with a survey by Deloitte this year finding that 93% of Gen Z respondents and 95% of millennial respondents used artificial intelligence in their day-to-day work.

An Emeritus survey found that 82% of respondents aged 15 to 28 used AI tools at least weekly to learn or develop new skills.

Dimple Khubchandani, founder of recruitment firm Synoverge Consultants, told DW that employers increasingly want young professionals who can think critically, solve problems and check AI-generated work rather than simply handle basic research or drafting.

"AI is not necessarily eliminating entire job categories, but it is creating leaner teams and raising expectations," she said.

Khubchandani said a marketing agency that once might have hired four junior copywriters could now hire one to use AI for initial drafts, leaving more time to edit, refine the tone and shape strategy.

Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), one of India's largest IT services companies, said in its 2025-26 annual report that it had scaled up its "Human+AI" operating model and was embedding artificial intelligence across services and software development.

Shrijay Sheth, co-founder of LegalWiz.in, an online legal and compliance service, sees the shift across the economy.

"AI's impact on job profiles isn't an anticipation," Sheth told DW. "It is a reality now. From basic research to strategy, operations, marketing, design and development, AI is changing how work gets done."

In software development, Sheth said, developers are spending less time writing code from scratch and more time monitoring, testing and refining AI-generated output.

"Business, product and problem-solving ability, coupled with skills around working with AI platforms, is valued over mere technical ability to work with any particular coding language," Sheth said.

A 2025 report by NITI Aayog, the Indian government's public policy think tank, cites data from the Center of Advanced Study in India suggesting that more than 60% of formal-sector jobs could be susceptible to automation by 2030 — particularly in IT and business-process services.

It also warns of slower hiring for entry-level software engineers and identifies junior quality-assurance and Level 1 support roles as vulnerable.

## How prepared is Kenya for an Ebola outbreak?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OHK4Kenya is on high alert after the country reported one death from Ebola and a second caseImage: Monicah Mwangi/REUTERSAdvertisementKenya is rushing to contain Ebola cases linked to the deadliest outbreak on record in the Democratic Republic of Congo. The East African nation has reported two cases and one death. The DRC has reported more than 9,000 cases and 4,400 deaths.

Kenya's first Ebola patient died at Nairobi hospital on October 5. The 40-year-old Kenyan had traveled by road from the DRC to Uganda before boarding a flight to Nairobi in Entebbe on October 3.

One of his relatives is now confirmed to have contracted the virus. The 39-year-old was under quarantine when he developed symptoms on October 13. "Both cases are linked, and ​there is no evidence of spread in the wider community," Kenya's health ministry said.

Contact tracing has been ongoing since officials first detected the case. Of the 128 people who came into contact with the first Ebola patient, 113 have been identified, according to the health ministry. All, except five — who are in unspecified countries abroad—have been quarantined.

Public anxiety is rising, along with fears of lockdowns and economic disruption, and skepticism about government readiness.

Another citizen said he was disappointed: "The government has been caught unaware. It appears to me that the government was not prepared, especially at the entry point of this particular person who was found to be suffering from Ebola."

One woman said she felt there was a lack of public information on the disease. "The government should do more in educating and sensitizing about it, and raising awareness," she said.

Kenya's Health Ministry, through Cabinet Secretary Aden Duale, has been seeking to allay fears, urging Kenyans to remain vigilant and encouraging anyone who develops symptoms to report to the nearest medical facility. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), symptoms can develop between two and 21 days after infection.

Early symptoms include: fever, fatigue, malaise, muscle pain, headache and sore throat. Later, a patient may suffer from vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, and kidney and liver infection.

However, these symptoms might be similar to other illnesses such as malaria or typhoid, and only an Ebola test can confirm if one has been exposed to the virus.

Several prominent figures, medical unions and opposition politicians have criticized Kenya's response to its first Ebola cases. Former Chief Justice David Maraga, who has declared interest in running for the presidency in the 2027 election, questioned how a man carrying the Ebola virus could pass through screening at Jomo Kenyatta International Airport, Nairobi.

"We don't know how many have been infected. We must demand appropriate action be taken so that we don't have deaths arising from that disease," Maraga told The Standard.

Kalonzo Musyoka, Kenya's vice president from 2008 to 2013, meanwhile noted that doctors at Nairobi Hospital, not border systems, detected the danger.

Dennis Miskellah of the Kenya Medical Practitioners, Pharmacists and Dentists Union believes that the Ebola cases show that Kenya's health system is not pandemic-ready. "This system is not yet resilient. This system did not learn from the COVID pandemic that we had a few years ago," he said.

"Since May, we've had this outbreak in the region. Uganda, our immediate neighbor, had almost 20 cases. So, we had ample time to prepare adequately. But it seems like somebody dropped the ball."

Kenya went on high alert after the DRC declared the Ebola outbreak in May. The government designated several hospitals as isolation centers.

It also enhanced screening at airports and border points, deploying mobile labs. Flights from several African countries were ordered to land in designated airport wings for screening.

"Top of it all is that there's has been a serious lapse of leadership in governance of healthcare this country," said Miskellah. "All those broken pillars, there's no clear responsibility or chain of command, whether its the Ministry of Health or the 47 county governments who are semi-automous."

Capacity at some isolation centers is limited. Rural facilities lack resources such as the protective gear available at major hospitals, according to Miskellah. And there's also a shortage of health workers due to a freeze on public service workers.

## Who was Eva Mamlok? Berlin square renamed after Jewish anti-fascist resistance fighter

https://p.dw.com/p/5OHWZThe only known remaining photo of resistance fighter Eva MamlokImage: Familienarchiv SiemsenAdvertisementIn the history of German resistance to Nazism, some names have become symbols: Sophie Scholl, Claus von Stauffenberg, Dietrich Bonhoeffer and Georg Elser. Yet many others have remained largely forgotten.

Among them is Eva Mamlok, a young Jewish woman from Berlin whose acts of defiance began in her teenage years and continued until her murder in a Nazi concentration camp.

Now more people will get to know her story as Berlin renames Blücherplatz, a square in the district of Kreuzberg, Eva-Mamlok-Platz on October 16.

"Eva Mamlok was a very remarkable person. As a young Jewish woman from Berlin, her spirit of resistance was particularly striking," said cultural researcher Alexandra Weltz-Rombach, who has worked extensively with the historians' network "Gruppe Eva Mamlok."

Mamlok was born on May 6, 1918, into a Jewish family in Berlin. They ran a wholesale wine business, and the family belonged to the vibrant Jewish middle class that formed an important part of Berlin's social and economic life during the Weimar Republic.

But Eva came of age just as Germany was descending into dictatorship. When Hitler became chancellor in 1933, she was only 14 years old. She was also a member of the Socialist Workers' Youth (SAJ). Following the suppression of the left-wing political youth organization later that year, she became active in the underground resistance.

According to testimony from Inge Berner (1922-2012), the sole survivor of Mamlok's later resistance group, Eva climbed onto the roof of a department store on Blücherplatz as a teenager and painted the slogan "Down with Hitler."

"She was caught doing that, though she wasn't arrested at that particular moment — that didn't happen until a year later," noted Weltz-Rombach.

Mamlok first appeared in official records in November 1934, after she was arrested for taking part in commemorations for socialist leaders Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht at Berlin's Friedrichsfelde Cemetery.

Authorities determined that the 16-year-old held "hostile views toward the state" and placed her in "protective custody until further notice" — a euphemism the regime used to justify imprisonment without trial. She was sent to the women's concentration camp at Moringen in Lower Saxony, where she remained until May 1935.

After her release, she continued her anti-Nazi activities despite the increasing danger, facing persecution both as a political dissident and as a Jew.

In these underground circles, she met Pieter Siemsen (1914–2004), and they remained a couple until he emigrated to Argentina in 1937. The only known surviving photograph of Eva comes from his personal archive.

She had a daughter, Tana, in 1939. The father's identity is unknown, however. The child was placed in a Jewish orphanage in Berlin and deported in 1942 to the Auschwitz-Birkenau extermination camp, where she was murdered.

Meanwhile, because she was Jewish, Mamlok was forced to perform compulsory labor at the Berlin firm F. Butzke Schrauben-Industrie in 1940. There she met other women who shared her opposition to Nazism.

Together they formed what later became known as the "Eva Mamlok Group," an informal resistance network of Jewish and non-Jewish women. They distributed anti-Nazi leaflets and wrote slogans critical of Hitler.

They also shared banned books. "They were books that are today part of Germany's educational canon," from Thomas Mann to Lion Feuchtwanger, said Weltz-Rombach. That the Berlin Central and Regional Library (ZLB), Germany's largest public library, stands on the new Eva-Mamlok-Platz is a fitting symbol of this act of resistance and the importance of books, she added.

Mamlok and others in her group were arrested for distributing this forbidden literature. On January 13, 1942, she was deported from Berlin to the Riga Ghetto in occupied Latvia.

According to Inge Berner's testimony, Mamlok remained active in the resistance there too. For example, she arranged for a contact still in Berlin to send her a cake with a miniature camera hidden inside it to document persecution in the ghetto.

In October 1944, she was sent to the Stutthof Nazi concentration camp, where she died in December of that year.

Blücherplatz was named after the Prussian field marshal Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher, whose most renowned achievement was leading his army against Napoleon at the Battle of Leipzig in 1813 and the Battle of Waterloo in 1815.

"There is no shortage of Blücher memorial sites," said Weltz-Rombach. "And perhaps it's also time to put an end to this old Franco-German enmity — which is, of course, inherent in the name Blücher, given its Prussian tradition — and to establish, in its place, a different form of remembrance."

The square also happens to be the site of the Carnival of Cultures, a multicultural street festival and parade held every year to celebrate Berlin's global diversity.

Having first heard about Mamlok when a Stolperstein commemorating her was laid in 2011, Weltz-Rombach has been researching her biography for the past few years. The Berlin-based filmmaker, producer and curator has also been sharing the resistance fighter's story through various projects, including an exhibition in 2024 and a podcast series with young Berliners.

Since Mamlok was only a teenager herself when she became politically active, the workshop participants can easily relate to her fate.

These teenagers learn about Mamlok's experiences at the actual locations where she was active, helping them imagine what it would be like to be arrested and imprisoned for six months without trial for doing things that normally wouldn't be considered crimes — such as simply expressing an opinion publicly or laying flowers at a commemorative event, explained Weltz-Rombach.

"Above all, she is, of course, a role model — an example of how one would like to act oneself, and how courageous one would like to be."

"[Women's] resistance was often of a more everyday nature, rather than planting bombs," said Weltz-Rombach. Their stories offer examples of "forms of resistance that have only recently received more attention." Other examples include a 2024 exhibition and the launch of a database dedicated to female resistance fighters.

Even though many of the eyewitnesses are now dead, Weltz-Rombach believes there are still many more of these stories that deserve to be brought into the spotlight: "There are definitely many names we do not know, that are waiting to be further researched."

## Starlink: How Elon Musk&apos;s company influences geopolitics

https://p.dw.com/p/58jZNStarlink satellites have become vital means of communication in war zonesImage: Depositphotos/IMAGOAdvertisementLack of internet access is a major problem in many regions of the world today.

Starlink was initially developed by the American tech billionaire Elon Musk to solve this problem. It was designed as a way of quickly getting the internet into regions all over the world where this was either impossible to do by conventional methods, or so expensive that it wasn't worth attempting.

Since then, Starlink's field of application has widened significantly. It's used as a means of communication in disaster zones, but also by armies or protest movements, for example in regions where the existing internet has been destroyed by enemy fire or shut down by authoritarian governments.

But there is a downside: Ukraine's military, for example, is heavily dependent on Musk's satellite system. This has become especially apparent as the service faces external disruption.

Starlink, which is operated by Musk's SpaceX company, is a satellite-based system consisting of 10,000 small satellites located around the world. They transfer data packages via laser links and do not require a constant, direct visual link to a ground station.

Unlike other internet satellites, which orbit at around 36,000 kilometers (22,000 miles) above the Earth, Starlink satellites are only about 550 kilometers up. This means they can transmit internet signals much faster than the others.

All users need is a small receiver terminal with an electronically controlled antenna, which automatically steers toward the satellites as they pass overhead. The terminals receive signals from these satellites and transmit them to a nearby router, which supplies the broadband internet connection.

Ukraine: Since the start of the Russia's full-scale war in February 2022, Starlink has become a vital instrument of communication in the war zone. The Ukrainian armed forces use Starlink to coordinate units, operate drones and control unmanned ground systems. In the civilian sector, the system is used by hospitals, emergency services, energy suppliers and the railway network.

Ukraine offers a particularly vivid example of how heavily its military capabilities now depend on Starlink.

In mid-October, Ukrainian army units reported disruptions to data transmission, which are said to have severely affected the control of combat, interceptor- and reconnaissance drones. Elon Musk said that restrictions on the speed and use of Starlink are determined by the US military and ultimately the US president. At this stage, it remains unclear who ordered the restrictions.

Iran: In early January 2026, protests erupted all across the country against the regime in Tehran. The government's hardline response included a nationwide internet shutdown. Reportedly, thousands of Starlink terminals were smuggled into Iran and sold on the black market. This enabled the protest movement to go on coordinating demonstrations against the regime, using Starlink to circumvent state censorship of the internet.

The country's authorities are cracking down on the satellite service and its users. Mohammad Amin Aghamiri, the secretary of the Supreme Council of Cyberspace, described Starlink as a hostile "tool of war" and said the regime had means at its disposal to disrupt satellite connections.

Starlink is officially banned in Iran. Anyone using the service faces a prison sentence of six months to two years. If users are also charged with endangering national security or espionage, the offence can even carry the death penalty.

Sudan: In the Sudanese civil war, Starlink is used primarily by the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), a paramilitary group fighting against the internationally-recognized government. Hundreds of devices were smuggled into the country via the United Arab Emirates, and there is now a thriving black market for Starlink terminals in Sudan. The RSF use them to communicate and coordinate with their militias, while the Sudanese Armed Forces are trying to block both the import and use of the terminals.

Venezuela: Here too, Starlink has been used in the past to sidestep information blackouts and secure access to the internet. For a long time, the service was only available unofficially, by buying kits from local dealers. Following the US intervention in early January 2026, during which then-head of state, Nicolas Maduro, was taken prisoner, Musk offered the people of Venezuela a month's free broadband internet service via Starlink.

Gaza: Starlink has been used for humanitarian purposes in the devastated Gaza Strip since July 2024. Aid organizations and a field hospital use it to facilitate telemedical assistance and logistical coordination. Access for civilians remains limited because of Israeli security concerns.

Starlink is often the only way to access the internet in conflict zones. This gives Musk and his operating company SpaceX tremendous influence. Previously, for example, Musk refused to activate Starlink in Ukraine to enable an attack in Crimea. US President Donald Trump has also threatened to switch off the satellites in Ukraine, as a way of forcing Kyiv to make concessions in peace negotiations.

There has also been criticism from an ecological perspective. The Starlink satellites have a lifespan of less than five years. Every day, on average, one or two of them burn up in the Earth's atmosphere. This releases aluminum oxide, which could affect the ozone layer, contributing to global warming. In addition, all those thousands of satellites reflect the sunlight, making the night sky brighter, and this may also have repercussions for the world's animals.

In an effort to break Starlink's monopoly, other companies, especially in Europe, are already working on alternatives. At present, the biggest private competitor to SpaceX is a company called Eutelsat. With a fleet of over 600 satellites, its OneWeb service is far smaller, focusing on business customers and maritime use.

But Eutelsat is also involved in the development of IRIS2 — an acronym for Infrastructure for Resilience, Interconnectivity and Security by Satellite, an ambitious EU project to establish a highly secure satellite network. The aim is to make Europe less dependent on Starlink, but IRIS2 won't be fully operational before 2032.

Elon Musk also has competition inside the US from another billionaire, Jeff Bezos, whose company Amazon is currently working on Amazon Leo, known until recently as Project Kuiper. This is establishing a network of more than 3,200 new low-Earth-orbit satellites. Amazon plans to roll out the service later this year in the US, Canada, France, Britain and Germany.

The sky is clearly getting crowded. China is also refining two satellite communications systems of its own. Guowang, a state project conceived primarily for national and military purposes, will consist of up to 13,000 satellites, while another project, Qianfan, with an estimated 12,000 satellites, is more commercially oriented and aims to provide services to newly industrialized and developing countries. The first mass starts for both of these projects are planned for this year.
