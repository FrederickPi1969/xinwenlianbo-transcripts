## Berlin: BER Airport Terminal 1 reopened after security scare

https://p.dw.com/p/5L2FLCrowds waited outside the terminal building amid the police operationImage: Ann-Marie Utz/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementTerminal 1 at Berlin Brandenburg Airport (BER) was reopened on Thursday evening following an earlier evacuation due to a security scare.

Federal police, responsible for transportation security in Germany, had evacuated BER Terminal 1 at around 5:45 p.m. after a woman reportedly told security staff at a service point that she had a dangerous substance in her luggage.

"A potential threat from the unidentified substance could not be ruled out," the police spokesperson told the dpa news agency.

Police then gave the all-clear at around 8:45 p.m. and confirmed that nobody had been in danger at any point.

Police initially provided no information as to the nature of the substance in question, with analysis results expected in the coming days.

"Due to the police operation, we will still be experiencing delays and some cancelations until the end of the day," said the airport spokeswoman.

She advised travelers to double-check the status of their flight before arriving at BER and to keep an eye on the boards while at the airport.

The airport had confirmed the incident itself earlier on Thursday but said planes that were already prepared to depart could take off as normal and incoming arrivals could also continue to land.

Passenger processing was "relocated to Terminal 2 to the extent that it's possible," a spokesperson said.

Many but not all flights departing Terminal 1 from around 7 p.m. local time were marked as delayed on the airport's website.

An airport press officer told DW that around 30 flights had been delayed but that they were trying to avoid cancellations by redirecting departures to Terminal 2.

Trains were also impacted by the operation for a time. The city's S-Bahn train service confirmed disruptions on both lines to airport, while Deutsche Bahn also reported widespread disruptions to regional trains.

BER or the "Willy Brandt Airport" in Schönefeld is Berlin's busiest and Germany's third-busiest airport since it opened in 2020. It also serves as the primary airport for many federal government flights. Some 26 million passengers travel through it each year.

The last comparable security disruption at the airport was in March of this year, when flights were disrupted but because of a potentially suspicious aerial object rather than a suspicious item in the terminal.

## Trump&apos;s Iran strategy: Could this become a forever war?

https://p.dw.com/p/5KwzuA US fighter aircraft launches from an aircraft carrier in the Arabian Sea (FILE: August 2026)Image: U.S. Navy/Planet Pix/ZUMA/picture allianceAdvertisementUS President Donald Trump has given a fairly clear description of what the next phase of the Iran war could look like. The Iranians had tried to rebuild their radar equipment, Trump stated after the most recent wave of US strikes. "We waited until it was almost built, and then we hit it," he said.

On Tuesday, American forces once again attacked several targets in Iran. According to US Central Command (CENTCOM), the targets destroyed included "air defense sites, radar systems, maritime assets and facilities, mine laying capabilities, and communications sites."

Iran responded by firing rockets and drones at US facilities in the region. With this, the war moved on to what CENTCOM had indicated just a few days earlier could be the strategy for the next phase.

According to the news portal Axios, it was considering limited strikes, at regular intervals if necessary, to prevent Iran from reconstituting its military capabilities in the Strait of Hormuz. An American government representative used a concept from Israeli security policy to describe this idea: He called it "mowing the grass."

The metaphor refers to a strategy that doesn't even attempt to promise that an opponent will be defeated once and for all. It is enough to regularly reduce the enemy's military capabilities to the point where the threat remains manageable. When the grass grows back, you "mow" it again.

Applied to the situation in Iran, this would mean the US keeping an eye on which military capabilities the enemy is reconstituting, and attacking once it crosses a certain threshold.

In the past few months, the US has scored some military successes. US forces have significantly reduced Iran's military capabilities in the Strait of Hormuz, and have begun clearing Iranian sea mines.

However, these successes will not last. Tehran is repairing or procuring new radar installations, anti-aircraft positions and anti-ship missiles. US bombs could certainly destroy important Iranian positions with targeted strikes. But the knowledge and the industrial basis of Iranian military technology would remain.

Reconstruction can therefore be delayed, but not prevented. The US will repeatedly be confronted with the same choice: whether to accept that Tehran is regaining its capabilities, or to attack again and destroy them.

Mona Yacoubian, director of the Middle East program at the Center for Strategic and International Studies in Washington, warned as early as April that this development could lead to an "enduring low-intensity conflict punctuated by more intensive interventions."

The fact that the strategy of "mowing the grass" is reaching its limits is also because it was never designed for a war with a state. Israel developed the strategy to combat non-state actors like Hamas.

However, as Yacoubian pointed out in her article, the Israeli academics who initially coined the term warned that if the opponent had "statist characteristics and/or more powerful capabilities, 'mowing the grass' might become an outdated military strategy." This is clearly the case with the Strait of Hormuz.

The US needs to keep the strait reliably open. Iran, on the other hand, only needs to make people believe that the passage remains dangerous. In this way, even a significantly weakened opponent can impose substantial costs on Washington.

Another problem for the US is that, while Washington can determine the intensity of its own attacks, it has no control over Tehran's response. This week, Trump warned Tehran not to launch a retaliatory strike, threatening that Iran would be "totally wiped out as a country" if it did.

Nonetheless, the regime in Tehran immediately fired missiles against American facilities in the region. Jordan alone reported 13 ballistic missiles in its airspace, 10 of which it was able to intercept.

But Yacoubian believes that repeated American attacks carry a far greater risk. She warns that they may lead Tehran to conclude that "pursuit of a nuclear weapon" is the only way to achieve "lasting deterrence." This suggests that, by attacking Iran, the US and Israel could actually accelerate the precise development they sought to bring under control.

From Washington's perspective, the alternatives available to Trump don't look much better. If the president accepts Iran rebuilding its military strength around the Strait of Hormuz, he risks renewed attacks on shipping and disruption of one of the world's most important energy routes.

The consequences for the global economy are well known. In spite of the upheaval and repeated negotiations, a political agreement between Washington and Tehran has so far failed because of apparently insurmountable differences.

For Trump, "mowing the grass" has domestic appeal, in spite of the uncertainty it brings to foreign policy. The US president would be able to avoid occupation, nation building and deploying large numbers of American ground troops.

At the same time, it allows him to distance himself from the so-called "forever wars" he repeatedly accused his predecessors in the White House of pursuing.

Ultimately, though, this supposed way of avoiding a forever war could become one of its own. Yacoubian describes it as a "Sisyphean attrition strategy without end." As long as Washington is not prepared at some point to accept the rebuilding of Iran's capabilities, each successful attack merely creates the justification for the next one.

## Germany: From emigration to immigration destination

https://p.dw.com/p/5Kev7A statue in the port of Bremerhaven commemorates the millions of Germans who emigrated abroadImage: S. Ziese/blickwinkel/picture allianceAdvertisement"Why should [they] be suffered to swarm into our Settlements, and by herding together establish their Language and Manners to the Exclusion of ours? [...] [They] will shortly be so numerous [...] and will never adopt our Language or Customs."

These defamatory comments about refugees and asylum-seekers sound as if they might have been made today by the right-wing populist Alternative for Germany party (AfD). Yet in this instance, the people vilified and accused of refusing to integrate are Germans — and the harsh judgement was made in the 18th century by none other than Benjamin Franklin, one of the Founding Fathers of the United States.

The pursuit of happiness and a better life are mentioned in the American Declaration of Independence, which Franklin helped write. Over the centuries, they have been the motivation for countless people to turn their backs on their respective homelands.

The Germans who emigrated to America in Franklin's lifetime were actually fairly limited in number. By 1820, only about 150,000 had successfully made the journey. Often, they were persuaded by compatriots who told tales of their tremendous success in their new homeland and celebrated the prosperity they had supposedly attained in a very short space of time. What most people back home didn't know was that these recruiters received a commission for every migrant.

"The crossing on a sailing ship cost about what a tradesman would earn in a year," said Simone Blaschka of the German Emigration Center in Bremerhaven. "To be able to afford that, families had to sell all their worldly goods."

They would have had no idea what awaited them on board the ship. During the crossing, which took several weeks, they were packed together below deck in a very confined space, plagued by bedbugs, fleas and rats. Many fell sick with dysentery, typhoid, smallpox or scurvy.

In his book "Journey to Pennsylvania in the Year 1750," the schoolmaster and organist Gottlieb Mittelberger described what he had experienced on the voyage: "Many people whimper, sigh and cry piteously for their homes; most of them get home-sick. Many hundred people necessarily die and perish in such misery, and must be cast into the sea."

On arrival in America, Mittelberger wrote, the majority of his fellow passengers were effectively auctioned off. These were people without means who were granted free passage after undertaking to work for years as bonded laborers in exchange. In the US, their contracts were sold off. Mittelberger saw families torn apart and parents forced to sell their children.

As these early migrants were traveling to America, there was simultaneously a big wave of emigration from Germany to the east. People followed the call of the Russian czarina, Catherine the Great, herself a German princess by birth.

On July 22, 1763, she issued a manifesto inviting people to come to Russia, with the aim of cultivating uninhabited regions for arable farming. She promised the new arrivals free transport to their chosen place of residence, land, also free of charge, the first year tax-free, schooling, exemption from military service, and a special political status that would allow them to maintain the German language and, for the most part, govern themselves.

However, in the 19th century the American continent once again became the dream destination. "The German population almost doubled in the 19th century, so you can imagine what the job market was like," said Blaschka from the German Emigration Center. The situation was exacerbated by failed harvests and famines.

"People didn't see any future for themselves or their children, and decided to emigrate," she said. In this way, around 6 million Germans were drawn to the New World.

"Between the beginning of the 19th century and the World War I, there were probably around 60 million Europeans who left their homes," said Jochen Oltmer, a professor at the Institute for Migration Research and Intercultural Studies in Osnabrück. Then, in the mid-20th century, this trend reversed and Europe went from being a continent of emigration to one of immigration.

In the 1950s and '60s, the future looked rosy for postwar West Germany and its "economic miracle." From 1956 onward, so-called "guest worker trains" arrived in stations like Stuttgart, Dortmund and Cologne, carrying Italians, Greeks, Spaniards, Portuguese, Yugoslavs and Turks, attracted by the promise of well-paid jobs in the new Federal Republic.

By the time the ban on recruiting foreign labor came into force in 1973, around 14 million people had come to West Germany as "guest workers." Around 3 million stayed permanently.

Yet Germany still did not consider itself an immigration country. "For a very long time, we were dealing with the mindset: It doesn't matter why people came to the Federal Republic; they don't belong, because in any case they'll leave the country again and disappear," said Oltmer.

In 2025, according to Germany's Federal Statistical Office, more than 30% of Germany's total population had a migrant background. Yet many third- and fourth-generation citizens still don't feel they belong, which has to do with the perspective of the society in which they live.

These days, Germany is keen to attract skilled workers, but refugees are only granted asylum if they are politically persecuted in their homeland, or are fleeing war.

As a migration researcher, Oltmer is constantly examining the reasons why some migrants are accepted and others are not. This question was especially relevant in 2015, when refugees came to Germany in very large numbers.

"The attitude was: 'OK, you people from Syria, in light of the Syrian civil war, you are entitled to protection.' But at the same time this society says of people from Afghanistan, 'In your case, we're a bit more sceptical,'" he said.

Yet asylum accounts for barely 10% of immigration to Germany, Oltmer explained. The majority of immigrants are not people fleeing their country. "This means that when it comes to scandalization and the creation of certain negative images, we are seeing a massive distortion of the debate." And the descendants of the German emigrants Benjamin Franklin once reviled as "Palatine boors" have long since perfectly integrated into American society.

In Germany, however, integration is taking much longer. "Many people have no possibility of regularizing their status and obtaining a stable residence permit, even though everyone involved knows they can stay, and will stay," said Oltmer. He believes people urgently need to sit down and clarify how they want German society to look in future.

## Data centers are everywhere. Here&apos;s what they&apos;re doing to the planet.

https://p.dw.com/p/5KaqfRows of servers hum inside Google's data center in Douglas County, Georgia — one of thousands of facilities worldwide powering the AI boomImage: Connie Zhou/ZUMA/picture allianceAdvertisementEvery question sent to AI models provides an instant reply that seems to appear out of nowhere, but answering queries requires physical infrastructure that consumes real energy, water and land.

Somewhere around the world — maybe in Virginia, Sao Paulo or Hong Kong — a massive warehouse filled with fiber-optic cables and seemingly endless rows of humming servers is working to provide the processing power for the more than 2.5 billion questions ChatGPT alone fields daily.

AI models, along with cloud storage, streaming, web hosting and social media, all have a very real physical footprint. If data centers were a country, their level of electricity use would make them the 11th largest electricity consumer in the world, according to researchers from the United Nations University.

Data centers are becoming some of the world's fastest-growing consumers of electricity. These warehouses of servers, routers and internet-connected devices run 24/7, while cooling systems operate continuously to keep the tech from overheating.

In Frankfurt, Germany, the grid is currently so saturated that no new connections will be available until at least the 2030s. There, data centers consume about 41% of the city's electricity. In Dublin, it's double that.

And data center electricity consumption is growing rapidly — it's expected to double by 2030 to about 945 terawatt-hours, around what Japan consumes annually, according to the International Energy Agency.

One 2024 study found that more than half of the United States' data centers are powered by fossil fuels, emitting in excess of 105 million tons of CO2. Offsetting that would take more than 31,000 wind turbines running continuously for a year.

The build-out is also keeping old coal plants alive. Utilities in the US have delayed the retirement of 15 coal-fired plants nationwide to meet surging demand, plants that together emitted nearly 65 million tons of greenhouse gases in 2023 alone.

And utilities are also constructing new gas-fired power plants, with more than 100 GW of additional facilities across the country already announced. That would lead to more carbon emissions than burning 111 million pounds (50.4 million kilograms) of coal.

Of course, data centers can also be powered by renewable energy. Pairing a facility with on-site renewables can ease pressure on the local power supply, and any surplus energy can even be sold back to the grid, making it more stable.

However, using green electricity for data centers means there is less available for electrifying transport or decarbonizing other emissions-heavy industries.

Many data centers consume millions of gallons of water to stay cool. Servers generate constant heat, and water is one of the most effective ways to absorb and carry that heat away through evaporation.

A single large facility can use up to 5 million gallons a day, roughly the same as a town of 10,000 to 50,000 people. About 80% of the water withdrawn for cooling simply evaporates; the rest becomes wastewater that can overwhelm local treatment systems never designed to handle that volume.

In northern Virginia, the almost 300 data centers clustered in one region of the US state supply about a third of the world's online traffic. There, in what is known as the world's data center capital, facilities collectively used nearly 2 billion gallons of water in 2023.

There are several more efficient ways to cool data centers. These include immersion cooling, where devices are bathed in a coolant, and closed-loop cooling systems which recycle water and can reduce freshwater use by up to 70%.

Cooling a data center doesn't get rid of the heat, it just moves it outside. That can exacerbate the urban heat island effect, which is when cities experience higher temperatures than rural areas.

Researchers in Phoenix, Arizona, found a single large data center can emit as much waste heat as tens or even hundreds of thousands of households, raising nearby land surface temperatures by up to 9 degrees Celsius (16 Fahrenheit) and air temperatures by up to 2 degrees Celsius. Those effects can be felt as far as half a kilometer away.

Since cities are already 0.5–4 degrees Celsius warmer than their surroundings, these higher temperatures can increase heat stress risks for nearby residents and push up air conditioning demand, requiring still yet more energy.

Not all of that heat goes to waste: a growing number of data centers are capturing and piping it into nearby homes and businesses. This process, called thermal energy networks, can reduce data center power demand by up to 30%.

First, coal and gas plants release toxic particulate matter, which can lead to respiratory disease, heart disease, asthma and more health conditions for nearby communities.

On-site diesel generators, used as backup power when the grid fails, also release harmful air pollutants. These generators pump out 200 to 600 times more nitrogen oxides than natural gas plants.

One estimate found that when backup generators for all of Virginia's data centers were emitting at just 10% of their permitted level, they could contribute to 14,000 cases of asthma symptoms, more than a dozen deaths and up to $300 million in public health costs a year.

Data center construction requires large plots of land to build on. Repurposing natural and agricultural areas into industrial zones can threaten biodiversity and disrupt local ecosystems.

In Hattersheim, Germany, for example, an 18-acre (7-hectare) parcel reserved for agriculture and groundwater protection — a local green space known for its rapeseed blossoms — was rezoned in 2024 to make way for a five-building data center campus.

The damage often extends beyond the site itself. Gas pipelines and power plants built to supply these facilities require their own land, fragmenting habitats and eroding soil along their routes.
