## Australia targets big tech with algorithm opt-out bill

https://p.dw.com/p/5M7bAPlatforms that fail to comply could face hefty fines.Image: Jeremy Piper/REUTERSAdvertisementThe Australian government announced planned new rules for social media platforms on Tuesday that would give users greater control over their social media algorithms.

Under the "My Feed, My Way" initiative, social media platforms would have to allow users to opt out of content recommended by an algorithm in their feeds.

Prime Minister Anthony Albanese unveiled draft legislation that would impose a "duty of care" on major social media platforms, including Facebook, TikTok, and Instagram.

"It's not about giving government control. It's about giving people control," Albanese said.

The bill is expected to be approved by the Australian Parliament and introduced later this year.

Companies that do not comply could be fined up to AU$100 million ($72 million, €62 million).

Anika Wells, Australia's Minister for Communications, said the measure would help ensure children's safety on social media. Australia became the first country to restrict social media access for under-16s in 2025.

Several countries, including Germany, Spain, France, Denmark, and New Zealand, followed Australia's lead and drafted plans to introduce similar age restrictions.

Some big tech companies have opposed such new regulations. They argue it could encourage young users to move to unregulated online spaces.

## PISA test reveals alarming drop in reading and math skills worldwide

https://p.dw.com/p/5M5pF760,000 students in 91 countries and economies worldwide took the testImage: Armin Weigel/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementThe results of the 2025 PISA study, published on September 8, show a dramatic drop in performance in reading and mathematics around the world.

The Program for International Student Assessment, better known as PISA, is a global study that measures how well 15-year-old students can apply their knowledge and skills in reading, mathematics and science to real-life situations. The test is designed to measure problem-solving skills rather than evaluate students on memorized school facts, which means they cannot revise in preparation for it.

First administered in 2000, it is considered the world's most influential measure of educational performance.

The study always includes in its ranking the average of the international group of 38 advanced, high-income countries that are part of the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development, the OECD.

Between 2015 and 2025, reading skills have seen the most significant decline, decreasing by 28 points in OECD countries, and by 16 points in the other participating countries and economies.

Scores in math have dropped by 22 points across the OECD on average, compared to a decline of 8 points in the rest of the world.

In science, the focus of the 2025 PISA assessment, a seven-point decline was measured in OECD countries, while the performances in other countries and economies remained broadly stable.

The latest PISA study reveals that around 29% of 15-year-olds in OECD countries are now estimated to be "low performers" across all three subjects.

In science, they struggle to interpret simple data or decipher a graph. In reading, they find it hard to find information in simple texts. In mathematics, they might be unable to understand, for example, the relationship between units and price — basic skills needed for grocery shopping.

The study points out that 20 score points are about equivalent to a year of learning. This implies that most 15-year-old students were performing at the level typically expected of 14-year-olds.

Among the OECD countries, the drops were most extreme in Iceland and Slovenia, with scores going down by more than 50 points.

Reading scores improved in five countries only: Qatar (up 11 points), Brunei Darussalam (18), Cambodia (26), the Philippines (27) and Zambia (50). Even though these education systems are still below the OECD average, they "deserve recognition for achieving measurable gains despite the broader global decline," notes the study.

In some countries, such as Iceland, Cambodia and Finland, for example, there is very little variation in performance among schools, showing less than 10% overall variation in student achievement.

But in 10 countries, including Germany, differences among schools account for at least 50% of the total variation in performance.

The study notes that these countries' systems presumably have schools reserved for higher-achieving students, leaving disproportionately large numbers of lower-achieving students enrolled in the other institutions.

For the authors of the PISA study presentation, this is a problem that needs to be taken seriously: "Where such academic segmentation compounds with social background, it can amplify inequalities, producing pockets of excellence while failing to ensure consistent learning outcomes across the education system as a whole."

The very first PISA study was held in the year 2000. As one of the 31 participating countries, Germany was shocked to discover that its 15-year-olds scored below the international average.

In the last PISA test, held in 2022 and published in 2023, the scores of German students had dropped to a new low.

Between 2022 and 2025, reading scores plummeted by 15 points and math scores by 11 points, while science scores have gone down by seven.

The top six rankings in reading skills are held by Asian countries. Germany barely remains above the OECD average, coming in 24th. The United States comes in 13th position, Poland ranks 14th and Turkey 18th.

In math, Germany is below the OECD average, coming in 26th, just a rank above the United States. All top seven positions are held by Asian countries, followed by Estonia in the 8th spot.

In science, Germany is only two score points above the OECD average, ranking 23rd. Turkey made important progress in this field, now ranking 19th, with an increase of 18 score points since the 2022 study.

The data itself cannot explain the decline in reading skills, but the authors of the study note that while school closures due to COVID-19 may be one factor, there is no direct relationship between how long schools closed during the pandemic and the students' performances in reading.

There was already a downward trend in many countries long before the pandemic struck, the study notes.

These long-term declines in skills "suggest deeper structural issues within education systems, with challenges at the school and system level," according to the study. These challenges include the impact of digital distractions on students and growing disinterest towards reading books.

AI has had an immediate impact on learning. The 2025 PISA study asked students about their use of AI in schoolwork; more than 40% said they used it to draft texts for writing assignments once or twice a week, or even every day. Students who reported using AI chatbots got lower scores in science, on average, than those who didn't use AI.

"Overall, the data suggests weaknesses in how education systems are adapting to social, technological and economic change," states the study's summary. "Looking ahead, the challenge for policymakers is not simply recovering lost ground, but to ensure schools meet the rapidly changing demands of the world. As PISA shows, it is not an easy task."

While waiting for policymakers to react, the study suggests that parents can contribute to children's potential by being supportive and curious about their studies and helping them develop positive attitudes towards school. "Even something as simple as asking children what they learned or did at school, about once a week or more, is associated with an average 26-point increase in science performance, after accounting for socio-economic status."

## Elversberg: Bundesliga newcomers from tiny town make waves

https://p.dw.com/p/5DtoJElversberg have got off to a dream Bundesliga start, with two wins from twoImage: Thilo Schmuelgen/REUTERSAdvertisementHow are Elversberg doing in the Bundesliga?
So far, remarkably well. The smallest town ever to be represented in the top flight got off to a dream start on the Bundesliga's opening weekend, racing in to a 3-0 lead against 2024 champions Bayer Leverkusen. In the end, they held off a late surge to claim a 3-2 victory.

A trip to Borussia Mönchegladbach on matchday two looked to have brought them back down to earth, with the hosts leading 3-1 with little over 30 minutes to play. But a sensational comeback was crowned by a Maurice Krattenmacher winner in injury time. The win moved Elversberg above champions Bayern Munich as one of only four sides to have won both their opening two matches, but Krattenmacher was trying to keep some perspective.

"We know every opponent is good in the Bundesliga, but Bayern is on another level entirely. We’ll have to play as a team, and then we’ll see how it goes," he said.

Elversberg is a small town northeast of Saarbrücken in the small state of Saarland, in southwestern Germany.

As part of Spiesen-Elversberg, the town has a population of around 13,000 but Elversberg alone has just 7,000 people living in it. There is no train station and just three bakeries.

In 2013, the club reached the third division alongside RB Leipzig, but they were promptly relegated. After two playoff defeats in 2016 and 2017, the club finally returned to the third division in 2022 and slowly began to gain a greater reputation.

The club's surge coincided with the appointment of Nils-Ole Book in 2017 and head coach Horst Steffen, a former Bundesliga player, a year later.

Book first joined as a scout before becoming sporting director, where he played a key role in recruitment. Steffen secured back-to-back promotions for the club as they reached the second division in 2023 as a newly-promoted side in the third division.

Germany and Newcastle striker Nick Woltemade played a key role in the club's promotion that year before moving on, and Bayern Munich's Paul Wanner enjoyed a successful loan spell at the club a year later.

In just their second season in the second division (2024-25), Elversberg were in the promotion playoff. But a 95th minute winner from Heidenheim broke their hearts, as they missed out on the top flight. Steffen left for Werder Bremen, star player Fisnik Asllani returned to Hoffenheim and five other key players departed. Not only did it look like the chance had gone, but the club looked more likely to battle against relegation the following season.

Aided by continuity at leading levels of the club, Elversberg found a way to turn a desperate situation into a remarkable one. What proved particularly helpful was having the father-son duo of Frank Holzer, chairman of the supervisory board, and Dominik, the president, leading the way and also calling the shots at main sponsor Ursapharm.

The appointment of head coach Vincent Wagner from Hoffenheim's reserve team was understated, but Wagner did not change much from Steffen's approach. Aided by former Bundesliga legend Mike Frantz, the assistant coach, and team manager Selina Wagner, who won a Champions League with Wolfsburg, the unit off the field appeared as united as the one forming on it.

The departure of star striker Younes Ebnoutalib to Frankfurt during the transfer window and sporting director Book to Borussia Dortmund in March didn't derail the club's promotion ambitions. After losing to relegation battlers Fortuna Düsseldorf in the penultimate game of the season, promotion hopes were wobbling once again. But a win on the final day was enough to keep their spot in second thanks to a superior goal difference. The Bundesliga had been reached.

"A friend of mine told me this week: 'If Elversberg get promoted, then it's like flying to the moon.' Well, today we made it to the moon," Wagner said afterwards.

At the Waldstadion an der Kaiserlinde, which has a capacity of 10,000 — more than the population of the town. Construction to reach Bundesliga requirements —15,000 capacity — is reportedly not expected to be finished until 2027, though Elversberg have already been granted an exemption to that rule.

The strength of this team appears to be the collective, and the continuity in leadership positions, rather than one or two individuals.

On the field though, Bambase Conte was signed on loan from Hoffenheim and the attacking midfielder was integral to the club's promotion push. Long-standing goalkeeper Nicolas Kristof enjoyed a strong campaign, as did winger Lukas Petkov who pitched in with 13 goals and seven assists. Striker David Mokwa was bought in January for just €1.5 million, but aided the team down the stretch last term before scoring in the club's first two Bundesliga games.

The squad's average age is 25.3, making it one of the younger squads in the second division. Having reportedly made €10 million from transfers and spent just €3 million on new arrivals, it's clear what this team's approach is. But this summer, it will be intriguing to see how the club approaches the transfer market as it looks to build a squad capable of staying in the Bundesliga.

## Female footballers offered egg freezing but doubts remain

https://p.dw.com/p/5M5IRAlexia Putellas was reportedly a key figure in urging the Spanish FA to pay for egg freezingImage: Action Foto Sport/NurPhoto/IMAGOAdvertisementBalancing motherhood and work is a challenge in any profession. But for footballers, whose peak sporting years coincide with their peak fertility, the choice can feel stark.

"For a woman whose sports career might last, say, 10 years at a high level, she starts thinking that getting pregnant really isn't an option," former Real Sociedad player, Larraitz Lucas, told DW. "When you are competing at a high level, all you are focusing on is the present."

Until the last few years, only about 2% of footballers got pregnant during their careers, with many leaving the sport to pursue motherhood. But with increasing professionalization, organizations are starting to wrestle with the question of how to deal with players who want to have children.

Binding maternity regulations were introduced to the sport only in 2021 and strengthened in 2024. But for those who wish to postpone their pregnancy while playing, the issue of freezing eggs as a fertility treatment has come to the fore.

US club, Racing Louisville, were the first club side to offer to pay for such treatments in 2021, while English club Chelsea have announced this month that egg freezing will be funded for any player that wants it.

But, perhaps more significantly, the Spanish football federation, RFEF, are expected to become the first national federation to announce a similar policy soon after reported lobbying from star player Alexia Putellas and others.

Such options weren't available to Spaniard Lucas in her playing career, which ended more than a decade ago. While she went through egg retrieval, in her case it was so her partner could have an embryo implanted to carry the baby.

"You keep competing as best you can but no matter how much you try to align it with a weekend break from matches it's tough. Really, before, during, and after the egg extraction, well... it wasn't ideal," said Lucas.

Though it depends somewhat on circumstances, egg freezing can take around three weeks. Women inject themselves every day before their eggs are harvested and frozen under sedation. To use the eggs at a later date, they must be thawed and injected with sperm to create embryos.

But, as athlete gynaecologist, Lamia Zafrani, told DW, egg freezing is far from straightforward. "Egg freezing is not an insurance policy that is guaranteed to pay out. It's always better if you freeze your eggs whilst you're young because you've got younger eggs to then use for future IVF.

"But actually there's no guarantee that when you defrost or thaw those eggs, turn them into embryos, that they'll actually be successful and result in a successful live birth. And I think it's these conversations that the athletes need to be made aware of because, at the end of the day, it's all about informed consent."

Zafrani added that the number of athletes in her London clinic asking about the procedure has risen sharply in recent times. "It is definitely something that athletes are very much asking for," she said.

"But there's always nuance to all of these conversations. While it's great because the federations are listening to the players, ethically, it always does bring up the question of whether by offering egg freezing and fertility preservation, the underlying message is that you're telling your players 'don't get pregnant whilst you're working for us.' And that can be problematic."

It's a concern that is shared by Alexandra Gómez Bruinewoud. The legal director of global players' union, FIFPRO, was a co-author of the maternity policies adopted by football's global governing body, FIFA. She said FIFPRO have been working on policies for assisted reproduction, including the freezing of eggs, but that ensuring players were educated about their choices was paramount.

"We don't want it [egg freezing] to be used as a tool to pressure players not to become pregnant while they are playing because they are and they should always be fully entitled to decide when and how to become pregnant," Bruinewoud told DW.

There are, she said, several other considerations with regards to fertility and football, including doping concerns.

"When players go through assistive reproduction in general and if they are going to freeze eggs, in some situations they have to take hormones. And these substances can be prohibited in the WADA (World Anti Doping Agency) code.

"So we want to be sure to prevent a player being sanctioned when they are actually only trying to become pregnant or prepare things to be able to become pregnant in the future," said Bruinewoud.

She added that with costs, recovery and procedures varying, mandating one blanket global rule would be near impossible, and should be guided by medical practitioners. It is also tough to apply any universal standard to a sport where professionalization and finance varies significantly between countries.

"In my view, the best solution for that is collective negotiation at national level,” Bruinewoud said. "These are very expensive procedures and most of the players worldwide will probably not have access to this because of the cost."

For all three women, the most important factors are choice, education and empowerment to get pregnant at a time that suits them. The hope is that, if managed well, egg freezing provides another option, rather than the default path.
