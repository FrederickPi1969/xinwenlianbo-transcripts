## UK rules out cyberattack after major flight disruptions

https://p.dw.com/p/5MIyAHundreds of thousands of passengers were affected by the air traffic disruptions on Tuesday Image: Jonathan Brady/PA Wire/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementMartin Rolfe, CEO of the UK's National Air Traffic Services (NATS), has ruled out a cyberattack after a major interruption Tuesday in operations at British airports.

"We will be doing a very, very thorough and full investigation," Rolfe told the BBC on Wednesday morning.

More than 1,000 flights were cancelled and delayed due to air traffic control problems that started Tuesday morning and lasted for several hours. NATS said a problem involved an issue with the flight processing system.

Although NATS said the problem was resolved, more than 150 flights were cancelled on Wednesday, and more delays were expected throughout the day.

London's Heathrow Airport on Wednesday said "some disruption may continue as airlines reposition aircraft and crew," and advised travelers to check flight status before heading to the airport.

"There are going to be dozens of cancellations in the morning of flights coming into Heathrow because, basically, the flights didn't go out tonight," travel journalist Simon Calder said to Sky News. "The consequences will be horrible," he added.

The UK's Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander said Wednesday that she had summoned Rolfe for a closer explanation of what caused the malfunction, amid concerns over the UK's air traffic network.

This is not the first time a major system failure has affected British airports. In August 2023, several flights were cancelled due to a "system meltdown," costing airlines $135 million. In 2025, problems with flight radars affected several airports in the UK, including  Heathrow Airport.

"I am seeking assurances that lessons will be learned and systems that support aviation are up to the ‌job," Alexander said on X.

Ryanair,  Europe's biggest airline, said at least 150,000 passengers using its services experienced delays of up to eight hours. Two hundred of its flights were cancelled.

Rolfe told Sky News he accepted "full responsibility" but did not say if he was considering stepping down.

NATS provides air control traffic services to 15 UK airports. It is a public-private partnership which is partially owned by airlines including British Airways and easyJet, pension funds and the government.

## The billion-euro trade driving Europe&apos;s eels to extinction

https://p.dw.com/p/5JTSLUnai Eizaguirre, an eel fisherman for 30 years, prepares gear with a colleague in the Basque CountryImage: Nicole Ris/DWAdvertisementIn Plentzia, a small waterfront town close to bustling Bilbao, eel fishing has long been part of daily life. Now fisherfolk across Spain's Basque Country can no longer catch the animals, a prized delicacy at the center of what authorities describe as one of the world's most lucrative wildlife trafficking trades.

The regional ban is meant to protect the European eel, a critically endangered species, which has seen population declines of around 95% since the 1980s. Yet local fishermen argue the regulations punish those who adhere to them, while criminal networks profit from a billion-euro trade in  illegally caught eels.

"So it's the Basque fishermen who will make the eel extinct when the rest are allowed to fish?" said  Unai Eizaguirre who's been fishing eels for 30 years and is now facing the prospect of income shorfalls. "It's ridiculous."

Conservationists support the ban as an emergency response in the face of multiple threats from pollution and climate change. But marine biologist Estibaliz Diaz says longer-term measures, like removing hydroelectric dams that block migration routes and kill eels with their turbines are also needed.

"I don't know if the eel has time to wait for these projects to show their effects," said Diaz, who works with local marine research institute AZTI.

Part of what makes European eels so vulnerable is their remarkable life cycle, which was, until relatively recently, a source of mystery. Scientists now know adult eels migrate thousands of kilometers from  European rivers to the Sargasso Sea in the North Atlantic, their only known spawning grounds. They breed and then die, leaving their larvae to drift to Europe. By the time they reach rivers, they've transformed into tiny, transparent "glass eels."

Today, only about 12% survive the journey from the Sargasso to the Gulf of Biscay and connected waterways, Diaz told DW. The young glass eels conservationists are trying to protect are the most valuable in the illegal trade, fetching up to €9,000 ($10,400) per kilogram.

"You can compare it with drug trafficking. Every year they seize tons of illegally caught eel in Europe," said Eizaguirre. "The big business lies in trafficking them to Asia," adding that the huge profits and comparatively small fines for smaller infractions by local fishers make the risk worth it. However, when criminal organizations are involved, penalties can be substantial with fines up to 6 times the value of illegally smuggled eel.

Demand in countries such as China, South Korea and Japan is driving prices. As is a biological peculiarity: So far, it's been exceedingly difficult to get eels to reproduce in captivity. This means fish farms — where the eels are raised before being sold to premium restaurants — need a steady stream of young from the wild. Sometimes, the same smuggled eels come back to Europe as fillets for the sushi market.

Conservationists had hoped pressure on the species would ease when the EU banned export of the species outside the bloc in 2010. Instead, organized crime groups have increasingly muscled in on the market, with the illegal trade now generating an estimated €3 billion in profits in peak years.

"Our estimated maximum was always 100 tons of glass eels smuggled annually. That's already around 300 million fish," said Florian Stein, head of policy and science at the German Angling Association. But information from his contacts in the illegal trade have led him to the conclusion that it was a "very conservative estimate."

International law enforcement agencies are also cracking down, with Europol's Operation Lake seizing 22 tons of glass eels and arresting 26 suspects between 2024 and 2025.

"There has been a lot of pressure from law enforcement throughout Europe," said Interpol's Sanchez Romero. "That's why the criminal groups always look for new routes."

Stein, who wrote his doctoral dissertation on international eel smuggling and advises the German government, said trafficking has increased since 2020, and illegal trade routes have become more complex.

"Ten years ago, Madrid airport was still one of the biggest smuggling hotspots," he added. This was due to its proximity to fishing grounds in northern Spain and Portugal.

Increased checks by Spanish authorities on flights to East Asia forced traffickers to adapt. Experts now say a new route running through North Africa via Morocco, Mauritania and Senegal has become increasingly important.

Geography is one reason the route is attractive. The Strait of Gibraltar is 14 kilometers at its narrowest point and a busy maritime junction. "We know that many drugs are also transported through this strait," Moroccan investigative journalist Yassir Al-Makhtoum told DW.

European eels also inhabit rivers and estuaries in Morocco. Though the country has banned the export of glass eels smaller than 12 centimeters (4.7 inches), it is building a legal domestic eel trading industry. Experts fear that's acting as a cover for trafficked animals coming in from Europe.

From Morocco, smugglers then move eels along numerous routes to Asia. The means are not always sophisticated. Glass eels can be transported in ordinary suitcases on domestic flights. They're placed in bags filled with a little water and insulated to keep their temperature stable. They can survive for up to 48 hours in those conditions. Once in Asia, they go to the fish farms.

Eels are an important indicator species for the health of ocean and riverine ecosystems because their long migrations across multiple habitats make them vulnerable to problems that could also harm other species. They also serve as important prey for herons, otters and larger fish.

"But the impact of its disappearance is difficult to predict," said marine biologist Diaz.

Under EU regulations, member states must undertake measures to allow 40% of adult eels to return to the Sargasso Sea to spawn. Diaz also wants to ensure at least 40% of glass eels make it back from the Sargasso Sea.

But European eels are not the only eel species under threat. A proposal to protect all freshwater eel species from illegal and unsustainable trade was rejected at the 2025 CITES wildlife conference. For Stein such protection is key.

"The proposal should be on the table again at the next conference. I just don't know how else we can get a handle on this problem," said Stein, adding more lobbying and coalition building is needed to advocate for a ban.

“The eel for us is more than just a fish,” said Unai Eizaguirre. “It's been part of our identity for centuries.” For fishermen like him, better protection for the eel represents a chance at a sustainable future.

## Trafficked into fraud: An African crisis situation in Cambodia

https://p.dw.com/p/5MGvXMany of the Africans trafficked to Cambodia are lured there with false promisesImage: DWAdvertisementSoutheast Asia has long emerged as a major hub of highly organized online fraud operations, targeting people all around the world.

According to the United Nations, these operations generate tens of billions of dollars annually, having long surpassed the illegal drug trade as the most profitable activity for criminal networks, as reported by the United States Institute of Peace.

However, those who fall prey to scams and other fraudulent activities aren't the only victims of these criminal rings; alone in Southeast Asia, the scam industry is powered by at least 300,000 trafficked workers from 66 countries. They are routinely subjected to torture, sexual abuse and forced labor, according to a recent UN Human Rights report.

African nationals now form the largest group of those trafficked to and stranded in countries like Myanmar and Cambodia, without any viable pathway to go back home, says the NGO Freedom Collaborative.

In Cambodia, one of the main obstacles to helping stranded Africans return home is that many countries on the continent, such as Uganda, Liberia, Ghana and Nigeria, lack consular representation.

"Some countries have zero interest," says Montse Ferrer, co-regional director of Amnesty International, who also blames the home countries for their inaction.

"They're not even picking up the phone, let alone providing the paperwork for the money for them to go back."

Ferrer believes that the Cambodian government also has an interest in keeping scam trafficking victims in the country. This is because many of the victims now have overstay fines for their visas against their names in Cambodia, reaching thousands of dollars in many instances.

"They don't allow NGOs to provide the support these people need, and instead they have resorted to arresting and threatening them," Ferrer told DW.

Following great pressure from the international community — including threats of financial sanctions by the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and the European Union — Cambodia did eventually agree to launch a crackdown on the scamming industry earlier this year.

According to the Cambodian Ministry of Information, the initiative resulted in many arrests and triggered a mass exodus of dubious operatives from scamming industry.

But the crackdown also created a secondary crisis: Thousands of trafficked foreign workers remain stranded in the country without documentation nor legal status; now, they also lack shelter and money in many instances, NGOs say.

Many who have sought in Cambodia help have reportedly been placed in immigration detention, while others have to sleep rough and beg for food.

Instead of being given protection, trafficking victimes often face stigmatization and abuse on the street of the capital Phnom Penh and elsewhere.

Emmanuel David is one of those stuck in Cambodia. The 28-year-old Liberian national arrived there in 2025 after a treacherous irregular journey originating in Vietnam.

"My friend told me there was work in Cambodia, but he did not explain that it was scamming work," David explained.

The scamming company had offered him a lucrative deal of making around $800 (€700) per month, which is more than double the average wage for qualified individuals in Liberia.

The reality hit quite differently: Once there, David was forced to work 14 hours a day to deceive women on various dating apps to part with their money.

But David was initially unable — and later unwilling — to meet the set target quota of scams per day.

In response, the criminal group he worked for started billing him for food and accommodation through his wages and later even confiscated his belongings, including his passport.

David says he also faced substantial physical violence, including beatings, for four months.

Eventually, he left "with no documents and no money," and is now hoping to find a way back to Liberia.

Another survivor, who asked to remain anonymous for fear of retaliation, also recounted how he was "beaten and [subjected to] electric shocks."

The abuse resulted in him having to be hospitalized for three weeks, and is now unable to pay his medical bills.

Pia Oberoi, chief of the Right to Development Section at UN Human Rights, told DW that "despite the promise of a good salary, most come out in debt."

She also confirmed that a growing number of trafficking victims in Southeast Asia come from African countries, "because there is clearly a recruitment industry that's built around Africa, targeting African nationals."

Other victims have corroborated this development, describing hundreds of Africans packed into heavily fortified compounds.

"Once you're inside there's no way out," an informant, who also chose to remain anonymous, told DW.

He said the compounds have their own laws, and that sexual abuse and psychological violence are an integral part of how they operate.

## The Bayeux Tapestry in London: What&apos;s behind the hype?

https://p.dw.com/p/5JKTdMasterpiece of the High Middle Ages: the Bayeux Tapestry (detail)Image: Bildagentur-online/picture allianceAdvertisementFirst, let's start with a clarification: Strictly speaking, it's not actually a tapestry, since tapestries are woven, whereas this artwork is an embroidery on linen. Nevertheless, it is commonly referred to as such.

This pictorial narrative which is approximately 70 meters (230 feet) long, depicts the events surrounding the Norman Conquest of England in 1066, culminating in the Battle of Hastings and William the Conqueror's victory over Harold II.

The Norman Conquest radically changed England: Politics, laws and culture all came under the influence of Norman rule. The Bayeux Tapestry bears witness to this event; at the same time, it is an outstanding example of English craftsmanship from the Middle Ages and is seen by many Britons as one of the most important symbols of British identity, alongside the works of Shakespeare or Stonehenge.

The tapestry is thought to have been commissioned by the Bishop of Bayeux, William's half-brother, who was present at the battle, from embroiderers in southern England for the opening of his cathedral in 1070. Anglo-Saxon embroiderers were famous throughout Europe at the time for their skills. The work then made its way to France, where it remained for almost a thousand years — until now.

The Bayeux Tapestry is considered one of the most important pictorial monuments of the High Middle Ages. Many researchers view it as an elaborately crafted piece of propaganda that showed and explained William the Conqueror's victory to people at the time. Its scientifically disputed message is that Harold Godwinson, then Earl of Wessex, had sworn a sacred oath on relics to support William's claim to the English throne. He broke this oath when he had himself crowned king. The war is therefore portrayed as his just punishment. The fact that Harold had probably not taken the oath of his own free will is irrelevant to this woven narrative.

The embroidery is part of the UNESCO Memory of the World Register and, because of its 58 pictorial scenes, is sometimes referred to as "the world's oldest comic."

Accompanied by Latin inscriptions, the pictorial narrative depicts the horrors of war — including massacres, rolling heads and the death of a king.

But it is also a valuable historical document for researchers. The tapestry illustrates shipbuilding and military equipment in surprising detail, as well as everyday medieval life. Even Halley's Comet is shown in it — the comet was visible in the sky in 1066 and was interpreted by the Anglo-Saxons as a bad omen in the pictorial narrative.

The tapestry is owned by the French state and had been placed on deposit with the City of Bayeux for display at the Musée de la Tapisserie de Bayeux.

Britain had already tried to borrow the tapestry for the coronation of Elizabeth II in 1953, as well as for various historical occasions afterwards, but France had always refused. The artefact was considered too fragile and the journey too risky. Finally, President Emmanuel Macron gave the green light last year for an exhibition at the British Museum — overriding the concerns of his experts.

Last September, the tapestry was taken down, packaged and initially placed in storage in a highly elaborate, six-hour procedure.

On July 9, it finally arrived in London in a secret transport operation, enclosed in a high-tech container that shielded it from all vibrations while maintaining a constant temperature and humidity level.

The British government has also insured the artwork for £800 million (around €932 million or $1 billion).

The British Museum has divided ticket sales into three phases. The first, covering September to December, began on July 1. All tickets sold out quickly, with up to 80,000 people reportedly trying to secure a slot at the same time. Many had to wait up to nine hours.

For those who missed out, there will soon be another chance — tickets for January to March will go on sale on October 21, and tickets for April to July in January.

The exhibition is being marketed as a "once-in-a-generation" event, and an extensive educational program is intended to involve schools across the country.

The British Museum is optimistic that the Bayeux Tapestry will help it set a new visitor record. The most popular exhibition in the museum's history to date is the 1972 "The Treasures of Tutankhamun," which attracted almost 1.7 million visitors.

If you're now eager to see the tapestry but unsure if you'll land a ticket, you can also head to the town of Reading, around two hours west of London. There, you can see a faithful replica of the tapestry, which is itself already 140 years old.

Even back then, there was a strong desire in England to have this artwork in the country. In 1885, 35 embroiderers set to work for around a year. Using colored photographs as a reference, they reproduced the tapestry on a one-to-one scale. However, they did change one detail: Some figures on the original border are naked and in suggestive poses. Apparently, this was considered too scandalous in Victorian England. One figure was retouched in the corresponding photograph — and the embroiderers copied the alteration. As a result, the figure is depicted wearing underwear.

While talk of reforming global governance will still be central, she thinks BRICS' best hope to focus on the institutions which it agrees most on. For example, India is reluctant to press for UN reform but is keen for a co-ordinated approach from BRICS to trade and the WTO.

"India's WTO ambitions are shared by more members," she says, which makes movement in that area "more likely."

He says issues ranging from China's growing global dominance to the Trump administration's stance on tariffs have made the bloc's limited goals even harder to achieve.

"With the kind of world that we are in and the lack of coherence and internal contradictions that BRICS has, it's very unlikely that it will achieve anything major," he says.
