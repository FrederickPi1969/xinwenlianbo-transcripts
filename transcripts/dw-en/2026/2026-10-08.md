## US prosecutors add new torture charge against Maduro

https://p.dw.com/p/5NwTjMaduro and Flores already face drug trafficking and firearms charges in the US [FILE: January 5, 2026]Image: Adam Gray/REUTERSAdvertisementUS prosecutors have announced new charges against ousted Venezuelan President Nicolas Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, accusing them of conspiring to torture political opponents and US citizens.

The new charges against the couple are in addition to the drug-trafficking charges they previously faced, according to a revised indictment released Thursday.

The revised indictment alleged that the couple committed human rights violations as part of a scheme to hold on to power.

High tensions between the US and a socialist and oil-rich Venezuela were a constant during Maduro's 13 years in power.

Human rights groups have long accused Venezuelan officials of torturing detainees, but Thursday's indictment marks the first time that Maduro could be held to account over the allegations.

"He authorized the use of torture against specific individuals and oversaw a system designed to use torture, arbitrary detention and violence as a means of repression against the political opposition," the indictment said.

Meanwhile, the 69-year-old Flores is due in Manhattan federal court on Thursday. She is expected to ask a judge to release her from jail and place her under house arrest, citing health problems.

Her lawyers say she has lost 25 pounds (11.4 kilograms) in custody, suffers from dizziness and shortness of breath and may have suffered a minor heart attack in July.

Prosecutors, however, oppose the request, arguing that Flores poses an "extreme risk of flight and danger to the public” if released.

Maduro, 63, and Flores are being held at a federal detention center in Brooklyn as they await their trial in June next year.

The couple was captured during an unprecedented middle-of-the-night raid on their Caracas home on January 3, which was hailed by US President Donald Trump as a "surgical law enforcement operation."

They were subsequently brought to New York, where they face existing drug trafficking and firearms charges.

Maduro has maintained his innocence, calling himself a prisoner of war and referring to his capture as a kidnapping.

Meanwhile, in August, Trump announced that the US had reached a deal to take control of part of Venezuela's vast oil reserves, which Venezuelan Interim President Delcy Rodriguez also termed "historic."

## EU pulls sponsorship from Disinfo2026 event in Lithuania

https://p.dw.com/p/5NzUlThe conference took place at the Radisson Blu Lietuva Hotel in Vilnius, but without several of its pre-planned backers [FILE: August 12, 2025]Image: Joko/picture allianceAdvertisementThe European Union on Thursday offered its reasons for withdrawing sponsorship for a disinformation event in Lithuania, following media reports claiming that it did so after pressure from the US government.

The EU's foreign affairs division, the European External Action Service (EEAS), and the governments of Canada and Lithuania all pulled their support from the event at short notice. Sponsors' logos on flyers and placards at the event were hastily covered over with paper strips.

They withdrew participation in Disinfo2026 after pressure from the US State Department, according to reports in several media outlets, including the British newspaper The Guardian.

EU spokesperson Christian Wigand made no mention of the US or external pressure on Thursday but also did not repudiate the reports.

"We were not comfortable with the framing of some of the discussions at the event," he told reporters in Brussels.

"The organizers chose to frame some of the discussions in way which does not align with the official positions held by the EU," he said.

He refused to discuss details of which discussions or what framing had perturbed the EEAS. He also would not comment on whether there had been talks with US officials on the matter.

While a number of the sessions planned on the event's website across two days at the Radisson Blu Lietuva Hotel in Vilnius appeared to have the potential to perturb more sensitive members of Donald Trump's administration, the opening two sessions on Wednesday morning stood out.

The opening of the conference was an interview with Kimberly Prost, a judge at the International Criminal Court (ICC), which has come under sustained and heavy fire from Trump and his administration during his second term.

This was followed by an hourlong session entitled "USA as a FIMI threat: acknowledge, adapt, push back," using the acronym for Foreign Information Manipulation and Interference. One of the planned speakers was a former CIA officer.

Strong US free speech safeguards in the First Amendment to its Constitution mean the US has maintained a comparatively hands-off approach to combating disinformation — both in the political and legal spheres. This has arguably intensified, however, under President Donald Trump's leadership, with critics alleging it's partly because of his own often uneasy relationship with the truth.

## How the Houthi conflict is affecting countries along Africa&apos;s Red Sea coast — and beyond

https://p.dw.com/p/5NwjQA Yemeni soldier pictured patrolling the Red Sea in AprilImage: Abdulnasser Alseddik/AP Photo/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementIn September, the conflict in the Middle East came within sight of Africa, as Iran-backed Houthi rebels captured Yemen's Perim Island.

The island forms the narrowest point of the Bab el-Mandeb Strait which connects the southern entrance of the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden. It is roughly 20 kilometers (12 miles) from Djibouti and 20-minutes by air to Ethiopia.

In early October, Yemen's Armed Forces and their Saudi allies launched a counteroffensive. TheIran-backed Houthis have been firing on merchant ships since the start of the Gaza war in 2023.

African economies are already suffering from crisis in the Strait of Hormuz, which is making fuel and fertilizer in particular scarce and more expensive.

If the Bab el-Mandeb Strait also becomes impassable, the situation could deteriorate. Shipping companies would have to make detours via the Suez Canal, controlled by Egypt or the southern tip of Africa, as some already do. The additional costs would be pass on to consumers.

The human cost of the escalation in Yemen hit Djibouti almost immediately. According to the UN refugee agency (UNHCR), at least 3,700 refugees have crossed from Yemen into Djibouti since September 10. Most of those who made it to the Obock region are women, children, and older people.

UNHCR is preparing to assist another 10,000 people who have not yet crossed into Djibouti. Obock, on the border with Eritrea, is extremely dry.

"We don't have much water here; there's no groundwater at all," Alessandra Roccasalvo, the UN Development Program (UNDP) representative for Djibouti, told DW. "Everything has to be desalinated."

Despite its size, Djibouti — a Muslim-majority Horn of Africa country of just over a million people — is a major player in the region. Its position at at the entrance to the Red Sea makes it an attractive commercial and military shipping hub.

The United States, China, France, Italy, and Japan operate military bases in Djibouti. The German Bundeswehr was also present until 2021 as part of a naval mission to combat maritime piracy.

The country's civilian ports serve as transshipment, or intermediate, hubs for global shipping cargo, goods, and containers. So far, the Houthi advance has not led to empty docks in Djibouti.

But there are still potential consequences, said Roccasalvo: "Basically, there's an economic upswing here — which means, as in any country experiencing an upswing, building materials are needed: cement, raw materials, and other things."

Less of that money comes in because ships now have to pay nearly twice as much for insurance and reinsurance. "Many ships no longer want to pass through the Suez Canal; many ships no longer want to pass through the Red Sea."

Ethiopia —  the world's most populous landlocked country — handles about 95% of its imports and exports through Djibouti's ports. Price hikes resulting from higher war-risk shipping insurance premiums affect the country greatly.

Shortly after the Houthis seized Perim, Ethiopia's economy faced another, even more immediate threat: On September 23, fighting broke out in the country's northern Tigray region.

Since then, the Ethiopian National Defense Forces (ENDF) have reportedly made gains against the Tigray-led rebel coalition that aims to overthrow the national government. The rebels briefly advanced toward the main corridor used for transporting goods between Djibouti's ports and the Ethiopian hinterland.

According to observers, the ENDF's advances have since reduced the threat of yet another trade blockade — but as long as the conflict remains unresolved, the situation could potentially deteriorate again.

For months now, Ethiopia has been suffering more than almost any other African country from the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz and has already had to ration fuel at times. In the long term, the country aims to reduce its dependence on imports, for example by powering vehicles with domestically generated electricity instead of imported gasoline.

Ethiopia and its northern neighbor, Eritrea, have severed diplomatic ties; other regional powers such as Sudan, Egypt, and Somalia may find themselves siding with Eritrea rather than with Ethiopia.

Eritrea sees itself as facing a threat that Ethiopia's Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed has reiterated on several occasions: With Ethiopia having been deprived of its own access to the sea since Eritrea's independence, Abiy has often alluded to rectifying this perceived injustice — either through a political agreement, as was once sought with Somalia's breakaway region of Somaliland, or by force.

The port of Assab is located on the edge of Eritrea's panhandle — and is often described as the most likely target should Abiy choose the path of aggression. If the fighting within Ethiopia further escalates into a full-blown regional war, this could also lead to additional restrictions on safe passage through the Bab el-Mandeb Strait and the surrounding waters.

Experts have also raised concerns about the Shiite Houthis increasingly collaborating with the Sunni terrorist group Al-Shabab, which is based in Somalia. Although their ideologies are fundamentally different, they are united by their hostility toward Israel and the US among other things.

The three African non-permanent members of the UN Security Council — the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Liberia, and Somalia — held an emergency meeting in mid-September on this issue, during which they jointly called for free shipping lanes and endorsed the demand that the crisis in Yemen be resolved under the auspices of the UN.

The situation at Bab el-Mandeb and in northern Ethiopia is once again demanding flexibility from Africa. In fact, some African economies can also benefit from relocation activities: Ports inKenya and Tanzania, for example, are becoming increasingly attractive for transshipment.

And while many countries are suffering from high oil prices, Nigeria's Dangote Group has announced an initial public offering and a new refinery in Kenya.

## Rodrigo Duterte ruled fit to stand trial by ICC judges

https://p.dw.com/p/5NzQqFormer Philippine President Rodrigo Duterte was ruled fit for trial Image: Piroschka van de Wouw/AFPAdvertisementJudges at the International Criminal Court (ICC) said Thursday that former Philippine President Rodrigo Duterte is competent to stand trial on charges of crimes against humanity.

Duterte walked into the courtroom for his first-ever in-person appearance at a hearing last month after a year of having been detained at The Hague, where the global court is located.

Judges at the court this April confirmed charges against him, accusing him of murder and attempted murder for his alleged role in the killings of dozens of people during his "war on drugs."

Duterte's trial is set to begin on November 30. He faces a maximum sentence of life imprisonment if convicted.

Duterte did not speak at a hearing last month but sipped water throughout the proceedings.

His legal team has argued that he suffers from cognitive decline that affects his memory and daily functioning.

ICC judges in January said his health was good enough for him to follow proceedings and requested his presence in court. He had missed several hearings or attended those via videolink.

Now, the ICC's ruling, dated September 28 and made public on Thursday, clears the road for his trial to get underway.

The court ruling said an independent panel of experts found Duterte "has the capacity to understand the charges" and follow the proceedings, including the implication of entering a plea — which he will be asked to do when the trial opens.

He also is mentally fit to understand evidence and discuss his defense with his lawyers, the panel of experts said.

Duterte was arrested last year on accusations of crimes that took place during his "war on drugs" campaign when he was president between 2016 and 2022 and before that, when he was mayor of Davao City.

The charges against him are tied to brutal killings that took place on his watch between November 2011 and March 2019.

Duterte's campaign to wipe out the use of illegal narcotics in the country ended with the deaths of thousands of people as police systematically targeted poor and defenseless people, according to Amnesty International.

Prosecutors alleged that police and hit squad members carried out dozens of murders at Duterte's behest starting in 2011, motivated by the promise of money or to avoid becoming targets themselves.

## 2026 Nobel Prize winner Anne Carson: Where to start reading

https://p.dw.com/p/5NzK5Anne Carson won the Nobel Prize for Literature on October 8, 2026Image: Hannah McKay/REUTERSAdvertisement"You remember too much,

This quote is from the long, lyrical poem "The Glass Essay" published in Anne Carson's 1995 collection "Glass, Irony and God" — an entry point into the works of the 2026 Nobel Prize for Literature laureate.

In the poem, the narrator explores the burden of memory and obsessive desire after a failed love affair, all while regularly reflecting on Emily Brontë — an author renowned for her intense literary depiction of destructive and passionate love in "Wuthering Heights."

The poem brings several of Carson's recurring obsessions together into one work. She has repeatedly written about desire, abandonment and jealousy, and has often used other literary figures as emotional doubles, as historical anchor points to her ideas.

In a way, "The Glass Essay" encapsulates Carson's method; the Canadian poet, essayist, translator and classicist has often intellectualized emotional themes by intertwining her personal reflections with analysis, etymology, translation, literary history and philosophical thought.

Another trademark of her work is the genre fluidity found in "The Glass Essay," which — as the title indicates — is partly a literary criticism essay, even though it is also a poem due to its form.

"Almost all her literary production has centered upon the intersection between the philologist's obsession with words and the poet's desire for new meaning," noted Anders Olsson, chair of the Nobel Committee, in his description of the Nobel laureate on October 8.

Born in the Canadian city of Toronto, Carson first developed her fascination for ancient Greek myths as a teenager. She told literary journal The Brick that she spotted a bilingual edition of "Sappho" by Willis Barnstone in the display of a bookstore of a shopping mall in Ontario, and that had led her to ask a high school Latin teacher to teach her ancient Greek.

She went on to publish her doctoral thesis in Classics, entitled "Odi et Amo Ergo Sum" (I hate and I love, therefore I am).

Carson's first book of criticism, "Eros the Bittersweet," was published in 1986. It lays the foundation of her approach, as she draws on ancient Greek writers such as Sappho and Plato to examine how desire — and the obstacles between the lover and the beloved — creates emotional tension.

The book suggests that the ache of wanting is not separate from love; it is one of the things that makes  desire so powerful.

Because "Eros the Bittersweet" moves between Greek poetry, philosophy and literary criticism and personal interpretations, it is not considered a conventionally easy read. A more immediately engaging work and good starting point to discover Carson is the verse novel "Autobiography of Red," published in 1998.

"Autobiography of Red"  was inspired by the ancient Greek myth of Heracles (or Hercules) and the red-winged monster Geryon, which the hero kills as his 10th task in his series of 12 monumental labors.

An otherwise minor figure from mythology, Geryon is turned into Heracles' lover in Carson's book. She reimagines the myth as a modern, queer coming-of-age story, with the monster experiencing love, jealousy, sexuality and heartbreak.

The work is accessible, funny and moving, and no deep knowledge of Greek mythology is required to enjoy it.

The key to reading Carson is not to expect every connection to be immediately clear. Her books reward curiosity rather than certainty, and her sprawling, non-linear web of knowledge can be compared to going down a hyperlinked rabbit hole of narrative ideas.

"Men in the Off Hours" (2000) is another intellectually playful collection of poems. Here Carson collides the modern with the ancient, with references to figures as diverse as Beethoven, Virginia Woolf, Emily Dickinson, Jean-Luc Godard, Sigmund Freud, Homer and Hokusai.

The poems bring together fragments of history, mythology and ordinary life, allowing unexpected emotional and intellectual connections to emerge.

In the spoken and sung performance piece "Norma Jeane Baker of Troy" (2019), Carson similarly brings together classical and pop culture, reimagining the story of Helen of Troy alongside the life and cultural image of Marilyn Monroe.

How do people become the victims of desires they cannot control? This is a core theme Carson explores in "The Beauty of the Husband: A Fictional Essay in 29 Tangos," published in 2001.

It features the Canadian author's wry humor in a powerful and moving way, with erotic, painful and heartbreaking scenes from a doomed marriage: a 30-year obsession with a pathological liar whose singular redeeming quality is his sheer physical radiance.

The collection of poetry, which won her the T.S. Eliot Prize, is dedicated to the English Romantic poet John Keats, challenging his famous aphorism, "Beauty is truth."

## India faces water and power shortages after weak monsoon

https://p.dw.com/p/5NzTYThe economic impact of a poor monsoon can extend well beyond agricultureImage: Arun Sankar/AFPAdvertisementRamesh Reddy, 44, put everything he had into his 3.5 acre tomato farm in June. The farmer from Kolar district in  the south of the state of Karnataka invested 320,000 rupees (€3,000, $3,357), borrowing 250,000 rupees at 24% annual interest and pledging his wife's wedding jewelry as collateral.

The harvest was meant to repay the loan and cover his son's overdue college fees. But then the farm's irrigation well ran dry. Within three weeks, the tomato plants had turned brown and Reddy could not salvage a single crate.

"I have lost everything I invested," he told DW. "I have no money left for the next crop and I don't know how I will repay the loan."

He is now negotiating a job as a security guard in the state capital, Bengaluru, and preparing to leave his farmland behind.

ForIndia, the monsoon is more than just a four-month rainy season. It replenishes reservoirs, supports agriculture and helps sustain water supplies and power generation across much of the country.

Nearly half of India's workforce depends on farming for their livelihood, doing jobs that are both tough and economically insecure.

This year, the country received 759.4 millimeters (ca. 30 inches) of rain between June and September, against a long-term average of 868.6 millimeters. The 12.6% deficit made it the country's driest monsoon in 11 years and the fourth driest this century.

Madhavan Nair Rajeevan, a climate scientist and former secretary in the Indian Ministry of Earth Sciences, said the main factor behind the poor monsoon was a severe El Nino. He had warned earlier that this global climate phenomenon would posed a serious risk to rainfall.

"As far as the Indian monsoon is concerned, El Nino is the most important influencing factor, which we should consider very seriously,” Rajeevan told DW.

The effects are now hitting India's water and energy systems. The country's major reservoirs ended the monsoon with less water than usual, leaving both farmers and the power sector with less of a buffer going into the months ahead.

Combined storage in the 178 reservoirs monitored by the Central Water Commission stood at 132.7 billion cubic meters, below the 10-year average of 151.1 billion cubic meters.

That shortfall is already being felt in electricity generation. Hydropower output fell nearly 16% in September from a year earlier, according to Grid India data. At the same time, electricity shortages reached 560 million kilowatt-hours, the highest monthly shortfall in more than three years. After sunset, when solar generation drops off, the grid has fewer options to make up the difference.

Coal-fired plants have, therefore, had to shoulder more of the load, but they face a problem of their own. Data from the Central Electricity Authority showed that around 40% of coal-fired plants had critically low stocks, with supplies below a quarter of the required level.

The government has ordered some private industrial power plants to operate at full capacity and feed surplus electricity into the grid.

Manufacturers are already feeling the strain. "Uninterrupted power supply is of paramount importance for manufacturing units, as power cuts disrupt production cycles," said Ashok Gupta, the president of the Mohali Industries Association, which represents nearly 600 small and medium-sized manufacturers.

"Such disruptions not only cause losses to manufacturers but also result in wastage of raw materials."

The economic impact of a poor monsoon can extend well beyond agriculture. Weaker farm output can hit rural incomes, employment and consumption, while higher food prices put further pressure on household budgets.

Arun Kumar, a retired professor of economics, said the effects could be wider than the headline economic numbers suggest, particularly because much of India's rural economy operates outside the formal sector.

"Economic development needs to be assessed through a wider set of indicators," Kumar told DW. He said weaker agricultural output could push up food prices while putting pressure on incomes and consumption.

Himanshu, a development economist at Delhi's Jawaharlal Nehru University, described the situation as a "twin shock" for rural households, with weaker farm incomes coming alongside higher food prices.

"When agricultural incomes fall and food prices rise, rural households face a squeeze on their purchasing power," he told DW.

The economist said that the uneven distribution of rainfall could compound the problem, with some farming regions suffering much heavier losses than the national rainfall figure indicates. "The impact on rural consumption could also be significant, as households facing lower incomes and higher food costs have less money to spend on other goods and services."

The pressure is also being felt in urban households. A survey in September by India's central bank found that households were expecting inflation to rise as food and fuel prices increasingly affected sentiment.

Sunita Sharma, a homemaker in New Delhi, said her usual shopping list now costs hundreds of rupees more than it did a few months ago.

"We have no option but to cut down on discretionary expenses just to afford basic, everyday meals," she told DW.

The prices of some staples have risen sharply recently. An analysis by ISignal using Department of Consumer Affairs data found that onions were 93% more expensive in September than a year earlier, while sugar prices rose by 28% and rice by about 8%.

The next test will be the rabi, or winter, crop. Farmers are now preparing to plant wheat, mustard and chickpeas, but success will depend largely on how much moisture remains in the soil after the weak monsoon.

Rajeevan said the longer-term outlook was also becoming increasingly difficult to predict as the climate warms.

"In a warming climate, longer dry spells and greater rainfall variability is a possibility," he said. "In future, in addition to the influence of El Nino events, we should expect the influence of a warmer climate."

Reddy, the farmer in Karnataka state, is now waiting to hear whether he has got the security job in Bengaluru, and preparing to leave his farm behind.

## World Cup 2034: Does Saudi Arabia shelving Neom Stadium spell trouble?

https://p.dw.com/p/5NxlENeom Stadium, one of the planned stadiums for the 2034 World Cup, is likely not being builtImage: Balkis Press/ABACAPRESS/IMAGOAdvertisementIt was billed as "the most unique stadium in the world," a key pillar of Saudi Arabia's World Cup 2034 plans. But now Neom Stadium, part of the kingdom's ambitious aims to build a 170-kilometer-long (111miles) city in the desert known as The Line, looks set to be scrapped, with the broader project scaled back significantly.

A recent Reuters report said the stadium, which according to the country's bid book was going to be "350 meters (1,148 feet) above ground" and have "stunning vistas, and a roof created from the city itself," would no longer be built for the tournament, where it was to host a quarterfinal and several other games.

"When I was in Saudi Arabia in December there were already plenty of signals that the whole Neom project was being put on the back burner, if not abandoned altogether," F. Gregory Gause III, a regional expert from the Middle East Institute think tank in Washington D.C., told DW.

"I see it as part of a general re-evaluation of their fiscal situation, both in light of the oil market before the current war and in light of the damage that the war is doing.  The draw-back from sports commitments is just part of a more general reassessment of priorities in the current, more straitened, situation."

Saudi Arabia's sports splurge that saw players like Cristiano Ronaldo join the country's football league and the kingdom invest in all sorts of sports in myriad ways has slowed dramatically. The country pulled funding for the multi-billion dollar LIV Golf as well as events in snooker, tennis and rugby and also withdrew from ambitious plans to host the 2029 Asian Winter Games in Neom.

Sports had formed a key part of Saudi Arabia's Project 2030, which aimed to diversify the country's economy away from oil and increase its soft power. But Yasir Al-Rumayyan, the governor of the Saudi-government operated Public Investment Fund (PIF), which also owns Premier League football club Newcastle United, said earlier this year that the PIF was "reassessing its priorities" amid war in the region.

"The impact of the war has not only made the economic rationalization more important, but it's also accelerated the desire to rethink some of these projects in the sense that Neom is now being repurposed as an industrial center on the Red Sea, which has obviously assumed much greater urgency since the Strait of Hormuz was effectively blocked,"  Kristian Coates Ulrichsen, author of "Kingdom of Football: Saudi Arabia and the Remaking of World Soccer," told DW.

There are, Coates Ulrichsen continued, clear parallels with the 2022 World Cup in Qatar, where plans to build 12 stadiums resulted in only eight being used at the tournament. The Arab Spring uprising arrived shortly after the bid was accepted by FIFA and, though it didn't impact the country directly, it destabilized the region both politically and economically.

Neither Saudi Arabia's World Cup organizing authority or Sports Ministry nor FIFA have responded to questions from DW about whether we may again see fewer stadiums than the planned 15 for the 2034 World Cup, which could feature 64 teams.

## Early-onset cancer rising worldwide: Causes, risk, prevention

https://p.dw.com/p/5Nyq9In Brazil, rates of bowel cancer in people under 50 increased by 7.6% per year from 2000 to 2023Image: Samunella/Depositphotos/IMAGOAdvertisementThe numbers are striking. Some cancer rates have doubled in the past 20 years. It's just not yet clear whether we're seeing a rise in actual cases or whether we're seeing more early-stage cases because of better awareness, diagnosis and treatment in hospitals.

Take, for example, colorectal (or bowel) cancer: In 2001, about one in every 26,000 people under 50 in England were diagnosed with it each year. By 2023, the rate of diagnoses was about one in 12,000. Twice what it was at the turn of the century.

That's according to the World Cancer Research Fund (WCRF), an international network of charities, which analyzed registry data from England, the US and the Netherlands. It found the number of new cancer cases had risen for seven cancers in England alone.

So, rates have doubled, but the numbers overall are still small, said Amy Berrington de Gonzalez, a professor of clinical epidemiology at the Institute of Cancer Research in the UK capital London.

"Cancer is still very uncommon among young people," Berrington de Gonzalez told DW. "Three times a small number is still a small number."

But she and other scientists still want to know why this is happening, because it is a worldwide trend. Latin America is a case in point.

A 2025 study analyzed registry data from 50 countries and territories up to 2017. It found early-onset colorectal cancer rising in 27 of those regions.

In Latin America, rates rose in Chile, Argentina, Ecuador, Costa Rica and Puerto Rico. Chile's rise — at almost 4% per year — was among the highest.

In Brazil, the evidence comes directly from hospitals. At the A.C. Camargo Cancer Center in Sao Paulo, the number of colorectal cancer patients under 50 grew by 7.6% per year between 2000 and 2023. That's a five-fold increase.

But since these figures came from a single hospital, they do not show whether cases are rising across Brazil. They may only reflect that this one hospital is treating more patients.

"We need better cancer registration systems," said Berrington de Gonzalez, "especially in countries like Brazil and Mexico, where obesity rates have also been increasing."

Marc Gunter, professor of cancer epidemiology at Imperial College London, said he expected Latin America to follow richer countries as diets there become more Westernized and lifestyles more sedentary. "I think it's already happening," he said.

In another study in 2025, involving 42 countries, Berrington de Gonzalez compared data from two age groups — 20 to 49 years and 50 years and older — between 2003 and 2017.

Six cancers — thyroid, breast, colorectal, kidney, uterine and leukemia — had risen among the younger groups in most countries. All but colorectal cancer were seen to rise in older adults, too.

In fact, colorectal cancer stood out. In about a third of the countries, it rose faster in younger adults than in older ones. "It is the one cancer that seems to be increasing in younger people whilst decreasing in older people," said Berrington de Gonzalez.

This has consequences for research. If cancers are rising in all age groups, the causes are likely to be widespread, so studies looking only at young people risk chasing complicated explanations with too little data to test them, she said.

A US study of pancreatic cancer in adults aged 15 to 39 years found the rise was almost entirely made up of early-stage tumors.

The authors said this suggested that instead of the number of cancer cases going up, it was the number of early diagnoses that had gone up. And that can be explained by better awareness in the community, with people getting checked more often.

But the data for colorectal cancer looks different: Some US studies show it rising in advanced stages of the disease as well, which suggests an increase in the number of cases, not just the number of diagnoses.

Obesity is an established cause of at least 13 cancers, according to the WHO's International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC).

According to a 2021 review of existing studies, young adults with obesity were almost twice as likely to develop colorectal cancer before the age of 50.

Obesity has also spread fastest among the young. The WHO reports that obesity rates have doubled among adults since the 1990s and quadrupled among adolescents.

Some also say ultra-processed foods and microplastics cause cancer. But Gunter said the evidence was inconclusive.

"There's a lot of hype," Gunter said, adding that more research was needed. Berrington de Gonzalez agreed: "Diets have changed, but we don't yet know the role they might play."

Gunter said individuals should not be blamed for living in societies built around convenience foods and desk jobs, and that lasting change needed public health policy.

But he also said that young people could lower their cancer risk by maintaining a healthy weight, moving more, eating more fiber and cutting back on processed meat.

"Going for a walk, being more physically active, eating more fruits and vegetables," he said. "Those things are maybe not so trendy, but they are really important."

For a 30-year-old reading this, Berrington de Gonzalez's message was one of reassurance: Cancer at a young age remains relatively rare.

## Middle Corridor: Can Bulgaria be a safe gateway for Asian imports to the EU?

https://p.dw.com/p/5NvZSFreight rail cars carrying containers are loaded onto a ferry at the Port of Kuryk in Kazakhstan for transportation across the Caspian Sea. The port is a key hub on the Middle CorridorImage: Jens Büttner/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementOn the morning of October 6, drones hit two commercial ships off Bulgaria's Black Sea coast, sinking one of the ships and causing alarm and outrage in both Bulgaria and the European Union.

The drone attacks come at a time when the EU is pushing for Bulgaria to become a key entry point for goods imported from Asia to the bloc via the trade route known as the Middle Corridor.

The Middle Corridor, which the EU also refers to as the Trans-Caspian Transport Corridor, connects Europe and Asia. It begins in China and runs through Kazakhstan, Azerbaijan, Georgia and Turkey before reaching the EU.

Although it is mostly made up of railways and roads, short sections of the corridor cross the Black and Caspian Seas.

The EU has this year declared its intention to bet big on this trade route, a move that could affect Bulgaria's railways, roads and ports.

Bulgaria's land border crossing with Turkey, Kapitan Andreevo, is already one of Europe's busiest, trucks frequently lining up at the checkpoint.

The country also has two strategic ports on the Black Sea — Varna and Burgas — and a new section of a railway line between Istanbul and the Bulgarian border is set to open this fall.

In September, EU Commissioner for Enlargement Marta Kos called Bulgaria "Europe's Connectivity Gateway" due to its strategic location on this route.

A few months earlier, Kos said the Middle Corridor could considerably cut transport times from Asia to Europe from 45 days to just 15 days and is safer than the two traditional routes for goods being transported to Europe from Asia.

One of these was an overland route via the Northern Corridor through Russia. Trade along this route dropped sharply after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022.

The other route, sometimes referred to as the Ocean Route, sees goods traveling by ship through the Red Seaand the Suez Canal. But fears of disruption caused by wars and instability in the Middle East mean that many goods are now being shipped all the way around Africa — a journey that takes around 45 days. That results in delays and costs for businesses and consumers alike.

All of this explains why the Middle Corridor suddenly seems so attractive — a fact confirmed by Vessela Tcherneva, deputy director of the European Council on Foreign Relations, who is based in Sofia, Bulgaria.

"The Middle Corridor is a project that basically avoids both war theaters that we have near Europe," Tcherneva told DW. "It avoids the route through Russia, and also it avoids the route through the Middle East."

As promising as it sounds, a lot needs to change before the Middle Corridor can really compete with the Ocean Route.

"You have, first and foremost, security limitations," said Tcherneva. "The security situation in Central Asia needs to really have improved enough so that one can make long-term plans for constructing big projects."

Tcherneva also stressed that weak institutions and bureaucracies in countries along the Middle Corridor are an issue. Russian involvement cannot be ruled out.

"We have seen infrastructure projects in the past not happen because they were not perceived as being in Russia's interest, and the Russians seem to have quite a bit of leverage in the countries along the route," she said, adding that "the outcome of Russia's war in Ukraine is going to have an impact on the success or failure of the Middle Corridor."

Tuesday's drone attacks on commercial ships in the Black Sea are a stark reminder that security on the route could well remain a problem.

In addition, the water level in the Caspian Sea, which is situated on the Middle Corridor, is falling, with climate change as a key driver. This means there is no guarantee that ships will be able to cross it in the future.

Then there is the problem of infrastructure: Ports and railways along the route lack capacity for big increases in trade volumes.

Despite these risks and limitations, the EU clearly wants to see more trade coming through the Middle Corridor, even if it cannot replace existing routes.

In her recent State of the Union speech, European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said that the EU wants to improve what it calls "connectivity." It wants to diversify trade routes, increase trade flows and reduce freight transit times.

"The Bulgarian railways have been underinvested in for many, many years now — for decades. Bulgarian ports would also benefit greatly from investment," Tcherneva told DW. "Developing better road connections from the Mediterranean up, but also east-west, would help, especially the north of the country, which is currently not very well connected. So, there are clear benefits."
