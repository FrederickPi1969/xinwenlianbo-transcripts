## What&apos;s going on with the polar vortex?

https://p.dw.com/p/5PQqvPrefer DW on GoogleCold snaps during winter can sometimes be linked to disruptions to the polar vortexImage: Angela Weiss/AFP/Getty ImagesAdvertisementHigh up in the atmosphere, there's a ring of cold air swirling over the Arctic that can intensify, weaken, and shapeshift through the seasons. This is the polar vortex.

If it gets disrupted, this twisting pool of chilly air can influence the weather we experience, increasing the risk of a cold snap in the Northern Hemisphere.

Current observations show the vortex has weakened unprecedentedly early this year, with its winds reversing directions.

It's not yet clear how the current disruption to the polar vortex will affect the Northern Hemisphere winter, but scientists agree it is a record-breaking event.

"As far as we know, this is the earliest stratospheric vortex disruption that has been observed," European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecast (ECMWF) principal scientists Tim Stockdale and Frederic Vitart told the AFP news agency.

The French meteorological agency Meteo-France, meanwhile, called it "an unprecedented event."

The early breakdown of the vortex "really increases the chance of cold air outbreaks across the latitudes where most of the densely populated population centers are across the Northern Hemisphere, US, Europe and East Asia," Judah Cohen, a climatologist at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology told AFP.

The polar vortex is an enormous band of cold, low-pressure air that spins counterclockwise around the North Pole, about 10 to 50 kilometers (around 6 to 30 miles) above the surface.

It's closely connected to the jet stream, another band of powerful winds just below the vortex. The jet stream forms a boundary where the cold Arctic air meets warmer air from the tropics and subtropics.

The vortex can sometimes be a tight circle, keeping the coldest air locked around the North Pole over winter. It can also form uneven waves that dip southwards. And as it changes in strength and shape, that can influence the atmosphere below and alter the position of the jet stream, which drives the weather on the ground.

So, if the jet stream is more "wavy," and plunges south, it may also allow freezing Arctic air to spill southward.

One of the biggest influences that can provoke this kind of disruption is known as "sudden stratospheric warming." This is when temperatures in the polar stratosphere rise dramatically within a few days, causing the vortex's strong westerly winds to weaken.

This can push the vortex off its spot at the North Pole, make it split apart into multiple rings, or — as is happening now — cause the westerly winds to change direction.

A previous polar vortex disruption contributed to the deadly Great Texas Freeze in February 2021, which caused more than a week of freezing temperatures that sparked power outages and killed hundreds of people.

But bone-chilling cold isn't a foregone conclusion. The Met Office, which is the UK's national weather and climate service, stresses that what's happening with the polar vortex can nudge odds in that direction, but it's not a switch that turns cold weather on and off.

For example, there's a possibility El Nino, which is associated with milder winters, could "moderate or modify" the impacts, Cohen told AFP. But he added this was not certain.

The El Nino weather phenomenon, caused by the warming of surface temperatures in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific, is currently intensifying and is expected to peak at the end of the year. The ongoing event has been classified as exceptionally strong and is disrupting weather around the globe.

Some research has also linked the rapid warming of the Arctic driven by climate change to increasing disruptions to the polar vortex. That means that, even as the planet warms, some areas may still experience more severe cold spells. But this connection is not conclusive and is still debated by scientists.

"There are rules: if a team does not take the ice, it receives a forfeit loss and sanctions are imposed," Tretiak added after a presidential sports council meeting in Moscow on Thursday.

"The IIHF as a way out — either allow us to participate or, if we cannot enter Canada, move the championship, don't hold it at all, or impose sanctions on Canada."

The threat comes after the Canadian government confirmed on Wednesday that it would not allow Russian players to play in the IIHF World Junior Championship starting on December 26 in Alberta.

"Canada's position is unequivocal: as long as Russia continues ⁠its war of aggression ​against Ukraine, there can be no return to business as usual," Canadian Secretary of State for Sport Adam van Koeverden said in a statement. "Canada will continue to stand with Ukraine."

Following last month's Court of Arbitration for Sport (CAS) ruling, in which it said that the security concerns the IIHF cited for barring Russian teams from taking part in international tournaments was not sufficient – the world governing body pledged to work to comply with the decision.

Norway then said it had been bumped from the World Juniors and had been informed by the IIHF that Russia would take its place. Several other national hockey associations said they had received a revised schedule for the tournament and stated that they would not play against Russia. The IIHF has not confirmed the change.

Germany has been watching developments related to the World Juniors and host nation Canada closely, as it is set to host the IIHF World Championship in Gelsenkirchen, Mannheim and Düsseldorf starting on May 14. With several nations saying they will not play Russia and major sponsors threatening to pull out, a German government spokesman described the prospect of hosting the tournament under these conditions as "effectively impossible."

"We believe that we should channel all our energy and efforts into helping sport—and ice hockey in particular—find a solution, which would, of course, entail excluding Russia from the World Championship."

What makes things even more complicated for German officials are concerns that taking a hardline on Russia could jeopardize Munich's chances as it bids to host the Summer Olympics in 2036, 2040 or 2044.

The International Olympic Committee (IOC) recently lifted its sanctions on Russian athletes and suspension of the Russian National Olympic Committee, opening the door for their return to competition despite the ongoing war in Ukraine.

The thinking goes that the IOC would expect national Olympic committees bidding to host an Olympic Games to follow its lead. The IOC is set to decide in the next few weeks whether Munich advances to the next stage of the process.

For now, though, the ball appears to be in the IIHF's court, after Ottawa confirmed its refusal to grant Russians visas to play in the World Juniors. How the dispute regarding that tournament gets resolved could go a long way to pointing the way forward for the men's tournament in Germany next May.

## Why does El Salvador&apos;s Bukele have troops in Haiti?

But earlier this week, Haiti's Prime Minister Alix Didier Fils-Aime hailed a major win in the battle against the violence, thanks to "the commitment of the Salvadorean forces in the fight against gangs."

El Salvador's President, Nayib Bukele had earlier said a joint task force between the countries had "destroyed" what he called "the house of Lanmo San Jou and the 400 Mawozo gang's headquarters." He also tweeted out an as-yet unverified video of helicopters firing on a sprawling property.

A number of Salvadoran troops have been stationed in Haiti, nearly 2,000 kilometers from their homeland, since the beginning of 2025, mostly in an attempt to curb gang influence.

Last month, Bukele dispatched more than 2,000 troops and special forces to Haiti, ostensibly to rescue four Salvadorans who were ambushed on October 18 and have not yet been found.

Writing on social media platform X, Fils-Aime thanked the Salvadorean military for helping "the fight against gangs." The two nations are, he added, working alongside the UN-backed Gang Suppression Force (GSF), in an attempt to "regain control of the territory and restore state authority."

The GSF has been operating in Haiti since 2016. But there are doubts to the legitimacy of El Salvador's presence.

"Where the lines are and where the coordination begins and ends between a bunch of distinct entities and security forces is a huge concern about how this actually plays out, and El Salvador are trying to frame it as their choice, their action," Jake Johnston, a Haiti expert and director of international research at the Center for Economic and Policy Research (CEPR) in Washington, told non-profit news agency The New Humanitarian this week.

A UN report about Haiti released on Thursday described "bloody turf wars, sexual violence and other horrors, noting that serious violations and abuses continue to be committed across the Port-au-Prince metropolitan area and in the Artibonite and South-East departments."

It further found that between July and September 2026, at least 1,167 people were killed and 459 injured, with more than half of that violence stemming from "ground or aerial operations carried out by security forces against gangs, sometimes with the support of a private military company using drones."

For many observers, this chaos is a major part of the appeal for Bukele, who appears to wish to export his mano dura (iron fist) policy on to foreign soil. The policy saw gang members jailed on a huge scale and a significant drop in the murder rate of a country once notorious for homicide. But Romain Le Cour Grandmaison, a Haiti expert from the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime, told DW earlier this month that Haiti's gang problem is more entrenched. He says using the same tactics as in El Salvador may not have the same result.

"Haiti does not currently have the conditions to replicate the Salvadoran model: Its prisons were emptied in 2024, its justice system is largely paralyzed and criminal groups resemble militias more than gangs, with territory, taxes, weapons of war and political networks that protect them," Le Cour Grandmaison said.

Despite El Salvador being on Donald Trump's list of "shithole countries" that the US president disparaged in 2018 in notorious remarks which he later partially denied, relations between the US and El Salvador have improved markedly during Trump's second term.

The number of migrants deported to El Salvador from the US rose by 98% at the start of 2026 and Le Cour Grandmaison said the US plays "a central, albeit very discreet, role in public life" in El Salvador.

Bukele's move to position himself as an ally of Trump has seen the US president offer him a level of endorsement and autonomy to do what Bukele deems necessary to protect El Salvador's security and that of its troops.

"He said that he has a big problem with Haiti. I said: 'Look, you're running your country. You have to do what you have to do,'" Trump said in November.

## Beijing faces its Russia dilemma amid talk of Xi-Trump-Putin summit

https://p.dw.com/p/5PQfzPrefer DW on GoogleAlthough no government has officially confirmed the meeting, some media outlets have already dubbed it 'the new Yalta'Image: Dmitri Lovetsky/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementAn unprecedented meeting between the leaders of China, Russia and the United States could be happening on the sidelines of Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in China's tech hub of Shenzhen next week.

China has yet to confirm that the meeting will take place. However, a Chinese Foreign Ministry official recently told the press that Beijing was "in communication" with relevant parties. In Moscow, a Kremlin representative said work on "coordination" was underway.

The clearest indication about the potential summit came from US President Donald Trump last weekend. Talking to reporters at a Florida airport, Trump said he "thinks" one of the meetings planned for APEC will be with Russian President Vladimir Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping.

Despite the lack of official confirmation from either Beijing, Moscow, or Washington, the media has already dubbed the potential trilateral summit a "new Yalta," invoking the optics of the US, UK, and Soviet Union convening at the end of World War II and deciding on the new geopolitical power balance in the Crimean resort town.

At the same time, nobody seems to be quite sure what the three leaders would talk about if China agrees to host the trilateral summit at APEC.

In his brief remarks to reporters on Sunday, Trump avoided mentioning Russia's war in Ukraine when asked about the possibility of the meeting, bringing up only "Iran" and "denuclearization" as possible talking points.

The Chinese Foreign Ministry reported "no information" when questioned earlier this week about the potential substance of talks.

A three-way meeting with a photo op would be a big diplomatic win for Russia's President Putin. Since launching the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, the Kremlin chief has faced years of diplomatic boycott by Kyiv's allies, most notably the US under Trump's predecessor Joe Biden.

Putin first pitched the idea of a trilateral summit last month during a call with Trump. The Wall Street Journal has reported that Putin wanted the meeting to discuss an end to his war in Ukraine.

For the US, the president standing shoulder to shoulder with the leaders of America's foremost rivals could be a bad look, although Trump has already made a show of personally greeting both leaders with a red carpet at different points during his second term.

As host, China is in an even trickier position. While Russia and China are broadly aligned on many issues, Beijing does not want to be explicitly tied to Russia's war in Ukraine and harm China's efforts to present itself as a long-term force for global stability.

The Chinese government is also working to rebalance economic ties with the EU. The European powers would likely bristle at a summit hosted by China and discussing the war in Ukraine without Kyiv or Brussels present at the table.

"China is clearly uneasy about the possible trilateral meeting with the US and Russia, and although Beijing has shifted its position from saying nothing to admitting that the three powers are in contact, the vagueness of these declarations seems deliberate," Alicja Bachulska, Asia policy fellow at the European Council on Foreign Relations (ECFR), told DW.

"Beijing is aware of the potential negative optics of the 'Yalta 2.0' framing, even if this historical comparison is inaccurate and does not reflect current geopolitical dynamics," Bachulska said.

She notes the trilateral meeting could "trigger further escalation in the already worsening relations between China and the EU" if Ukraine is discussed with no Europeans in the room.

For China, there is also little to gain in terms of optics. Images of Xi standing with Trump, the leader of Beijing's top rival,  and Putin, Beijing's partner in opposing the US, could present an awkward look.

"Even if the meeting only yields a photo opportunity, it could nevertheless severely erode China's image," Bachulska added.

The two neighboring countries have grown especially close following the declaration of "no limits" partnership accord in early 2022. The move was announced by Xi and Putin on the sidelines of Beijing Winter Olympics, just weeks before the full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

At the core of the axis between the two nations is a long-term strategy to contain US power while fostering an alternative global order both Moscow and Beijing can use for their own ends.

Throughout the course of the Ukraine war, which is nearing the end of its fifth year with no solution in sight, Beijing has quietly provided Russia with crucial market for energy exports, while supplying technology and so-called "dual-use" goods.

While China did not explicitly provide Russia with weapons, its exports included high-tech components that could be used for modern weapons systems like drones.

Officially, Beijing maintains neutrality on the Ukraine war and has refused to take responsibility for its outcome.

"China is more interested in reforming global governance in ways that reflect its preferences and red lines while preserving a collegial appearance," Aurelio Insisa, senior analyst at the Berlin-based Mercator Institute for China Studies (MERICS), told DW.

Even if China agrees to host the meeting, it will likely avoid framing it as three top powers making decisions for the rest of the world, according to Insisa.

"Embracing the 'G3' narrative would undermine its image as a champion of the Global South and developing countries. It is also worth noting that Beijing has shown no interest in engaging with the one pressing issue that could realistically be addressed in such a trilateral format: nuclear disarmament," he added.

In a report published in August, the US-China Economic and Security Review Commission (USCC), painted China as a "decisive enabler" of Russia's war in Ukraine.

Beijing was "converting Moscow's dependence into lasting military, technological, economic, and geopolitical advantages while avoiding the obligations of a formal alliance," according to the US body.

However, the commission also noted there were limits to Beijing's strategic support, and intelligence and security concerns remain a constraint on deeper cooperation. The report emphasizes the relationship is more of a strategic alignment than an alliance, with deep mistrust remaining in some key areas.

Russian defense firms, for instance, have accused Chinese companies of stealing intellectual property. Beijing, on the other hand, is  concerned with Russia's growing strategic relationship with North Korea, which has reduced Pyongyang's dependence on China.

## Cyber attacks and hybrid warfare: Germany gives sweeping powers to its police and intelligence services

https://p.dw.com/p/5PQR9Prefer DW on GoogleCyber crime can also affect supermarkets, whose cooling systems can be hacked as happened to Irish chain SuperValuImage: REUTERSAdvertisementGermany's intelligence agencies have been given wide-ranging new powers. The lawmakers in the Bundestag agreed to a reform package on Friday.

Under the new rules, the Federal Intelligence Service (BND), Germany's foreign intelligence service, will be allowed to respond to cyberattacks by foreign powers with counterattacks of its own. The range of possible measures is broad, from deleting and manipulating data to disabling entire systems and, if necessary, even destroying physical weapons such as drones that could be deployed against Germany.

Under certain conditions, the agencies will also be permitted to enter homes and business premises covertly, for example, to prepare surveillance operations. They will, however, not be allowed to use force against people. An independent oversight board is to be established to monitor the agencies.

Opposition lawmakers specializing in domestic policy warned against granting powers that are too broad.

The president of Germany's Federal Criminal Police Office (BKA), Holger Münch this week again pointed out that today's hybrid attacks pose a new challenge to the organizations safeguarding the country's security. At his organization's annual meeting in Berlin this week, he said the "hybrid attacks" are challenging the "separation principle," which means that the police conduct investigations while intelligence services gather information. The exchange of data between the two is permitted only under strict conditions.

Münch has long objected to this strict division of responsibilities. He has been calling for expanded powers for the police and closer cooperation with the domestic intelligence agency, the Office for the Protection of the Constitution, which is responsible for countering espionage and sabotage.

The separation principle results from lessons learned from the Nazi dictatorship, when the Secret State Police (Gestapo) operated largely outside judicial constraints and served as the regime's central instrument of terror, used to oppress and murder Jews, Sinti and Roma, homosexuals, communists, social democrats and many others. The Allied powers that defeated Nazi Germany in World War II then decided that the police and intelligence services should remain institutionally separate and be granted different powers.

Historian Sönke Neitzel of the University of Potsdam considers the separation principle understandable in its historical context. At the BKA's autumn conference, however, he described it as "nonsense" in light of today's threat landscape and said it was no longer necessary in a country that has been a constitutional democracy for decades.

The controversy has renewed calls for reforms that would prevent police surveillance capabilities from becoming instruments of political competition.

## Could lack of money sink TFFF rainforest fund before it even launches?

https://p.dw.com/p/5PA5hPrefer DW on GoogleIf Malaysia does a better job protecting its forests, it could receive millions of dollars in funding — but only if the TFFF collects the necessary startup fundsImage: Mohd Rasfan/AFPAdvertisementThe world's tropical forests play a vital role in slowing global heating and preserving biodiversity, storing climate-damaging carbon dioxide and providing a home for an extremely large number of species — when they're intact.

More than 140 countries committed to halting global deforestation by the end of the decade at the 2021 UN climate change conference in Glasgow, but five years later logging continues.

In 2024, over 8 million hectares (19 million acres) of forest were lost. And since 1990, nearly 490 million hectares of forest have been wiped out.

More recently, the trend has shifted in the opposite direction. Deforestation in the Amazon region of Brazil decreased by 41% from August 2025 through 2026 to reach its lowest rate since records began.

To ensure lasting protection, the Tropical Forest Forever Facility was launched in 2025 at the COP30 climate conference in Belem, Brazil.

Because rainforests are so important for the world, the idea behind the fund was to give money to the countries where they are located to help them preserve and protect these ecosystems.

So far, these countries have made more profit through deforestation — for example, by mining natural resources on rainforest land or farming the area.

Starting in 2027, the TFFF is expected to generate the funds needed for rainforest conservation, in part via contributions from various countries. By the end of this year, a total of $10 billion (€8.9 billion) in government capital is due to flow into the fund, mainly in the form of low-interest loans.

In addition, the TFFF is expected to issue bonds to private investors, such as banks and investment firms, to raise additional funds — initially totaling about $40 billion.

Going forward, the plan is for governments to contribute a total of $25 billion to the pot, and for private donors to contribute $100 billion. The fund will then invest this money in international financial markets with the aim of generating profits.

Private investors who purchase TFFF bonds can expect to earn about 5% interest per bond per year, said Felix Finkbeiner, the founder of the German environmental organization Plant-for-the-Planet, where he is responsible for forest conservation and advocacy.

"Private investors' capital is very well protected," Finkbeiner said, adding that any losses the TFFF might incur on the capital markets — for example, if the price of its bonds should fall — would be offset by government funds.

Countries would receive $4 per year for every hectare of forest that they preserve instead of chopping down. At least 20% of these funds must be allocated to Indigenous peoples and local communities.

According to calculations by the TFFF Watch website, an independent platform launched by Plant-for-the-Planet to track investments, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, for example, would receive about $333 million for its 2024 forest stock under this system.

Finkbeiner said the subsidies would be paid only for intact ecosystems and not for timber plantations, with forest areas and their condition mapped and assessed using satellite imagery. Though it can sometimes be difficult to identify plantations accurately on satellite photos, he said, the technology has improved significantly over the last decade.

That means any NGO or external party with access to the satellite data can check the condition of the region, providing further oversight. If forest areas are cleared or damaged, money is deducted from the total.

Another safeguard: If countries don't use the TFFF funds for rainforest conservation, their payments could be frozen for the next few years.

The TFFF is supposed to invest, whenever possible, in government bonds issued by emerging and developing countries. Above all, the idea is not to invest in projects that harm the climate — such as those linked to oil, gas, or coal investments.

The TFFF concept has received plenty of support from environmental organizations, in part because it gives countries in the Global South significantly more sovereignty than traditional forms of development financing.

"For example, if the government of Ghana wants to protect its rainforest today, it usually asks the World Bank for financial assistance — and then the World Bank in [Washington] DC decides whether Ghana is allowed to do so," Finkbeiner said.

The TFFF fund is also designed to be immune to donor countries' backing out in the event of a change in government, for example. Once a contribution is made, the money remains in the fund for 40 years before being paid back.

Continuing protection is also an incentive for countries receiving money from the fund. "When a new government comes into power that wants to cut back a little on climate protection, they will realize: We'll get more money if we protect the rainforest," said Finkbeiner.

For the rainforest fund to actually get off the ground, a total of $10 billion in seed capital must be raised by the end of 2026. So far, the confirmed pledges from countries, NGOs and foundations total only $7.9 billion.

Eight countries have expressed a willingness to contribute so far, including Brazil, Indonesia, France, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands and Luxembourg.

Norway, with a pledge of $3 billion, is the largest would-be donor. However, the government in Oslo is willing to honor its commitment only if the agreed-upon total amount is in place by the end of the year.

"This deadline isn't set in stone, but otherwise the Norwegian government will have real trouble getting it through parliament," Finkbeiner said.

Germany's commitment must also still be approved by the parliament. The German government has pledged about €1 billion to the TFFF starting in 2027, to be paid out over 10 years.

Other countries are also considering investing in the TFFF, including Canada, China, South Korea and the United Arab Emirates.

Philanthropic organizations have pledged $88 million to the fund so far, including $73 million announced this week during the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey. Finkbeiner remains upbeat.

"Promises like that always come at the last minute," he said. "I'm very optimistic that we'll secure more commitments at the COP — and in the course of December — and that we'll then reach that $10 billion."
