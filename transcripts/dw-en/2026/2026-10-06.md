## Munich up against at least six competitors for 2036 Olympics

https://p.dw.com/p/5NqvWMunich hosted the 1972 Summer Olympic GamesImage: Sven Hoppe/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementMunich's bid for the 2036 Summer Olympics will be up against at least six candidates from Europe, Asia, and Africa. On Tuesday, the International Olympic Committee (IOC) announced that, in addition to Germany, Hungary, Turkey, India, Qatar, South Korea, and South Africa all intend to bid for the Games. All are currently engaged in the "Continuous Dialogue" stage of the bidding process with the IOC.

However, there could be further applicants, as the deadline for interested parties to submit a bid is October 31. The candidates are to present their plans virtually to the Future Host Commission in November. In March 2027, the IOC is to deliberate, analyze, and narrow down the field of applicants to a group selected to enter into the Strategic Dialogue stage.

A subsequent Targeted Dialogue stage with one or more bidders is planned for 2028, with the final decision on the 2036 Olympic host expected at the IOC Session in the summer of 2029.

However, the IOC did not disclose exactly which cities, alongside Munich, are in the running – although Ahmedabad, Doha, Budapest, and Istanbul are seen as possible hopefuls.

Munich secured its bid late last month after beating the Cologne/Rhine-Ruhr region in a vote by delegates of the German Olympic Sports Confederation. The city last hosted the Games in 1972. Berlin had withdrawn its bid in the days leading up to the vote.

"Wherever the Games take place, one thing must never change: athletes have to be at the heart of everything we do," IOC President Kirsty Coventry said.

"As an athlete, you spend years working to make your Olympic dream come true. The experience you have when you finally reach the Olympic Games stays with you for the rest of your life," added the former swimmer who won gold in the 200-meter backstroke for Zimbabwe at the 2004 Athens and 2008 Beijing Summer Games.

"That is why, for every future host, delivering the best possible experience for the athletes must always be the starting point."

The IOC is to evaluate the bidders' proposals based on "technical factors." These include a master plan for competition venues and the athlete experience, the financing strategy, political and public support, the capacity to deliver the event, and a sustainability strategy. The German Olympic Sports Confederation believes Munich is well-positioned in all these areas.

The Munich bid is not focused solely on 2036 but was also designed with 2040 and 2044 in mind. If it misses out on 2036, Munich can expect to face a similarly large pool of competitors for the following two Summer Games. According to an IOC statement, there are National Olympic Committees "taking part in noncommittal talks with the IOC about hosting future Olympic Games beyond 2036."

## Germany is shaken by an espionage and treason scandal

https://p.dw.com/p/5NqtuFormer BND chief August Hanning has been arrested on espionage chargesImage: Michael Kappeler/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementIf the allegations are confirmed, it would be an espionage case of historic proportions for Germany: August Hanning, former head of the Federal Intelligence Service (BND), has been arrested, with the federal prosecutor accusing him of espionage, treason, and attempted treason.

August Hanning, now 80, headed the BND from 1998 to 2005. But even before then, he had enjoyed a meteoric rise within the security apparatus. After leaving the BND, he served as state secretary in the Federal Interior Ministry, then moved into the private sector and worked as a security consultant.

According to the federal prosecutor's office, his informant was his former chief-of-staff, named in the statement only as Manfred D.*, who went on to hold this position under Hanning's successors from March 2012 to March 2026. D. thus had access to a great deal of classified information.

Manfred D. is alleged to have passed on nearly 2,000 BND documents — some of them highly sensitive — to Hanning over the course of several years in exchange for money. Hanning is then said to have used the information in his private consultancy business.

The most serious allegation is that Hanning is said to have maintained close contact with an employee of a foreign intelligence service for years and to have leaked classified information to him. It is not known which country was involved, but reports say it is not Russia.

Investigators also mention an analysis that Hanning is said to have prepared for an employee of another foreign intelligence service. However, it is not certain whether the analysis ever reached that person.

The classified information also concerned the routine meetings of the heads of German security agencies at the chancellery. Topics included, among other things, the Iranian nuclear program and analyses of the Russian armed forces.

Since intelligence from foreign partner agencies is also said to have been shared in this context, this could also undermine their trust in the German BND and lead to difficulties in future intelligence cooperation with Germany.

"A good agent is one who doesn't draw attention to himself," Hanning was once quoted as saying. When he finally drew attention to himself, it was in an entirely different context.

In September 2025, the Hamburg state police searched Hanning's home and offices in connection with the famous case of the kidnapping involving Christina Block, heiress to the Steak-House restaurant chain. State prosecutors in Hamburg accused her of having ordered the kidnapping of her two youngest children in late 2023 due to a custody dispute.

A little over a year before the operation to bring the children back, there is said to have already been a failed kidnapping attempt. At the time, there were suspicions that Hanning was involved, which he denies.

But during a search in 2025, investigators found an electronic storage device containing top-secret BND documents. The police handed the documents over to the federal prosecutor's office, which launched a covert investigation against Hanning and Manfred D. in May 2026. The most recent documents found so far are said to date from June 2022.

Throughout its history, the BND has seen a number of espionage cases, some of them spectacular. In the 1960s, Heinz Felfe headed the "Counterintelligence: Soviet Union" division while simultaneously working for the Soviet secret service, the KGB.

In 2014, a BND employee was exposed as a spy for the US CIA. There is also an ongoing trial against a BND agent accused of betraying secrets to Russia's FSB in September and October 2022 — that is, after the start of the war in Ukraine. Yet never before has a former official of August Hanning's stature been at the center of suspicion of espionage.

"The BND launched extensive internal investigations early on and without delay, which corroborated the suspicions," said current BND President Martin Jäger. "For the BND and for me, there can be no compromises when it comes to internal security."

However, there was some fierce criticism from the political sphere. Günter Krings of the conservative governing party, the Christian Democratic Union (CDU), told the Rheinische Post newspaper: "The mere suspicion that a former BND chief is selling secrets is damaging to trust in our security agencies."

Konstantin von Notz of the environmentalist Green Party and deputy chair of the parliamentary committee that oversees the intelligence services said: "These are serious and grave allegations that have been leveled against August Hanning." Speaking on behalf of his party, he called for greater scrutiny of lobbying activities by former top officials and those with the highest security clearances. "These are clearly also a gateway for hostile intelligence services," von Notz said. The federal government must put an immediate stop to these "illegitimate practices."

Clara Bünger, domestic policy spokesperson for the socialist Left Party group in the Bundestag, went even further: "The investigations against former BND chief Hanning show once again: The intelligence services themselves are a security risk."

The new name Skydance hails from billionaire investor David Ellison, who first bought out Paramount and its subsidiaries and then moved on to target the much larger fish that is Warner Bros.

The bid faced stiff competition in a bidding war with Netflix, and later scrutiny from US regulators and politicians, but ultimately went through.

"Today is a historic day, not just for Skydance but for our entire industry," said Ellison, whose family has ties to US President Donald Trump.

As well as the two giant film studios, Paramount Pictures and Warner Bros. Pictures, the deal also brings together the CBS News and CNN news networks, and the HBO and CBS television brands. Ellison says he plans to merge the subscription streaming platforms Paramount+ and HBO Max.

Ellison will serve as chairman and CEO of the new company. Former Mattel boss Ynon Kreiz is operating as co-CEO in charge of day-to-day operations.

Skydance Corp. looks set to have a total revenue in the region of $70 billion, based on the performance to date of its component parts. It says it's targeting cost savings of around $6 billion within three years as it takes advantage of overlaps created by the merger.

Puigdemont fled to Belgium in 2017 after leading a failed attempt for the wealthy northeastern Catalonia region to break away from Spain.

He had been wanted by authorities ever since, accused of embezzling public funds to hold an illegal independence referendum when he was head of Catalonia's regional government.

Judge Pablo Llarena lifted the arrest warrant shortly after Spain's Constitutional Court ruled that a 2024 amnesty for Catalan separatists also applied to embezzlement cases.

Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez welcomed the decision, saying it would allow Catalonia to "look to the future."

"We should celebrate that this also helps Catalan society, as it has been demanding throughout this time," he told journalists in Madrid.

In 2024, in the face of strong opposition from Spain's conservative and right-wing parties, the Sanchez government passed an amnesty law. It intended to cover all offenses related to the secession attempt and ease tensions in the Catalonia region.

It also allowed Sanchez to secure the backing of Catalan separatist parties and stay in power after a 2023 election produced no clear majority.

But Puigdemont's Junts party withdrew its support last year, accusing Sanchez of failing to fulfill promises such as providing amnesty for its leader.

Around 400 separatists have benefited from the amnesty, but Puigdemont had been excluded because Spain's Supreme Court initially ruled the law did not apply to those accused of embezzlement. That changed after the Constitutional Court on Tuesday sided with one of Puigdemont's allies who had appealed against the top court's ruling.

Last week, Junts voted against housing relief measures proposed by Sanchez's government amid huge protests, prompting the prime minister on Monday to call a snap vote for November 29.

## As more Iranian women reject hijab rules, regime looks at new ways to force compliance

https://p.dw.com/p/5NqTqImage: Vahid Salemi/AP Photo/picture alliance AdvertisementWomen appearing in public without wearing a hijab has become an increasingly common sight across Iran, marking one of the most visible social changes since the 2022 "Woman, Life, Freedom" protests, which were sparked after a woman died in police custody after she was arrested for allegedly wearing her headscarf improperly.

Authorities have been unable to restore the level of street enforcement that existed before the protests. But the relative retreat of so-called "morality police" patrols does not mean the compulsory hijab has disappeared.

Now, authorities and conservative groups are experimenting with different ways to resist that change without necessarily recreating the same street confrontations that helped fuel the 2022 protests.

Judiciary chief Gholamhossein Mohseni Ejei recently called for action against what he described as social "abnormalities," "indecency" and "nudity," urging prosecutors to coordinate with police, intelligence bodies and government institutions.

At the same time, supporters of hijab mandates have circulated online campaigns encouraging people to avoid businesses run by women who do not follow the state dress code and instead patronize women who do.

The result is a more fragmented form of enforcement, moving in some cases from police patrols toward workplaces, businesses and access to everyday services.

The online campaigns call for what supporters describe as "conscious purchasing," encouraging consumers to support hijab-observing vendors.

A female lawyer in Tehran told DW that such campaigns risk deepening social divisions rather than encouraging adherence to a social norm.

"This is a deliberate attempt to categorize people by identity and put them against one another," she said under condition of anonymity for safety concerns.

She argues that encouraging economic pressure against people solely because of their appearance goes beyond an individual's right to choose where to shop.

"When economic sanctions are imposed on them [the regime], they call it pressure, injustice and economic warfare," she said. "But when they use the same logic domestically to force part of society to change its behavior, it becomes 'support for values.'"

She also questioned the assumption that women who conform to one particular dress code uniquely represent Iranian social values.

Accounts posted by Iranian women suggest that dress-code enforcement can also appear in everyday services, with some women reporting on social media being denied access to clinics and banks for not wearing a headscarf.

Shirin Shams is an exiled Iranian political activist and director of the Women's Revolution Organization, which advocates for secularism, women's liberation, and human rights in Iran.

She told DW the recent campaigns targeting women who ditch the compulsory hijab are an attempt to revive social divisions that were weakened during the "Woman, Life, Freedom" movement.

"The Islamic Republic has tried for years to divide women according to their clothing," the Germany-based activist said.

She describes the compulsory hijab as one of the Iranian state's most important ideological lines and argues that the widespread presence of women without headscarves shows how far social reality has moved beyond official expectations.

The renewed campaigns, in her view, are about more than clothing, representing an effort to reconstruct a boundary between women who comply and those who do not.

Saeed Paivandi, a professor at the University of Lorraine in France, says the conflict reflects a broader struggle over social control.

He told DW that Iranian authorities have spent years creating distinctions between women considered "properly" veiled and those officially labeled "badly veiled" or "unveiled."

"The government is once again turning women's bodies into a social, moral and even security issue," Peyvandi said.

A woman without a headscarf is not interpreted merely as someone making an individual choice, he said. For parts of the political establishment, she can also represent a visible challenge to an ideological order.

While Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian has repeatedly spoken about social cohesion and national unity, conservative judicial and political figures continue to demand tougher enforcement of dress regulations.

This produces an uneasy balance and exposes differences within Iran's political establishment.

Parts of the establishment appear aware that a return to large-scale confrontations between police and women could carry significant social and political costs. Others are unwilling to accept the normalization of women appearing in public without wearing a hijab.

The result is neither the old system of comprehensive street enforcement nor an end to compulsory dress rules.

Instead, pressure can appear intermittently through courts, workplaces, businesses, administrative procedures and social campaigns.

Paivandi believes direct coercion has reached its limits and that supporters of Islamic dress codes are looking for alternative ways to enforce conformity.

A bank employee, clinic receptionist, employer, shopkeeper or customer can become involved in enforcing standards even without a police officer being present.

## Southeast Asia&apos;s haze crisis: Have lessons been learned?

https://p.dw.com/p/5NqUKIndonesia's long 2026 dry season, coupled with this year's El Nino, has worsened what has become an almost annual crisisImage: Willy Kurniawan/REUTERSAdvertisementIndonesia's worst wildfire season in more than a decade is exposing how much, and how little, Southeast Asia's response to haze has changed since 2015.

Forest and land fires have burned across parts of the Indonesian islands of Sumatra, Borneo and Papua for weeks, sending smoke across large areas of the country and into neighboring Malaysia and Singapore.

Indonesia's long 2026 dry season, coupled with this year's El Nino, has worsened what has become an almost annual crisis, exposing millions of people to hazardous smoke containing harmful microscopic particles.

More than 170,000 cases of acute respiratory infection were recorded in haze-affected areas of Indonesia between August and mid-September, according to the country's Health Ministry, including more than 40,000 cases involving young children.

The haze has forced hundreds of schools to close in Malaysia and pushed more than 1.4 million Indonesian students into remote learning, although the full economic cost is not yet known.

This year's severe weather is only part of the explanation. Fire has long been used as a cheap way to clear land for agriculture in Southeast Asia, while the expansion of palm oil and pulpwood plantations has drained and degraded large areas of peatland.

The echoes of 2015 are hard to ignore. That year's fires burned roughly 2.6 million hectares of land, produced months of severe haze across Southeast Asia and cost Indonesia an estimated $16 billion (€14.2 billion) in economic losses, according to the World Bank.

The risk is growing that severe haze will become more frequent as climate conditions worsen, Paruedee Nguitragool, an associate professor at Chiang Mai University in Thailand, told DW.

"Haze, which used to be mainly a problem in maritime Southeast Asia, has become a concern for most countries in the region," she added.

Governments within the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), the regional bloc, "need to be more open to one another and work closely together."

"Farmers and companies need alternative methods of land clearing and incentives to adopt them, while land and forest rehabilitation must also be implemented," Nguitragool said.

"At the same time, consumers need to be more aware of whether the products they buy come from sustainable practices and whether burning was involved in their production," she added.

The 2015 disaster triggered major policy changes. Indonesia strengthened peatland restoration, fire monitoring, enforcement and firefighting, while ASEAN expanded regional cooperation, David Mark Taylor, a professor of tropical environmental change at the National University of Singapore, told DW.

Indonesia established the Peatland Restoration Agency in 2016 to restore millions of hectares of degraded peat. Its mandate was later expanded to mangrove rehabilitation, with the agency renamed the Peatland and Mangrove Restoration Agency.

A permanent moratorium on new concessions in primary forests and peatlands also restricted further development in some vulnerable areas.

Authorities have also increasingly pursued companies in court. Last year, an appeals court ordered palm oil company PT Banyu Kahuripan Indonesia to pay around €14 million in environmental damages over fires covering more than 3,300 hectares of its concession. Other companies have faced much tougher restoration orders.

At the regional level, ASEAN adopted a new Peatland Management Strategy and a second Haze-Free Roadmap for 2023-2030, and the long-awaited ASEAN Coordinating Center for Transboundary Haze Pollution Control opened its secretariat in Jakarta in April, more than two decades after the 2002 ASEAN Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution.

Extensive areas of peat remain drained or degraded, including cleared land that has yet to be put into productive use. Such poorly managed or "idle" peatlands can become highly combustible during prolonged drought, Taylor noted.

The Peatland and Mangrove Restoration Agency ceased operating at the end of 2024, with its responsibilities returned to other government ministries and institutions, raising questions among environmentalists about whether they can maintain the same institutional focus.

ASEAN faces its own limitations. Its 2002 haze agreement is legally binding, but many targets and implementation plans depend on national governments acting on them. The regional bloc has few mechanisms to compel governments to meet those targets or punish failure.

But the ultimate driver for all of this "is the global demand for cheap, plantation-grown commodities, such as palm oil and pulp and paper, which most of us are responsible for," said Taylor.

"So long as that demand persists, so too will the pressure to convert peatlands and forests to plantations," he added.

This year's fires have turned attention from creating new agreements to how Southeast Asian governments can make existing mechanisms work.

"To prevent this from worsening further, there needs to be a real pooling of capacity among ASEAN. Indonesia, for all its efforts, does not have the capacity to manage it alone," Helena Varkkey, an associate professor at Universiti Malaya, told DW.

The existing ASEAN mechanisms for joint emergency response should be operationalized, she added, but this should have been arranged before the start of the haze season, as meteorological reports made it clear that a "Super El Nino" was comingearlier this year.

"Southeast Asian governments need to work together to prevent biomass-burning episodes by implementing stringent laws to stop peat and forest fires. They need to use all available technologies together to prepare for dry conditions," Mohd Talib Latif, a professor at Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia, told DW.

## Tigray war may spill over to Eritrea — helped by Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed&apos;s rhetoric

https://p.dw.com/p/5NpxVPrime Minister Abiy Ahmed was on Monday sworn in for a new five-year mandateImage: Ethiopian PM OfficeAdvertisementAmid escalating conflict in Ethiopia's northernmost Tigray region, Abiy Ahmed has been sworn in for another term as Prime Minister of Ethiopia.

Shortly after the ceremony on Monday afternoon, Abiy reiterated his will to work toward giving the landlocked country direct access to the Red Sea.

"Based on the principle of give and take, we will work diligently to ensure that Ethiopia has an outlet to the sea," Abiy said, a remark that risks exacerbating tensions with neighboring Eritrea. The two countries have been at odds over Ethiopia's desire to control Eritrea's port of Assab.

DW correspondent Solomon Muchie, who followed the swearing-in ceremony in Addis Ababa, said despite the renewed mandate, Abiy faces challenging times ahead.

"The new term gives Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed stronger political authority," Solomon said. "But the country's complex internal and regional challenges could make it difficult to translate the authority into lasting stability."

Sunday's meeting in Cairo of countries that opposed Ethiopia was a case in point, Munchie said. "The leaders of Egypt, Eritrea, Somalia and Sudan discussed regional issues, but Ethiopia was excluded from this meeting, so this is also what Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's government will face in his tenure in the coming years."

For analyst Abdurahman Sayed, who leads the London-based Horn of Africa Center for Regional Integration, Eritrea now faces an imminent threat of entering the war in Tigray. This comes after a three-year Ethiopian campaign to gain control of the port of Assab.

"Eritrea has legitimate concern of Ethiopia's intentions to occupy [the country] if it can, and then if not, then at least take the port of Assab, which is a very important port, not only for commercial purposes but also strategically," Sayed said.

"It oversees the Bab el Mandeb [Strait], which is the southern entrance to the Red Sea. Now, without that port, Eritrea's strategic significance would be reduced to almost nil, so there wouldn't be any importance of Eritrea."

Last week, Ethiopia severed its diplomatic ties with Eritrea and Egypt. According to Sayed, this might be a sign that the government is determined to pursue its push for the Red Sea port by force.

The renewed fighting in Tigray might point in that direction. Just days ago, the Ethiopian National Defence Forces took control of Tigray's capital, Mekelle.

On Monday, a pro-government media outlet reported that the Ethiopian army had begun mobilizing its troops towards the Eritrean border, following a movement of the opposed Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF) retreating to the area near Adigrat, an important gateway to Eritrea and the Red Sea.

"The Tigrayan people have cultural, linguistic and religious links with those in southern Eritrea," Sayed said. "In fact, the ones in power in Eritrea have many more similarities with those in Tigray."

He added that there could be a spillover effect, especially now that the TPLF is out of Mekelle, and they might be out of major towns as well. "So they might go for a kind of ragtag fighting tactic."

According to Sayed, the Ethiopian government and army are only waiting for Eritrea to enter the war.

"They've already been making allegations without substantiating them, without any evidence, that the Eritrean army is already in Ethiopia fighting against the Ethiopian government. Although the truth is the Ethiopian rebel movements, they are quite well experienced in fighting, and they have plenty of armaments and so on," Sayed said.

"So there is not much need for the Eritrean army to be there physically to support them. But the Ethiopians would love to see that, so they can justify their attack on Eritrea by claiming that they were the first one to be attacked, and therefore they were now responding to that attack."

While allegiances in the Horn of Africa region and the Near East have been much debated in the context of the recent crisis, actors from the global North have remained relatively silent — though experts like Abdurahman Sayed have welcomed a statement by the United States for diplomatic efforts to be renewed.

One decisive factor, then, might revolve around access to Djibouti, the one Horn of Africa country with which Ethiopia has long been on good terms. At a recent webinar by the independent conflict monitor ACLED, the institution's senior East Africa analyst, Dr. Jalale Getachew Birru, elaborated on this.

"Ethiopia's main trade route is through Djibouti, and that is why we're seeing one of the fronts being within [Ethiopia's adjacent region] Afar. Just like in the previous war, the TPLF wants to control the main route from Djibouti to Addis Ababa," Jalale said.

She also said that, since Israel officially recognized Somalia's autonomous Somaliland region as an independent state, the past year has brought a new dynamic to diplomatic relations in the Horn.

"This also includes Djibouti sometimes aligning with Eritrea, Egypt and so on, but they're also trying to remain neutral," Jalale said.

At the moment, however, both Ethiopia and Djibouti seem to be determined to maintain the trade from which they both profit.

"There is also hyperinflation in Ethiopia," Abdurahman Sayed said. "And if there is a shortage of supplies and oil, that can cause major pressure on the Ethiopian government between yesterday and today."

Also, Djibouti, located strategically at the entrance to the Red Sea, hosts many international airbases, including those of the US, China, France, Italy, and Japan. Cutting off the country's trade route to mainland Africa could be the tipping point that finally prompts international players to speak up and pressure Ethiopia to end the conflict.

## Germany&apos;s far-right AfD makes history again in Saxony-Anhalt

https://p.dw.com/p/5NqWoAfD leader Ulrich Siegmund (r) congratulated Tobias Rausch (l) on his election as parliamentary speaker in Saxony-AnhaltImage: Jan Woitas/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementFirst thing in the morning, about 500 people gathered on Cathedral Square in the center of Magdeburg, the capital of the state of Saxony-Anhalt, to send a clear message that October 6 is not just another ordinary day. And this marks a turning point not only for Saxony-Anhalt but for all of Germany. For the first time since the end of World War II, a German state parliament has elected as its president a representative of a party that the Office for the Protection of the Constitution, the domestic intelligence agency, has classified as right-wing extremist.

## Nobel Literature Prize: How the winner is secretly chosen

https://p.dw.com/p/5NqGlThe building of Nobel Prize Museum in Stockholm is also the home of the Swedish Academy, which selects the recipient of the literature awardImage: Steffen Trumpf/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementOn Thursday, October 8, at around 1:00 p.m. CEST, all eyes in the literary world will be set on Stockholm.

That's when the new permanent secretary of the Swedish Academy, Ingrid Carlberg, will hold a press conference to announce the winner of the 2026 Nobel Prize in Literature, marking the end of a lengthy selection process that officially began a year earlier.

The Swedish Academy is the body responsible for awarding the prize. Its 18 members consist of writers, professors and intellectuals.

In September of the previous year, the Academy's Nobel Committee sends forms to hundreds of individuals and organizations eligible to submit nominations for the Nobel Prize in Literature.

According to the Nobel Prize website, these include professors of literature and linguistics at universities and colleges, previous Nobel laureates in literature and the heads of writers' associations that represent the literary community in their respective countries. Anyone from one of these groups who has not received a form may also submit a nomination.

"The list can reach 200 names per year of nominated authors," explains Gisele Sapiro, research director at France's National Centre for Scientific Research. She studies how the Nobel Prize, as a global institution, influences the international circulation of literature, power dynamics in the literary world and translation markets.

"The committee can also add names itself, such as authors who were nominated in previous years," she says.

In the spring, the Nobel Committee then selects five names through a multistage process and presents them to the Academy. Over the summer, Academy members read the nominees' works and discuss them. The Academy always announces the decision in early October.

As for the criteria the Academy uses to make its decision, it prefers to keep them to itself.

The public doesn't learn which nominees missed out until 50 years later — that's how long the Academy waits before releasing the list.

Sometimes decades pass between an author's first nomination and actually receiving the prize. Danish writer Johannes V. Jensen, who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1944, waited 40 years. Doris Lessing received the award in 2007 after 35 years, while Thomas Mann, who won in 1929, had to wait 27 years.

In his will, Alfred Nobel, the prize's founder, stipulated that it should be awarded to the person who had produced "the most outstanding work in an idealistic direction." That leaves plenty of room for interpretation.

According to Gisele Sapiro, the Academy aims to honor ambitious, influential literature that has, for example, given rise to a new literary movement. "At the same time, the work has to be universal, being able to transcend its own culture," she says.

Translations can be one indication of this. "I noted in my research, however, that all the laureates since 1990 had been translated into English, French and German — except for Abdulrazak Gurnah [the Tanzanian-British 2021 Nobel laureate], who had not been translated into German before winning — meaning that I think these three languages are the most important," Sapiro explains.

Sapiro says it is also a major advantage if nominees have already won other major literary awards, such as the International Man Booker Prize, the Cervantes Prize or the Büchner Prize.

On several occasions, the prize has gone to writers who openly opposed the regimes in their home countries, such as Belarusian writer Svetlana Alexievich in 2015. She is an outspoken critic of authoritarian President Alexander Lukashenko.

Genre, on the other hand, does not matter. In the past, laureates have been honored not only for novels but also for poetry and plays. In 2016, singer-songwriter Bob Dylan received the prize for his song lyrics.

So far, only 33 of the 122 laureates have come from outside Europe. There is also a clear gender imbalance: only in the past eight years have men and women alternated as winners, significantly increasing women's share of the total number of laureates to 14.9%.

In August, a drone rigged with explosives was discovered there near a Ukrainian cargo plane. Selen, head of the Office for the Protection of the Constitution, Germany's domestic intelligence agency, again pointed to clear evidence of Russian involvement.

Germany's domestic intelligence agency is particularly concerned about the increasing use of so-called low-level agents, or people who are recruited locally via digital channels, for example, for espionage, transport services or acts of sabotage. "Social media plays a central role here," Selen stressed.

The intelligence chiefs also said Russian services were collaborating with organized crime groups. An increase in this area is a development that security agency heads say they will need to prepare for more thoroughly in the future.

According to the BND, Russia is gradually pushing boundaries through targeted actions. Examples cited included drone overflights of NATO territory and airspace violations in Moldova. "Russia is very deliberately shifting the bar here toward a kind of new normal," said Jäger.

Jäger said covert operations against individual NATO countries, particularly in the Baltic states, were conceivable, recalling events in Crimea in 2014, when Russian troops appeared as covert units without any insignia. Now the agency heads suggest similar operations could be conducted to test the alliance's ability to respond.

In addition to Russia, China continues to be a concern for security agencies. The Federal Office for the Protection of the Constitution once again warned against attempts to gain access to German know-how through research collaborations and universities. Particular focus is on artificial intelligence, quantum technology, hypersonic technology and biotechnology.

The authorities are also watching the spread of Chinese technology products with concern. Data leaks from connected vehicles or household appliances could be misused. In extreme cases, it is even conceivable that systems could be shut down remotely.

The topic of extremism also took up a significant portion of the discussion with the parliamentary committee. Regarding right-wing extremism, Selen spoke of an "extremely alarming trend" toward groups of young actors who are prone to violence and are increasingly forming international networks.

MAD President Martina Rosenberg reported an increase in suspected cases of extremism within the Bundeswehr. The increasing radicalization of society is not sparing the armed forces either.

Against the backdrop of growing threats, the heads of the BND and BfV defended a planned expansion of intelligence powers. The plan is to significantly expand agencies' powers regarding the collection, storage, and automated analysis of data. In addition, the agencies are to be given the ability for the first time, to actively combat threats such as acts of sabotage or hacker attacks through counterattacks.

"Amid geopolitical instability, conflict-prone systemic rivalry and hybrid aggression, there is a greater need than ever for tailored capabilities and robust security systems," Selen said.

Jäger said: "If we have these powers, then we will use them. And our adversaries will feel the consequences."

Critics in Germany, however, warn against blurring the line between intelligence gathering and police intervention. In light of these expanding powers, Konstantin von Notz, a member of the Parliamentary Control Panel with the opposition environmentalist Greens, is also calling for greater oversight of the intelligence services. He expects the Federal Constitutional Court to address the plan to expand the powers of the intelligence services.
