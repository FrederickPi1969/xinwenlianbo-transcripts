## China&apos;s 13-year-old swimming star raises questions on child athletes

https://p.dw.com/p/5N83LYu Zidi has won three golds and set three Games records at the Asian GamesImage: Wu Zhizun/Xinhua/IMAGOAdvertisementThe third Asian Games gold medal looked as convincing as the first two, even if Yu Zidi insisted that: "It didn't feel easy at all. It was quite challenging."

On Wednesday, the 13-year-old Chinese swimmer broke the Games record for the third time to add the women's 400 meter individual medley title to her golds in the 200 meter medley and 200 meter butterfly. The three medals add to a bronze she won last year at the World Championships.

Even two years out from the Los Angeles Olympics, talk is turning to a potential rivalry with Canada's Summer McIntosh, 20, who herself finished fourth in the 2020 Olympics at 14 and won a world title at 15. As well as their remarkable talent, the pair's youth has raised some questions.

"The first reaction you have when you see something like this is you think, wow, there's a big career ahead," three-time open water swimming world champion Leonie Beck, of Germany, told DW.

"But I think maybe this is the problem. People around [the athlete], or maybe the media can create this pressure on a young talent. But just because you're really good and fast at this age, doesn't mean that your whole career will continue like this."

Beck has already retired from elite competition at 29 and swimming is a sport where athletes often peak early. Katie Ledecky, of the US, and Japan's Kyoko Iwasaki both won Olympic gold at 15 and Japan's Yumeki Kojima, 17, has been a home favorite at the Asian Games, winning a gold and silver in the pool.

While the temptation is to think of Yu as a prodigy, Beck said that to reach the level she has also requires extraordinary dedication and sacrifice.

"My first international competition was in 2011 [at age 14]. At that stage already, I was training eight times a week. You wake up at 5 a.m., by 6 you're in the pool, out at 7:30 so you're in school at 8:00, school until 1, going home, eating, maybe doing some homework, going back in the water training at 3. Then one hour of dry land [training], then in the water again and coming back home at 7 (p.m.). I had this when I was young too."

Yu, who was discovered by chance by a swimming coach at a water park aged 6, admitted in an interview with state media Xinhua at an event in China in January that the workload takes its toll.

"Being in class all day and still having to train was really tiring," she said. "Sometimes I was so tired I could fall asleep even while eating."

While, for Beck, the sporting success made such struggles worthwhile, not all athletes who dedicate their childhoods to their sport reach the pinnacle. The German swimmer added that putting educational, physical and mental support structures in place is critical for sports that allow under-18s to compete.

"Sport psychology should start at a really young age because it should be normal, the same as physiotherapists," she said. "Especially at a young age, where sport should be also fun."

World Aquatics rules state that the minimum age for international championships is 14 but exceptions can be made for those fast enough, like Yu. While swimmers peak earlier than athletes in other sports, teenagers at the top level are not so unusual.

In football, Lamine Yamal won the Euros with Spain at 16, Vaibhav Sooryavanshi, of India, made his debut in cricket's Indian Premier League at 13 and Nadia Comaneci, of Romania, became the first gymnast to be awarded a perfect 10 in the 1976 Olympics in Montreal aged 14.

Neither are young athletes a modern phenomenon. Dimitrios Loundras remains the youngest ever Olympic medallist, winning a bronze with the Greek gymnastics team in 1896.

While support structures in sport are improving, challenges remain, according to Rosemary Purcell, who played a part in the International Olympic Committee's (IOC) review into youth development ahead of the Paris Olympics in 2024.

"There are pretty stark differences between what we see in the community and in these elite youth athletes," Purcell told DW during those Games.

"Some sports do have a higher rate of eating disorders and body image disturbance in young athletes, but conversely, they may have lower rates of depression and anxiety than we would see in the community."

In general, sporting bodies tend to take the approach that if an athlete is good enough, they are old enough. Beck said, allied with the right support, this approach is correct.

"Every athlete has completely different development. Why should you take away the success of a young athlete just because she's young and successful in that age? In my opinion, you should give her the possibility to be successful at that age because maybe, who knows, in a later stage of her career, she will be not that successful. It's not just a solution to say: 'look, you're that age, you're not able to compete.'"

But she also cautioned that because success won't always last, young elite sportspeople should be helped to find a plan away from the pitch, pool or mat, something she struggled somewhat with post Paris 2024.

"You have this idea that, ok, I want to reach the Olympic Games, win this medal. So you have a structure of where to go, what to do. And at one point it's gone and you're like, ‘ok, but now what am I doing with my life? What do I want to reach? What are my goals?'

"It's really, really important that people are starting to think that, 'hey, we are also human beings and you should do something to have a life after the sport because the sport is not endless.'"

"When we last spoke to him on the phone back in March, he said that he had lost 85% of the vision in his right eye and that despite his complaints, it was neglected for months," they said.

Khan's sons are urging authorities to allow their father to receive an independent medical assessment and restore regular access to his family and medical team.

"We are asking the Pakistani authorities to obey the Supreme Court orders immediately and shift our father to Shifa International Hospital so that he can be assessed by independent doctors," they said.

They also called for Khan to be allowed his mandated family visits, phone calls with his sons, and access to a personal physician.

Despite being in jail, Khan remains at the center of Pakistan's political confrontation between the PTI and the country's powerful military. He also remains a popular political figure in Pakistan, despite being ousted from office in 2022 in a parliamentary no-confidence vote.

Amid plans for a PTI protest march set for this weekend, Khan's three sisters were detained in the Punjab capital of Lahore on Monday.

The detention orders cited concerns that the sisters were a threat to public safety and maintenance of public order. All three were ordered to be held for 30 days under a public order ordinance.

Khan's sons told DW the arrests were intended to intimidate their family and supporters before the planned protest rally.

Pakistan's government has rejected the PTI's characterization of the crackdown and says its priority is to maintain public order during the planned protest.

Talal Chaudhary, Pakistan's minister of state for interior, has warned that the government will not tolerate unlawful activity or actions threatening lives and property.

"If you are a peaceful citizen, come to Islamabad from any route, come 100 times," Chaudhary told DW.

In 2024, pro-Khan protests on the streets of Islamabad turned violent. Demonstrators breached high security zones and clashes with security forces, leaving six dead. When Khan was first arrested in May 2023, nationwide PTI support rallies also led to violence, with military and government buildings suffering damage. Several people were killed and hundreds injured.

Chaudhary said the government would not tolerate unlawful activity or actions threatening lives and property.

Authorities have stepped up security preparations in Islamabad and Rawalpindi ahead of the protest, including the deployment of thousands of police personnel and the placement of shipping containers and other barriers at key entry points.

## Slovakia: PM Robert Fico&apos;s NATO Article 5 remarks spark criticism and raise questions about his country&apos;s NATO commitment

https://p.dw.com/p/5N6CI'I do not want Slovakia to be part of any military conflict because of some Article 5,' said Slovak PM Robert FicoImage: Martin Baumann/TASR/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisement"I'm afraid of war," Robert Fico told medical students at a teaching hospital in Presov last week.

"What is happening in Europe today, what we're hearing and seeing today, is that the military conflict in Ukraine has got out of control," he went on, warning them that Europe faced the possibility of a wider war.

He asked the students to imagine Slovakia being told to prepare "5,000 soldiers, 10,000 soldiers" who would go somewhere to fight "without us even knowing whom they are fighting, or why, or what for."

Then he turned to Article 5 of NATO's founding treaty, under which an armed attack on one member is considered an attack on all.

"I do not want Slovakia to be part of any military conflict because of some Article 5," Fico said.

"Yes, we are in NATO. We value that. Cooperation and fulfilling our obligations matter to us. But as prime minister, I will do everything to ensure that Slovakia is never drawn into a military conflict."

The remarks prompted uproar from the Slovak opposition and also a spat with Petr Pavel, president of neighboring Czechia and former chairman of NATO's Military Committee.

"If anyone says they're glad to be a member of NATO … but are unwilling to get involved if anyone else is attacked, then they have not understood the word solidarity," Pavel said.

Fico replied that he understood that Pavel, as a former NATO general, would like to fight, while he himself preferred peace.

The Slovak prime minister's fear of a wider war is shared by many Europeans. But critics at home gave his remarks short shrift.

"Isn't he a prime minister? Isn't he the leader of a country? Is he not supposed to lead people instead of expressing fears?" asked former Slovak Foreign Minister Miro Wlachovsky, now a distinguished fellow at the GLOBSEC think tank.

"My question for him as prime minister is: OK, even if the situation is as bad as you are saying, what I want to hear from you is how the country is prepared. What have you done for preparedness? Who will come to our aid in case we are attacked?" Wlachovsky told DW.

Asked whether Fico's remarks could damage Slovakia's standing among its allies, Wlachovsky replied: "It's already done."

The Slovak prime minister will face a difficult reelection fight next year. Some observers believe Fico is trying to steal votes from the extreme-right Republika party, which is viscerally hostile to NATO and the EU and opposes military support for Ukraine.

Wlachovsky compared Fico's rhetoric to that of Hungary's former Prime Minister Viktor Orban and the current Czech Prime Minister Andrej Babis, both of whom have presented themselves as the candidates of peace.

Babis was heavily criticized during his unsuccessful 2023 presidential campaign after questioning during a televised debate whether the Czech Republic should send troops to NATO ally Poland if it came under attack.

Oana Lungescu, a distinguished fellow at the Royal United Services Institute and a former NATO spokesperson, told DW that Article 5 was "the most solemn pledge that a country can make to another."

But its central purpose, she said, was deterrence: convincing a potential aggressor that it would face the alliance together, rather than an isolated member.

"This is about solidarity, standing together, underpinning deterrence and therefore avoiding war," she said. "NATO is a defensive alliance. It's not an offensive alliance."

Nor would invoking Article 5 automatically send every member's troops into combat, she said.

The North Atlantic Council, where every ally is represented, would assess an attack and decide by consensus whether it constituted an Article 5 situation — Slovakia's voice would be equal to that of its 31 partners in that room, she said.

Once consensus was reached, each country would then assist the attacked ally by taking the action it deemed necessary, which might — or might not — involve armed force.

Article 5 has been invoked only once, following the September 11, 2001 attacks on the United States.

Lungescu pointed to one of NATO's initial responses: sending AWACS surveillance aircraft to patrol US skies and help prevent another attack.

She pointed out that Slovakia already benefits from the same solidarity Fico appeared to question. The country hosts a NATO multinational battlegroup established after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, with personnel from several allies alongside Slovak troops.

For former Slovak Defense Minister Jaroslav Nad, the danger lies in allowing that fear to dictate policy towards Ukraine.

"This is exactly what Vladimir Putin wants," said Nad, who was defense minister from 2020 to 2023 and is now head of the non-parliamentary opposition Democrats party.

"He wants citizens in the EU and NATO to believe that if EU and NATO countries continue to support Ukraine, this escalatory situation might lead to a Third World War or something like that," Nad told DW.

Nad was in office when Slovakia decided to hand over its entire fleet of ageing MiG-29 fighter jets to Ukraine. Fico's government later sought a criminal investigation into the transfer; prosecutors closed the case, finding no crime.

"Even when I was defense minister, I heard this not only from ordinary people but also from some ministers around the table: that we shouldn't help Ukraine with this or that equipment or capability or ammunition, because it might create a difficult situation in terms of escalation towards Russia," he went on.

## Germany&apos;s oldest party, the center-left SPD, is in a difficult position following its success in the Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania state election

https://p.dw.com/p/5N3XPManuela Schwesig is the new center of power in the SPDImage: Annegret Hilse/REUTERSAdvertisementFollowing state elections in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania and Berlin, two of Germany's 16 federal states, the mood within the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) is oscillating between relief and frustration, confidence and bewilderment.

"The SPD is clearly capable of winning more than 30% of the vote," party co-leader Lars Klingbeil said jubilantly in Berlin. "This result in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania is an encouragement for the entire Social Democratic movement."

As Klingbeil put it, Manuela Schwesig, the SPD state premier of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania and her party had shown "how to make up ground, how to fight, and how, with the right issues and the right approach, to stage a comeback and ultimately win people over through that effort."

What Klingbeil conveniently omits is that Schwesig's success was also because she distanced herself as much as possible from the federal government's policies, and by extension from the SPD at the national level, which it governs in coalition with the center-right CDU/CSU alliance of the Christian Democratic Union and its Bavarian sister party, the Christian Social Union under Chancellor Friedrich Merz.

Meanwhile, the national government has become more unpopular than any of its predecessors.

Schwesig scored points above all with her opposition to parts of the federal government's pension reform plans. At the post-election review meeting in Berlin, she intensified her criticism, launching a pointed attack on the chancellor. She argued that 81% of people in her state consider the reforms unfair and that Merz is misjudging the situation.

On election night, Friedrich Merz made clear that he was all the more determined to stay the course. "The reforms must go ahead," the chancellor said. "Of course, this puts all of us in a difficult position. What needs to be done now requires courage, resilience, and patience. I will demonstrate those qualities as leader of the CDU and, above all, as chancellor of our country."

Schwesig had a clear response. "My advice to the chancellor, if he wants to regain people's trust, though I'm not sure that's still possible, is that he needs to engage with the reality on the ground." She argued that Merz must ask himself what needs to change. "There can be no business as usual."

The SPD, and above all its leaders, Lars Klingbeil and Bärbel Bas, now find themselves in a dilemma. Both serve as cabinet ministers in Merz's government and therefore help implement policies that many SPD members are increasingly questioning.

At the same time, they are accountable to their party and must ensure that SPD priorities are reflected in government policy. The success Schwesig achieved with her unequivocally critical stance is now putting them under considerable pressure.

Following her electoral victory, Schwesig has emerged as one of the most influential figures within the SPD. Many party members would like to see Schwesig become the party's next leader.

She already appeared to be embracing that role as she stood before the microphone at the post-election review, dressed head-to-toe in red and exuding confidence. By contrast, Bas and Klingbeil appeared almost defensive, seeking to strike a balance between criticizing the coalition and supporting it.

"If people in our country perceive reforms as something that threatens their way of life and leads only to things getting worse, then it is not just our communication that needs to improve. In some areas, the substance of the reforms themselves needs to change as well," said Klingbeil.

Bas called for both a "reality check" and a "fairness check" on the reforms, arguing that they must be workable in practice and socially equitable.

Will that be enough? Calls for the SPD to be "more like Schwesig," in other words, to assert its own positions with greater confidence, are growing ever louder within the party. The only question is how much dissent a governing party can afford to show. "Nothing would be more damaging than allowing this coalition to collapse over a policy dispute at this point," Bas warned. Solutions, she argued, "are possible within this coalition as well."

At a leadership committee meeting on the morning after the state elections, the SPD leadership adopted a strategy paper. Across nine key points, it sets out the priorities the party intends to champion in government. One section is headed "Real Change Instead of Simply Cutting Spending," while another is titled "An Affordable Life for All."

The SPD wants inheritance tax reform to ensure that the resulting revenue can fund a "genuine education initiative." On pension reform, the paper states that important questions remain unresolved regarding phased retirement and the right to retire without pension reductions after 45 years of contributions.

The SPD leaves no doubt about its stance: "The lifetime achievements of people who have paid into the system for many years must continue to make a difference."

It remains unclear whether the CDU/CSU will respond to these demands. The same is true of the simmering debate over whether Bas and Klingbeil should be replaced at the helm of the party.

Pressure continues to come from Philipp Türmer, leader of the Jusos, the SPD's youth wing. "I'm not saying that the two of them are the problem, but at the moment they're not part of the solution either," he told German broadcaster RTL and its news channel ntv.

Türmer and others had called for a special party conference that could have paved the way for replacing Bas and Klingbeil. Instead, the party will hold a convention in December, a smaller party gathering that cannot elect or remove party leaders.

From now on, however, SPD members are likely to judge their leaders even more by how effectively they defend the SPD's positions in government and whether they can restore the party's appeal among voters.

## Ethiopia: Fighting resumes in Tigray

https://p.dw.com/p/5N83UEthiopian government forces have come under renewed attack at regional airports across Tigray as well as positions in Afar and AmharaImage: Minasse Wondimu Hailu/Anadolu Agency/picture allianceAdvertisementRebels from the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF) have reportedly taken control of regional airports in Tigray, in northern Ethiopia, and further clashed with government forces in the neighboring Afar and Amhara regions.

Wednesday's clashes are the latest of several in recent months and have sparked fears of renewed intense fighting in the war-ravaged region.

The African Union (AU) estimates that some 600,000 people were killed in fighting between the TPLF and Ethiopian forces during a 2020-2022 civil war and that more than 1 million people remain displaced.

On Wednesday, state-owned Ethiopian Airlines announced that it had canceled service at Mekelle, Tigray's capital, as well as Axum and Shire regional airports "due to the current situation."

Local witnesses reported that TPLF rebels armed with light weapons had taken Mekelle Airport — which is normally controlled by government forces — without a fight.

Renewed fighting comes just days after the TPLF announced that it had entered an "Alliance for Survival" with other groups aimed at removing Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed and his government from power.

Abiy and his Prosperity Party won reelection in June, though the vote was boycotted by Ethiopia's main opposition groups.

The TPLF ruled Ethiopia from 1991 until 2018, when it was replaced by Abiy, whose election sparked the country's civil war.

The Pretoria Peace Agreement brought an end to the conflict, but peace has remained elusive, with government troops resuming drone strikes in 2025 as TPLF rebels began amassing troops at regional borders.

An advisor to Prime Minister Abiy called Wednesday's development "heartbreaking," saying rebels had launched offensives against government forces in Afar and Amhara as well as taking control of airports in Tigray.

However, the Tigray army "has been instructed to counter the attack and genocidal invasion launched against us, and has entered into a defensive battle," the Tigray government said in a statement on Facebook.

## ICC convicts CAR ex-rebel leader of crimes against humanity

https://p.dw.com/p/5N6EMThe International Criminal Court is situated in The Hague, NetherlandsImage: Remko De Waal/ANP/AFPAdvertisementThe International Criminal Court on Wednesday convicted a former Central African Republic rebel commander on four counts of crimes against humanity committed while he was in charge of a detention center in 2013.

The court found that Mahamat Said Abdel Kani, 56, oversaw beatings and maltreatment as the country was gripped by sectarian violence.

Said also faced three counts of war crimes but was acquitted of them. During his trial, he pleaded not guilty to all charges for the crimes, which were carried out between April and August 2013.

Said was a top commander of the mainly Muslim Seleka rebel group that ousted President Francois Bozize in 2013 amid a civil war.

He was put in charge of the Central Office for the Repression of Banditry (OCRB), a prison unit in the capital, Bangui.

Prosecutors allege that prisoners at the facility, who were suspected supporters of Bozize, were subjected to various forms of torture, including whippings and body mutilation.

According to the court, inmates were also denied food or water, with some drinking their own urine out of desperation.

One cell, dubbed "the hole," where prisoners were held in dire conditions, was said by the court to be directly under Said's office.

Said stood accused of overseeing this maltreatment and personally participating in the beating of at least one prisoner, the court found.

Said was extradited to the ICC by Central African authorities in 2021, with his trial beginning in September 2022.

The ICC, established in The Hague in 2022, is the world's only independent tribunal capable of prosecuting those accused of the world's worst crimes.

## As the EU advances free trade with Southeast Asia, why is there no ASEAN-wide deal?

https://p.dw.com/p/5N6W6The Philippines is one of the many ASEAN countries pursuing closer ties with the EUImage: Ezra Acayan/AFPAdvertisementThe EU and several Southeast Asian nations are nearing the finish line on bilateral trade deals, and if the current deadlines hold, the European bloc will have agreements in place with six out of 11 ASEAN states by the middle of next year.

Free trade agreements with Singapore and Vietnam are already in force. Negotiations with Indonesia have concluded, and Jakarta aims to sign the agreement in October, with implementation targeted for early 2027.

Talks with Thailand, relaunched in 2023, have reached nine negotiating rounds, while Malaysia resumed negotiations in early 2025 after a 13-year pause. Both are expected to be finalized by the end of the year or early 2027.

Earlier this week, the EU and the Philippines announced that they had reached "substantial agreement" on their free trade agreement, putting it on course for formal conclusion in the coming months.

Speaking to DW, Chris Humphrey, executive director of the EU-ASEAN Business Council, described EU engagement with ASEAN on trade and investment issues as "at an all-time high."

EU-Southeast Asia goods trade reached €274.9 billion ($313.7 billion) last year, up around 6% from the previous year and more than 50% from the €177.9 billion recorded in 2016, according to EU data.

The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) is now the EU's third-largest goods trading partner outside Europe, behind only the United States and China.

This week, Manila hosted both the ASEAN–EU Business Summit and the 22nd meeting of ASEAN economic ministers and the EU's trade commissioner. ## El Nino explained: How the weather pattern affects hurricanes and tropical storms

https://p.dw.com/p/5N515None of the six tropical storms registered in the Atlantic so far this year have reached hurricane status (FILE photo of Hurricane Matthew in Oct. 2016)Image: Tim Aylen/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementIn a typical year, storm watchers have logged several hurricanes by late September. At this point in 2005, one of the most active years on record, five major hurricanes had already churned their way through the Atlantic Ocean — including Katrina, one of the most devastating in US history.

But 2026 has been quiet so far. As of September 23, only six tropical storms had formed over the North Atlantic, and none had intensified into hurricanes — a first since satellites began tracking storms in 1966, according to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) in the US.

"In the satellite era, we have never had a year without a hurricane," said Matthew Rosencrans, lead hurricane season forecaster with NOAA's Climate Prediction Center. "This would be a first."

That's due, in large part, to the latest iteration of the El Nino-Southern Oscillation (ENSO), a shifting cycle of winds and sea surface temperatures over the Pacific Ocean that shapes weather worldwide and makes it a "dominant factor in total hurricane season activity." Meteorologists have said this year's El Nino could be one of the strongest on record.

NOAA's Climate Prediction Center, which tracks Atlantic storm data in the US, said the accumulated cyclone energy of this year's tropical storms — a calculation that factors in storm duration and intensity — was at a record low for so late in the season, which typically peaks on September 10.

None of the six tropical storms registered so far have reached a wind speed of at least 119 kilometers per hour (74 miles per hour) — strong enough to be considered a Category 1 hurricane with the potential to cause some minor damage to homes, trees and other infrastructure.

The name for such a catastrophic tropical storm is regional. What's known as a hurricane in the North Atlantic and eastern Pacific will be called a typhoon on the other side of the Pacific, in eastern Asia. In the Indian Ocean and the South Pacific, meteorologists will generally classify a similar storm as a cyclone.

How do tropical storms form?To view this video please enable JavaScript, and consider upgrading to a web browser that supports HTML5 video

NOAA's latest data showed that El Nino is still strengthening, with sea surface temperature rise in the key Pacific region now exceeding 3 degrees Celsius (5.4 Fahrenheit), creating a "very strong" El Nino over the next half year — and a "below-normal" hurricane season.

Almost all Atlantic tropical cyclone activity occurs between June 1 and November 30, though hurricanes can form at other times of the year. And meteorologists have warned that this year's calm doesn't rule out a major hurricane before the season is done.

The naturally occurring El Nino weather pattern returns on average every two to seven years, with its effects lasting up to a year. During El Nino, the regular trade winds moving east-west across the Pacific weaken and sometimes even reverse. The changes in humidity and wind shift the jet stream, the strong winds in the upper atmosphere — and disrupt storm formation in the Atlantic.

NOAA's Climate Prediction Center said any hurricanes that do appear in an El Nino year tend to form along the US East Coast and near the Gulf of Mexico, giving them less time to turn into major hurricanes — but also leaving coastal communities with less time to prepare. That pattern is largely dictated by the steering currents when it forms, which typically last around a week.

"Formations deep in the tropics are hampered due to high wind shear," said Rosencrans — the strong changes in wind speed or direction at different altitudes are more likely to disrupt storm formation. "The stronger the El Nino event, the stronger the wind shear."

During El Nino, vertical wind shear isn't as strong in the eastern and central Pacific Ocean. Combined with the warmer waters in the Pacific, which fuel storms with heat and moisture, that tends to result in an increase in tropical cyclones in this region. This part of the Pacific has already seen several major hurricanes this year, including Polo, which this week has grown into one of the most powerful Pacific cyclones in decades — fueled in part by El Nino.

"Typically, El Nino means the storms form further east […] toward the international date line and Marshall Islands," Rosencrans told DW, anticipating an "above average" hurricane season in the Pacific. "This does mean that storms have longer life spans before reaching large land masses."

Anne-Claire Fontan at the World Meteorological Organization said during an El Nino event, storms on the Asian side of the vast Pacific tend to track "northward, rather than continue westwards towards East and Southeast Asia."

That meant, according to Rosencrans, that Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands, Japan and the Korean Peninsula "can face slightly higher odds of tropical storms and typhoons."

A strong El Nino will weaken monsoon winds over the Indian subcontinent, often triggering severe drought. It also generally corresponds with a less active tropical cyclone season in the Indian Ocean, said Fontan, a scientific officer with the WMO's Tropical Cyclone Program.

Much like in the Atlantic, increased wind shear in this region can disrupt or delay the formation of storms, which Fontan said lessened the risks for countries in eastern Africa, like Madagascar and Mozambique.

"In terms of overall tropical cyclone activity in the Southwest Indian Ocean, there is a non-linear, or 'bell-shaped,' relationship with ENSO," said Fontan, highlighting El Nino and its opposite phase. "As ENSO moves towards either of its extremes — a strong El Nino or a strong La Nina — tropical cyclone activity in the basin tends to be below normal."

The El Nino-Southern Oscillation is a natural phenomenon, and not caused by climate change. And, as Rosencrans pointed out, "the interplay of El Nino or La Nina and the longer-lived changes is just one piece of what makes hurricane seasons active or inactive."

Among those factors is the dry, dusty air drifting out over the Atlantic from the Sahara Desert, which can also suppress hurricanes.

But Fontan said that the especially strong El Nino effect, combined with the warming effects of climate change from burning fossil fuels, is setting the stage for unprecedented weather extremes in the months ahead.

"El Nino is occurring in a warmer world, and this can amplify some of its impacts, including extreme heat, heavy rainfall and drought," she told DW. Already, some forecasters like the UK's Met Office have said the combined effects of El Nino and climate change could shatter heat records in 2027. A new report from the University of Chicago-based Climate Impact Lab has projected that extreme heat could cause nearly half a million more deaths worldwide during this El Nino period, especially in the Global South.

"We are already seeing the disruption and devastation caused by droughts and floods, and we expect these impacts to worsen as El Nino intensifies," said Celeste Saulo, head of WMO, in early September, in a stark warning for governments and humanitarian organizations.

## China&apos;s new travel rules unsettle tech giants and talent

https://p.dw.com/p/5MojpChina wants its best minds to stay, while adding extra scrutiny for foreign expertsImage: Wang Yu/Xinhua/picture allianceAdvertisementFor decades, China has faced accusations from the United States and the European Union of stealing their technology.

From robotics and advanced semiconductors to precision engineering and automotive parts, Chinese firms have recruited top engineers, allegedly spied on Western rivals and been linked to cyberattacks aimed at gaining access to valuable trade secrets.

It is perhaps ironic, then, that China is now taking steps to prevent its own high-end technologies and talent from escaping abroad.

Last week, new entry-exit rules took effect, giving Chinese authorities the power to stop engineers, founders and other specialists from leaving the country if their expertise in batteries, rare earths or artificial intelligence (AI) is judged a threat to "industrial and technological security."

Recently, China has also tightened outbound-investment rules, cracked down on Chinese nationals holding their wealth offshore and restricted the posting of technical staff overseas.

Henry Gao, a law professor at Singapore Management University (SMU), believes that, taken together, the curbs "offer a rare glimpse into the true state of China's economy."

"These measures suggest that Beijing is deeply concerned about economic weakness and substantial capital outflows," Gao told DW, adding that authorities are also determined to stop "entrepreneurs and skilled personnel from leaving the country."

The strain on China's economy is increasingly hard to hide. While demand for exports remains strong — especially for high-tech goods — the country's property crash has severely weakened domestic consumption.

Bank lending fell to a record low over the summer, and new car sales in August dropped nearly a quarter year-on-year.

The same anxiety now extends to people and money leaving. The most prominent recent example is Manus, an AI startup created by two Chinese nationals in Beijing. Last year, the company moved its headquarters to Singapore, partly to avoid US investment curbs and expand into global markets.

When Facebook owner Meta tried to acquire Manus last December for $2 billion (€1.74 billion), Beijing blocked the move and barred the firm's two founders from leaving the country.

Just this month, Chinese social media has been awash with rumors that Huawei founder and CEO Ren Zhengfei and his daughter, CFO Meng Wanzhou, may have left the country.

The claims were picked up by Taiwanese and Indian media outlets. On Wednesday, however, Chinese-language media shared a photo of Ren in Shenzhen a day earlier, signing a cooperation deal with a Chinese automaker.

Bloomberg Intelligence estimated that some $1 trillion in Chinese wealth had exited the country last year — the largest amount of "hot money" leaving since 2006, when records began.

Beijing has not tightened its annual $50,000 foreign-exchange quota that households may take out of the country. What has tightened, says Alicia Garcia-Herrero, chief economist for Asia-Pacific at French investment bank Natixis, are the unofficial routes the wealthy used around that cap.

"They haven't changed the $50,000 quota. They are now squeezing the people and agents that money usually travels with," Garcia-Herrero told DW.

An entire industry has grown up, in China and abroad, to help people get themselves and their capital out. Emigration agents arrange visas and relocations, while offshore brokers and trusts help Chinese nationals to park wealth overseas.

Companies sometimes use a foreign subsidiary, a listing or a staff posting to take money and know-how out together.

SMU's Gao believes the new curbs may prove counterproductive, as the more difficult it becomes to move capital or people abroad, the greater the incentive for those with the means to find other ways out.

"Over time, that could further erode confidence, accelerate capital flight and deepen the very economic problems the measures are intended to contain," Gao told DW.

Garcia-Herrero, meanwhile, thinks the block on talent will matter more than the squeeze on capital.

"You can still move money slowly, with approvals," she told DW. "You cannot easily replace a process engineer who cannot board a plane — or who will not take an overseas job because of an indefinite ban."

The risk is already hitting China's AI firms, which are fast catching up with their US rivals in the race to produce the most advanced AI systems.

In May, Bloomberg reported that Chinese authorities now required top AI researchers, founders and executives at firms like Alibaba and DeepSeek to obtain approval before traveling abroad.

Last year, some DeepSeek staff were asked to hand in their passports, according to tech news site The Information. Beijing has neither confirmed nor denied the practice.

Bloomberg reported in January last year that other tech engineers face similar pressure, including those helping foreign firms cut their reliance on China by building factories in Vietnam and India.

For foreign nationals, both Chinese and foreign firms sometimes disguise a working visit to China to avoid scrutiny and visa delays. They may suggest a trip is for tourism, business meetings or a factory tour rather than actual work.

Ahead of the new curbs, international law firms like DLA Piper advised companies to keep visa filings "truthful" and "complete," warning that a mismatch between the paperwork and the actual work could now mean an entry ban of up to five years.

The new rules state that a visitor should, in principle, be told if they are banned. However, Chinese authorities may withhold notification if it "may affect national security or the investigation of criminal cases."

In an updated travel advisory earlier this month, the US State Department went further, warning Americans to "exercise increased caution in mainland China due to ... the use of exit bans without a fair and transparent process under the law, and the risk of unjust arrest or detention."

https://p.dw.com/p/5N5DsOnly 10% of global oceans are currently designated for conservation and could only reach 12% by 2030, the research revealsImage: DWAdvertisementWorld governments are off track to meet a historic United Nations target to protect at least 30% of the planet's land and seas by the year 2030, according to a new study published Wednesday.

The research by environmental groups Campaign for Nature and Together for the Ocean shows that about 20% of land and inland waters are designated as protected or conserved, following the signing of the 30×30 goal in 2022.

If nations fully deliver on existing quantified commitments, the estimate could increase to 27% by the turn of the decade, the report said.

The 30x30 goal was adopted under the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework at COP15 and was signed by nearly 200 nations that pledged at least $200 billion (about €175 billion) annually to reduce biodiversity loss.

The coverage is currently at 10% of global ocean and could only reach 12% by 2030, the research revealed.

"The ocean is emerging as one of the defining implementation challenges of 30×30," Together for the Ocean said, adding that important marine ecosystems, like offshore and deep-sea habitats, remain "vulnerable."

Last month, the Convention on Biological Diversity, the UN treaty dedicated to the global nature goals, also cautioned that countries were not on track.

The report singled out finance for conservation as "one of the greatest barriers" to reaching global targets.

The research said that between $100 billion and $180 billion was needed each year to make the 2030 target. Current annual spending, however, amounted to just $24 billion.

"The gap is particularly stark for the ocean, where approximately $16 billion is needed annually, but only an estimated $ 1.8 billion is currently spent," the study said.

From October 19, the UN biodiversity summit in Yerevan will see the nations address the progress shortfall and assess ways of getting back on target.
