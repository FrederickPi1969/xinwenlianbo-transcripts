## Schools crisis in France: Investment fails to inspire confidence

https://p.dw.com/p/5O5ISUniversity students have joined in on the protests to support schoolchildrenImage: Christophe Agostinis/MAXPPP/picture allianceAdvertisementRats in the toilets, leaking roofs, broken heating systems: Cellphone videos filmed by French schoolchildren and circulating on social media bear witness to the dilapidated infrastructure in many educational institutions across France.

At the Lycee Paul Eluard in the Parisian suburb of Saint-Denis, a center of the current protests, young people have reported that there are vermin in the building, and say burst pipes are a regular occurrence. In nearby Pantin, pupils complained that windows couldn't be opened, even when temperatures soared to 40 degrees Celsius (over 104 degrees Fahrenheit) in summer.

Yet, by EU standards, France invests a substantial amount in its education system — 6.7% of its GDP. The fact that this investment has not translated into a better learning environment may be partly attributable to the way responsibilities are divided.

The central government in Paris mainly deals with paying teachers, and setting national curricula. But the responsibility for maintaining school buildings lies with France's various levels of local government. For elementary schools, the administration responsible is the municipality; for "colleges" (lower secondary schools, ages 11–15) it is the wider department, and for lycees (upper secondary schools, ages 15–18) it is the region.

Depending on the region, the local authorities have different financial possibilities at their disposal, and must plan renovation projects themselves. Progress on making improvements is often very slow as a result. In addition, more than two-thirds of their budget is already allocated to staffing costs and pension payments, leaving relatively little room for investment.

Over 80% of elementary school buildings in France are more than 50 years old. An estimated 10 to 20% of all school buildings are considered to be in serious need of renovation. In many instances, politicians have been trying to address the problem. Ile-de-France, which covers Paris and the region surrounding it, approved a 10-year program of renovation and expansion for the period 2017 to 2027 at a cost of €6.6 billion ($7.4 billion).

According to the local authorities, two-thirds of upper secondary pupils now attend a new school, or one that has been renovated. But even after mold has been removed and roofs repaired, it is still rare for buildings to have effective protection against the summer heat. It is also a bitter irony that some buildings newly constructed at considerable expense, or renovated just a few years earlier, have been damaged in the recent protests.

Imbalances are also evident in ongoing education expenditure. While France invests a comparatively large amount in upper secondary education, according to OECD calculations Paris spends less per primary school pupil than the average for industrialized countries. Education researchers emphasize that it is precisely these early years that lay the foundation for subsequent academic success.

The governments put in place by French President Emmanuel Macron have attempted to address this imbalance. Since 2017, class sizes in the first years of primary school have been halved in socially disadvantaged areas, and around 16,000 additional teaching posts created.

Amid the ongoing protests, the government has said it now intends to recruit more than 3,000 supply teachers at short notice. French Education Minister Edouard Geffray  announced that software would be used to identify schools that are in a particularly difficult situation.

These announcements are the government's first concrete response to the demands of the students, who have been demonstrating for almost two weeks now, calling for better facilities in secondary schools, more supply teachers and greater investment in the education system generally.

The latest scores for the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development’s (OECD) program for international student assessment (Pisa), which were published in September, illustrate the scale of the problems in the education system. According to the results, more than one in three 15-year-olds in France no longer achieve a basic level of competence in mathematics, for example.

Macron's reform of the upper secondary education system has also been criticized. The abolition of academic streaming in favor of specialist subjects selected by the individual was meant to give pupils greater freedom of choice. However, the large number of possible subject combinations make it difficult to timetable lessons. Students complain that they have too many free periods and longer school days.

The problems also extend to universities. A lecturer at a Paris university described a seminar attended by 50 students — so many that they have to fetch chairs from other rooms or sit on the floor. So far, she said, the university administration had done nothing about it.

Compared with pupils in other EU countries, French schoolchildren have a high number of teaching hours per school year — on paper, at least. But the French Court of Accounts recorded that 9% of lessons at state colleges were cancelled in the 2023–24 school year. A study from the school year 2024–25 suggests that only around 12% of lessons cancelled at short notice are covered by other teachers.

The relatively low salaries of French teachers compared to those in neighboring countries are also a problem. OECD data shows that after 15 years in the profession teachers at lower secondary schools in France only earn about half as much as their German counterparts.

France takes pride in its republican model of education, which is supposed to provide all children with equal opportunities, irrespective of their background. In reality, academic success in France is much more dependent on parents' socioeconomic circumstances than in many other countries. This is also apparent in that children from poorer families are far more likely to be affected by the cancellation of lessons.

The government's financial room for maneuver is currently restricted by the country's precarious budgetary situation. Although the draft budget for 2027 includes an additional €1.2 billion for the Ministry of Education, there are also plans to cut teaching posts, on the grounds that pupil numbers are falling.

It's not just videos of dilapidated school buildings that are circulating on social media. As the discontent has spread, there is also footage of demonstrations where people carry placards with political slogans that have little to do with education policy. Some videos show burning barricades and clashes with the police, which faces accusations of usingdisproportionate force.

Some members of the far-left France Unbowed (LFI) party have associated themselves with the protesting students. Lawmakers have appeared at school blockades and shared related videos. The government has accused LFI of deliberately exploiting young people's anger for political purposes, an allegation that the party rejects.

With the French presidential election just six months away, the protests over the crisis in education are evolving into a power struggle.

## Trump, Putin agree on deal to release Russian diesel to US, world markets

https://p.dw.com/p/5O5foTrump said he had had a 'highly successful' discussion with Putin [FILE: August 15, 2025]Image: Julia Demaree Nikhinson/AP Photo/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementUS President Donald Trump on Friday said Russian President Vladimir Putin had agreed to release Russian diesel to US and global markets.

The move, which aims to address rising fuel costs before the US midterm elections, marks a dramatic turnaround in US policy toward Moscow since the start of the war in Ukraine.

In a social media post, Trump wrote he had made a deal with Putin during a phone call that was supposed to be about a suspected plague outbreak in Russia.

According to Trump, Russia would immediately supply more than 300,000 tons of diesel, followed by an additional 500,000 tons in November and 1 million tons "immediately thereafter."

Trump wrote that Russia would deliver another 3 million tons "within a short period of time" after that.

The US Treasury Department issued a temporary license that will allow Russian diesel to reach global markets through April 2027.

Trump's announcement follows his signing of a broad Russia sanctions law last month. The law is intended to clamp down on Russia’s energy sector and other areas that fuel its war against Ukraine.

Kirill Dmitriev, the Russian presidential envoy, reposted the Treasury Department's announcement on X, saying that "Russia-US cooperation on diesel and energy will benefit the world."

Alexander Novak, Russia's Deputy Prime Minister, said that the country was ready to start supplying additional volumes of diesel to the United States and other countries from October.

He also said that Russia's exports are expected to rise further in November and December as refineries resume operations after maintenance.

Later, the Kremlin released a statement from Putin saying that, during their call, he and Trump had also devoted "significant attention to the prospects of resolving the Ukrainian crisis" and had discussed "the situation around Iran and various aspects of bilateral relations."

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has condemned the agreement, saying that any easing of sanctions without a clear and lasting de-escalation agreement with Russia is an obvious sign of "weakness."

"Gifts to Putin will not bring peace or any benefit to the civilized world. Russia will 'repay' the diesel with further terror and perfidy," he wrote on social media.

"Allowing Russia to ⁠sell ​petroleum products ​is an investment in a ​war that ‌must be ended, not prolonged. What ​is needed ⁠is real de-escalation with Russia ⁠on ​a reciprocal basis," the Ukrainian president stressed.

Zelenskyy added that Ukraine hopes the US will have a strong conversation with Russia: "A strong one, not a weak one."

Since its 2022 full-scale invasion of neighboring Ukraine, Moscow has been hit with rounds of sanctions by the United States and its Western partners.

But as the war has continued with no resolution in sight, Washington's commitment to that approach has appeared to waver.

Earlier this year, temporary US sanctions waivers that allowed the sale of Russian oil raised concerns because they could hinder efforts to deprive Moscow of funds for its war in Ukraine.

Last month, Trump blamed Ukrainian strikes on Russian oil refineries for soaring diesel prices, and even publicly told Zelenskyy to stop the attacks.

Trump's announcement on Friday came just as US, Ukrainian, and European officials were meeting in Miami for new diplomatic talks aimed at ending Russia's war in Ukraine.

According to US envoy Steve Witkoff, Ukrainian, European, US and NATO representatives held "constructive" discussions in Miami, and agreed to continue diplomatic efforts to end the conflict.

According to Ines Pohl, the chief of DW's Washington D.C. bureau, the diesel deal is mainly about domestic politics.

With the midterms only weeks away, Trump is under heavy pressure over high energy prices. The deal allows him to show that he is taking action and appeal to voters who feel the rising cost of fuel directly, while the war in Ukraine seems far away.

Politically, that is significant: Trump is accepting a loosening of sanctions on Russia, even though Washington had actually wanted to increase pressure on Putin.

It also makes one thing crystal clear: when push comes to shove, Trump consistently chooses what benefits him personally over what Congress has decided.

He appears to be betting that, for many Americans, prices at the pump matter more in the end than the geopolitical consequences. Whether the promised deliveries will actually bring noticeable relief, however, remains doubtful.

## Strong earthquakes shake Panama, buildings damaged

https://p.dw.com/p/5O5ceThe governor's office in Penonome was severely damaged Image: Martin Bernetti/AFPAdvertisementA powerful magnitude-7.7 earthquake struck Panama on Friday morning.

According to the US Geological Survey (USGS), the quake's epicenter was located approximately 10 kilometers (6 miles) southwest of the remote community of Pitaloza Arriba, near Panama's southern tip.

The quake struck at a depth of 10 kilometers. Shallow earthquakes (up to 70 kilometers deep) are considered more dangerous and destructive.

The USGS later reported that a second powerful earthquake, measuring 6.6 in magnitude, struck the country following a series of aftershocks.

The tremors were strongly felt in the capital Panama City, causing buildings to shake and sending people rushing into the streets.

"We felt it very strongly. It knocked things over in the kitchen, but we're okay, though we're very scared," Rosendo Domínguez, 60, who is from Herrera province, where the earthquake struck, told The Associated Press by phone. “Everyone is outside, scared."

Panamanian President Jose Raul Mulino described the earthquake as a "major disaster" which had destroyed roads near the epicenter.

According to Omar Smith of the National Civil Protection Service, damage was concentrated in the central provinces of Veraguas, Herrera and Los Santos.

Smith reported several injuries, as well as partial collapses and cracks at the cathedral in the district’s main square, as well as damage to the governor’s office building and the Dos Continentes hotel.

Meanwhile, local news outlets reported that the colonial-style Church of San Atanasio in Panama’s Los Santos province collapsed. The church was declared a National Historic Heritage Site in 1938.

The US Tsunami Warning System issued an alert stating that hazardous tsunami waves were possible along parts of the coasts of Panama, Colombia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, Peru, Nicaragua, Guatemala, El Salvador, and Mexico within the next three hours.

The system also reported a tsunami threat to Hawaii. The warning was lifted later on Friday afternoon.

Initially, there were no reports of damage to the Panama Canal. The Panama Canal Authority stated that the waterway was operating as usual.

Tocumen International Airport in Panama City briefly suspended operations and activated evacuation protocols before reopening later on Friday afternoon.

Panama is located along the so-called "Ring of Fire," a line of seismic faults encircling the Pacific Ocean where most of the world's earthquakes occur.

## China, EU reach deal to &apos;moderate&apos; China&apos;s car exports to EU

https://p.dw.com/p/5O5A2China's increased exports of electric vehicles to the EU have become a problem for European carmakersImage: Avalon/IMAGOAdvertisementEuropean Trade Commissioner Maros Sefcovic announced on Friday that China and the European Union had reached a deal that could cut Chinese exports of hybrid cars to the EU by more than half.

This announcement followed two-day talks in Beijing aimed at reducing the EU's growing trade deficit with China.

Earlier, Sefcovic said this week's talks were the culmination of three months of intensive work.

According to Sefcovic, the deal would "moderate China's export of hybrids and plug-in hybrids to the EU."

He added that this opens the prospect of cutting China's exports by more than half, based on projections over a four-year period.

"By this step, we're actually preventing several millions of cars exports from China to the European Union," Sefcovic said.

Although he did not offer details on how the agreement will be implemented, Sefcovic said he believed the two sides had achieved a very good result.

Sevcovic added the two sides also reached an agreement to reduce Chinese import duties on €4 billion ($4.5 billion) worth of EU exports, including car parts, olive oil and footwear.

They also agreed to simplify China's process for granting export licenses for rare earths and permanent magnets.

The agreement will require approval from the leaders of all 27 EU nations, who will discuss the outcome at the start of their summit in Brussels on Thursday.

According to UN Comtrade data, Chinese exports to the EU totaled $560 billion (€500 billion) last year, up from $517 billion in 2023. Exports to major economies, including Germany, Italy, Spain, and Poland, each rose approximately 10% from a year earlier. Shipments to Hungary increased by 43%.

Meanwhile, China imported $268.3 billion (€240 billion) worth of European goods last year, down from $269.4 billion the previous year. Purchases from Denmark, Ireland, and France led the way.

Last month, European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen warned lawmakers at the European Parliament that the trade gap had reached a tipping point. She said the EU would use all available tools to rebalance the relationship.

Masked protesters dressed in black threw rocks and other projectiles at police in Brussels. Anti-police slogans echoed through the streets, and similar graffiti covered nearby walls.

An AFP reporter witnessed two buses being attacked after the passengers were evacuated. The rioters smashed the windows and ripped out the seats. Meanwhile, youths set fire to trash cans, rental bikes, and scooters.

"Those individuals have nothing to do with the message with which thousands of others come out on the streets," Engelaar wrote on Facebook.

Chaotic scenes of violence occurred during a demonstration in which between 30,000 and 50,000 people participated. It was one of many nationwide union protests against planned austerity measures.

Belgium is burdened by substantial public debt and a persistent budget deficit. Led by Prime Minister Bart De Wever, the center-right government aims to find €10 billion ($11.2 billion) in savings by the end of its term in 2029.

Rather than imposing cuts in areas like health and hiking VAT, trade unions are pushing for the government to increase taxes on the highest earners.

## Eritrean troops in Ethiopia raise fears of a regional war

https://p.dw.com/p/5O3VxENDF patrol on October 7: The airport of Mekelle is under Ethiopian government control. But witnesses have reported Eritrean troops towards Tigray's northern border.Image: REUTERSAdvertisementVarious sources have confirmed the presence of Eritrean armed forces in Ethiopia. An AFP report quoted a foreign security source, speaking on condition of anonymity, as estimating that 5,000 to 8,000 Eritrean soldiers were present in the northenmost state of Ethiopia, Tigray.

According to Reuters, the government of Ethiopia has written to the UN Security Council, calling for the immediate withdrawal of Eritrean troops from its territory.

In the letter, dated ‌Oct. 8, Ethiopian ‌Foreign Minister Gedion Timothewos said the presence of Eritrean troops in Ethiopia left "no option but to defend itself against Eritrea,"

in a post on X on October 7, Eritrean Minister of Information Yemane Meskel had accused Ethiopia of "incessantly beating the drums of war."

Two days later, he wrote in Tigrinya, a working language of Eritrea and the main language of Tigray, that "the sovereign state of Eritrea is bound by blood and law." adding "woe to those who touch it."

Some experts have suggested that Ethiopia may have intentionally triggered the escalation. Just last week, it expelled diplomats from Egypt and Eritrea, causing the latter to sever diplomatic ties altogether.

On Monday, when Abiy Ahmed was sworn in for another five years as Ethiopian prime minister, he stated that access to the sea was a priority. The statement fueled fresh speculation that he intends to take Eritrea's strategic port of Assab by force and prompted fierce reactions from the Eritrean side.

While the Ethiopian National Defence Forces (ENDF), aided by the Tigray Peace Force milita, have made significant advances and notably recaptured the Tigray federal state capital of Mekelle, opposition to Ethiopia has found new formats for collaboration.

One is the Tsimdo movement, named after a Tigrinya term that loosely translates as "engagement."

"The Tsimdo concept initially surfaced through cross-border peace initiatives involving activists, religious figures, and local communities in the Tigray and Eritrea border villages in 2025," Dr. Jalale Getachew Birru, senior East Africa analyst with the US-based conflict monitor ACLED, told DW.

It has since undergone several reconfigurations and now encompasses diverse groups within Ethiopia, mostly defined along ethnic lines and with long-standing grievances against the federal government.

The Ethiopian Peoples' Forces Alliance for Survival, or simply Alliance for Survival, that was proclaimed in September, stems from this movement. According to Jalale, the alliance aims at removing the current government and initiating a constitutional dialogue.

The new alliance says a lot about the long history of unresolved conflict in Ethiopia, said Abdurahman Sayed, an analyst at the Horn of Africa Centre for Regional Integration.

"With the Oromo Liberation Army, it's been going on for decades, with the Amhara, it's been going on for the last three years, at least. With the Tigrayans, the Pretoria agreement about three years ago was just a cessation of hostility that would have led to some kind of sustainable peace by addressing the root causes of the conflict — but it failed to do that, so they're back to the conflict situation now."

However, of the seven groups in the alliance, ACLED data shows that only three were involved in recent fighting: The Tigrayan Defence Forces (TDF), the Amhara Fano National Movement (AFNM), and a fairly recently formed ethnic Afar group called Ugugumo.

The success of the Alliance for Survival would depend in large part on the support they get through external actors, according to ACLED. Those could include neighboring Sudan, Egypt, Eritrea, and Somalia.

"All these four countries have grievances against Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's government," Sayed said.

"The Egyptians have issues with the Nile River and how it should be administered, including the dams that are already there and those planned to be built around the Nile River. The Sudanese also have their own concerns that Ethiopians are supporting the [Rapid Support Forces] militia, which is fighting the Sudanese army."

In the case of Somalia, the issue of sea access has sparked tensions. Landlocked Ethiopia has negotiated access to the port of Berbera in Somaliland . In 2024, it signed a memorandum of understanding with the breakaway region, causing uproar in Somalia, which considers the deal a violation of its national sovereignty. Turkish mediation was needed to calm things down.

"So all those neighboring countries have their own concerns and they feel this threat that is coming from Ethiopia needs to be tackled collectively."

On October 4, the leaders of the four countries issued a joint declarationthat didn't mention Ethiopia but seemed to send a strong signal. The leaders stressed the importance of international law, "particularly respect for the sovereignty, independence and territorial integrity of the states of the region."

And they maintained that "the governance of the Red Sea is the exclusive responsibility of the littoral states."

Although the leaders of Sudan, Egypt, Eritrea and Somalia vowed to promote "peaceful solutions to conflicts," the reality on the ground may prove otherwise.

## Macron and Steinmeier struggle to revive Franco-German ties

https://p.dw.com/p/5O4RXPresidents Steinmeier (l) and Macron and their wives met in Paris for a farewellImage: Bernd von Jutrczenka/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementThe German delegation passed through Paris without being hindered by the   student strikes taking place on the streets of Paris. The motorcades moved through the French capital without disruption. Around the Arc de Triomphe and the Champs-Elysees, however, traffic was at a standstill for hours. Due to the high-profile visit, the streets were cordoned off over a wide area.

Emmanuel Macron had decided on a special gesture to send off Frank-Walter Steinmeier, his German guest: The military honors took place under the Arc de Triomphe. And so, early in the morning, the German national anthem rang out in the heart of Paris — amid sunshine and bitter cold.

It was a generous gesture of friendship and symbolism, yet it seemed to be from a bygone era. In both countries, populists on the political fringes are gaining support. In Germany, the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) ranks first in nationwide polls.  In France, Emmanuel Macron could soon be succeeded by either Jean-Luc Melenchon of the far left or Marine Le Pen of the far right.

Added to this is an economic crisis that threatens prosperity. Unresolved issues of social justice are driving younger generations into the streets.  At the same time, social media is increasingly becoming a tool of disinformation and "accelerating doubts about democracy," Macron said in Paris.

While the AfD may soon form its first state government in Saxony-Anhalt, President Steinmeier remained confident. He insisted that Germany would continue to "play a leading role in Europe" in the future and that Germans would remain good Europeans. But he did not say where he drew this certainty from. Questions from journalists were not permitted at the joint press conference at the Elysee Palace.

Political scientist Jacob Ross isn't surprised by this. Both Macron and Steinmeier lack a "genuine agenda for Germany, France, and Europe." Both represent political systems that "seek to respond to 21st-century crises with 20th-century solutions," said the expert on German-French relations at the German Council on Foreign Relations (DGAP).

Even though Steinmeier and Macron have different political styles, Ross sees parallels — especially where their policies have failed. One example of this is strategic foreign policy issues, such as dealing with Russia. "For a long time, they pursued a policy based on integrating Russia into Europe, and then both were overwhelmed by the events of 2022 and had to adjust their course," said Ross. Since then, both have merely reacted to current affairs, according to the political scientist.

Emmanuel Macron has made some bold moves for Europe, but without support from Berlin, says Ross. Germany's political leadership has long reacted with caution to proposals from Paris.

A joint initiative that both countries recently promoted to the European Commission is aimed at combating unfair competition. Countries that gain an advantage through unfair practices should face strict European measures.

Macron joined German Chancellor Friedrich Merz in speaking out in favor of this. Although China was not explicitly named, the initiative is seen to be primarily aimed at that country. Beijing uses enormous subsidies to support its companies, whose products flood the European market, while European firms continue to face barriers when trying to access the Chinese market.

Germany and France have also long sought greater cooperation in the defense sector. Yet major joint defense projects are making little progress. When the planned joint fighter jet, the FCAS, fell through in June, officials emphasized ongoing cooperation on the future MGCS main battle tank. And yet differences on this became apparent during the state visit.

As German Defense Minister Boris Pistorius arrived in Paris, French Chief of the General Staff Fabien Mandon stated during a hearing in the French National Assembly that Germany had decided to withdraw from the tank project. A day later, Mandon clarified that he had expressed himself ambiguously. But some observers believe that such misunderstandings are a sign of dissatisfaction on both sides — below the political level — regarding cooperation with the other partner.

It is this political level that often ends up having to set the record straight in such cases. This time was no exception: Pistorius firmly refuted the claim that the project was on the verge of being scrapped. "The MGCS project is not over; it is not dead; it has not been terminated by anyone — quite the contrary. It is a core project of Franco-German cooperation  on land systems," said the German defense minister.

The focus is primarily on digital information processing in combat, as well as unmanned and automated systems. Germany and France would continue their cooperation, Pistorius insisted, and MGCS is an important future project for both countries and their armed forces.

On Friday, the presidents of France and Germany visited several facilities near Bordeaux that are closely linked to space exploration and nuclear deterrence. These include ArianeGroup, a Franco-German company that, among other things, manufactures the nuclear-capable M51 missiles for French nuclear submarines. The itinerary also included visits to startups that are looking far into the future.

It was unofficially reported during the visit that this focus of the trip was a personal priority for Macron. The French president has long spoken of a European dimension to France's nuclear deterrent. Considering announcements from the US saying it would reduce its commitment to Europe, his views are gaining increasing traction.

As early as 2020, Macron proposed a strategic dialogue on the role of French nuclear weapons in European security. Several countries, including Germany, have since expressed interest. Now, further steps are being considered. The focus is on what Macron once called "forward deterrence," such as joint military exercises and visits to strategically important locations. A permanent deployment of French nuclear weapons in other countries remains off the table. ## Author or artificial intelligence? Literature in crisis

https://p.dw.com/p/5O4R8The pavilion of the Guest of Honor, the Czech Republic, at the 2026 Frankfurt Book FairImage: Hannes P. Albert/AFPAdvertisementIt's the hot-button topic at the Frankfurt Book Fair: How profoundly will artificial intelligence (AI) transform the book industry, and how can this transformation be regulated?

Given the warnings about the technology's long-term risks to humanity as a whole, such debates might appear rather narrow in scope. In reality, however, they concern very concrete issues — copyright protection, the power of tech giants and the future of creativity. Many agree that the political measures taken don't go far enough.

This year, however, another aspect has entered the debate: Can we actually recognize when a text has been written with the help of artificial intelligence, and if so, can this be proven?

These are some of the questions raised by the current scandal surrounding Haitian-Canadian author Thelyson Orelien and his debut novel, "C'etait ca ou mourir," (This title means "It Was Either That or Die" in English but an English translation has not yet been published), which was published in Canada in the spring.

The book about global migration was universally acclaimed, with translation rights sold in more than 20 languages. Soon after the book was published in France, it was longlisted for the prestigious Prix Goncourt.

But then, all of a sudden, allegations emerged that the book had been written almost entirely using AI. Orelien, a rising literary star, found himself branded a fraud despite denying all the allegations. His nomination for the Goncourt was withdrawn.

So, who should we believe? An author or an AI that claims to have identified AI-generated writing?

"We will have to learn to live with doubt," says Hannes Bajohr, a professor of German literature at the University of California, Berkeley.

He has spent years examining how AI is changing both writing and reading. His prediction: If the origins of texts can no longer be reliably established, we will have to evaluate them differently. What matters will be their quality alone, rather than whether AI was used in the writing process.

Nina George, a best-selling author and the honorary president of the European Writers' Council, is unwilling to accept this.

At the Frankfurt Book Fair, she was awarded the Friedrich Perthes Prize in recognition of her commitment to issues such as AI and copyright reform.

George has addressed the European Parliament and the German Bundestag, speaking and writing tirelessly about the dangers of AI models. It is perverse, she says, that an author should suddenly have to prove that their writing is the creation of a human. The focus of her campaigns are the tech corporations' self-serving mentality and copyright protection.

US author and artist Luke Stoffel deliberately used AI in his memoir, "How To Win One Million Dollars and Shit Glitter," and was open about doing so.

"I'm dyslexic," he said in a video interview. "I've always written and made up stories, but I never dared to take them any further."

It was only with the help of AI, he said, that he was able to fulfill his dream. The technology drew his attention to weaknesses in his writing and suggested a marketing strategy that actually worked.

In May 2026, he received an award from the Independent Book Publishers Association (IBPA) for the book.

For South African author Coral Hart, AI is actually the foundation of her business model. Publishing under various pseudonyms, each year she releases more than 200 different novels, spanning the genres of romance, thriller and mystery.

She sees herself as an ideas person, as well as an editor and publisher. Although none of her books has yet become a bestseller, the business is profitable, she says, and each book reaches more than 5,000 people according to her.

Stoffel considers the entire debate hypocritical and points out that many publishers have been working with AI tools for some time. He's certain that "right now, a million-copy bestseller is being printed that was created with the help of AI. The author won't admit it, and then there'll be a scandal."

It is entirely possible that the scandal surrounding Thelyson Orelien is precisely that story. But we may never know for sure.

For now, the battle against the tech giants continues, some of which are themselves increasingly concerned about losing control.

At the very start of the Frankfurt fair, the German Publishers and Booksellers Association issued an appeal to policymakers in Berlin and Brussels, calling for stricter regulation of artificial intelligence, which they said is more urgent than ever. More than 140 publishers have signed it.

But for now, it remains just an appeal. And the question of what authors themselves should be permitted to do with AI will undoubtedly continue to fuel debates in the future.

## Bosnia and Herzegovina will soon have a new Grand Mufti

https://p.dw.com/p/5O0jyThe headquarters of the Islamic Community of Bosnia and Herzegovina are in SarajevoImage: Samir Huseinović/DWAdvertisementThe reis-ul-ulema or Grand Mufti of Bosnia and Herzegovina, the head of the country's Islamic community, represents the interests of Muslims before the state, expresses himself on political, moral and social issues and defends the independence of the Islamic Community in Bosnia and Herzegovina against attempts at foreign influence.

Three candidates are competing to become his successor: Salem Dedovic, the Mufti of Mostar, Nedzad Grabus, the Mufti of Sarajevo, and Mensur Malkic, the head of the Directorate for Religious Affairs of the Islamic Community in Bosnia and Herzegovina and the Grand Mufti's envoy.

Grabus, who has the most international experience as the former Grand Mufti of Slovenia, says he would like to strengthen the community in times of globalization and secularization.

"No other Islamic personality in Europe holds a position like the Reis-ul-ulema in Sarajevo," said Imam Benjamin Idriz, the head of the Islamic Community in the Bavarian city of Penzberg and the chair of the Munich Forum for Islam. "That's why the importance of this election extends far beyond the country, to Muslims and non-Muslims alike, across Europe."

"Bosniak" is the name Bosnian Muslims use for themselves. With around 100 communities across the country, the IGBD is one of Germany's smaller Muslim associations.

Muslims have lived in Bosnia and Herzegovina for centuries. Islam was introduced to the population when to Ottoman Empire spread through southeastern Europe. The organizational structure of the Islamic Communities was established after the Berlin Congress in 1878, which, among other things, placed Bosnia and Herzegovina under Austro-Hungarian rule.

Following the example of Christian churches, the Austro-Hungarian rulers soon officially recognized Islam as a religious community and in 1882, they founded the Council of Ulema and appointed the first Reis-ul-ulema as president.

This kind of Islamic self-organization could become a model for Muslims across Europe. The Islamic Community of Bosnia and Herzegovina is independent from the state and is financed with donations, membership fees and zakat, a form of charity given by Muslims.

The Islamic Community has its own sort of parliament, the Sabor, and a Constitutional Court that ensures the community's actions are in line with the constitution of the country in which it operates. The Riyaset is the community's highest executive body, responsible for implementing decisions. Its headquarters are in Sarajevo.

In the 150 years since its founding, the Islamic Community of Bosnia and Herzegovina has survived numerous political upheavals and repeatedly adapted to new circumstances. After the Ottoman Empire came the Habsburg monarchy and Austro-Hungarian rule, then the Kingdom of Yugoslavia and the Republic of Yugoslavia under Josip Broz Tito. Following Tito's death, the Bosnian War for independence ended in 1995 with the Dayton Accords.

Today, Bosnia and Herzegovina is a secular state with a democratic constitutional order. Time and again, the Islamic Community has clearly distanced itself from radical tendencies. It has a history of cooperation and coexistence with Jewish and Christian communities.

This has also left an impression on the roughly 400,000 Bosnian Muslims living in Germany.

"In the process of institutionalizing Islam in Germany, the experience of the Bosniaks' Islamic Community should be taken more strongly into account," said Esnaf Begic, a former imam and a lecturer at the Institute for Islamic Theology at Osnabrück University. "We are autochthonous European Muslims and to a certain extent, we share more similarities with Germans than with Muslims from Afghanistan, Iran or Saudi Arabia."

Mrakovic, head of the IGBD, agrees that Bosnia's experience with organizing religious life in a democratic and pluralistic society could also be useful to other Muslim communities. "It's not about telling other people how to live their Islamic faith but about learning from our own experiences: how we preserve our religious identity while remaining a natural part of European, democratic society.

The new head of Bosnian Muslims will be assuming his role in challenging times. Statehood in Bosnia and Herzegovina remains fragile, and foreign actors are vying for more influence. "Attempts to exert more influence aren't only coming from Ankara or Riyah, but also from Washington, Berlin or Brussels. They, too are, trying to act upon the Reis-ul-ulema in their own interest," Begic said.

In Europe, anti-Muslim racism and Islamophobia have reached levels that are becoming increasingly frightening for Muslims. Right-wing populists are on the rise, and extremist parties are attracting more and more supporters bent on denying Muslims the right to participate in society.

Given the circumstances, the new Reis-ul-ulema will be expected to employ the full democratic and historical legitimacy of his office to actively engage in debates about Islam in Europe.

This is what Idriz, the imam from Penzberg, hopes for, too. He believes the new Reis-ul-ulema could inspire more confidence not only in Bosniaks, but in all European Muslims, while signaling to majority societies that Islam is a part of Europe, and that it affirms and embraces values such as the rule of law, gender equality and freedom of expression. "In the future, the Reis-ul-ulema should lay an even stronger emphasis on this," Idriz said.

## Finland, the world&apos;s happiest nation, faces a glum winter

https://p.dw.com/p/5Nl7MFinland's policymakers will spend the winter deciding where the ax should fallImage: Ardan Fuessmann/IMAGOAdvertisementThere's an uneasy mood in Finland, the country that's ranked the world's happiest for nine consecutive years. A colder-than-usual winter is on the way and so is the toughest austerity budget in years.

With an election due in April, the argument is no longer how much to cut, but which services, benefits and pensions will face the biggest cuts.

Finland's debt and deficit levels are in their worst state since the 1990s, when a banking crash and the collapse of the Soviet Union — a key export partner — pushed the Nordic nation into its own Great Depression.

Last month, Finland's national statistical institution confirmed that the national debt had reached 90.3% of gross domestic product (GDP) in the second quarter. Before the pandemic, that figure was around 65%.

Trouble to the east is, again, part of the reason. Finland ramped up defense spending after joining NATO a year after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Helsinki, which used to rely on Moscow for about a third of its energy, also faced much higher costs as it transitioned to other suppliers.

The Finnish government has spent more than it earns since the 2008/9 global financial crisis, partly due to an aging population and sluggish economic growth. Last month, the State Treasury projected the fiscal deficit would reach 4.2% of GDP in 2026.

Brussels has piled pressure on Helsinki to bring down the borrowing gap, as European Union rules require member states to stay below 3% of GDP. In January, the European Council opened an excessive deficit procedure and gave Finland until the end of 2028 to reach the desired target.

Prime Minister Petteri Orpo's government, which took office in June 2023, said it would aim to save about €9 billion ($10.1 billion) during this parliamentary term. But whoever wins the next election will have to cut much deeper, economists warn.

"The amounts currently estimated range from €8 to €11 billion," Jarkko Kivisto, an advisor to the Bank of Finland's monetary policy and research department, told DW.

Earlier this year, all but one political party agreed to support a debt brake, where the next government commits to tightening the country's deficit to about 2% of GDP by 2031.

To date, Orpo's center-right National Coalition is the only party to promise a further €9 billion in cuts if he wins a second term. As well as further savings from health, social care and welfare, Orpo also plans to target workplace pensions, without reducing current payouts.

While the Social Democrats plan to split the reforms between spending cuts and tax hikes, Orpo plans to achieve his target without raising taxes — meaning public services will bear the brunt.

Kivisto, however, cautioned: "The deficit is so large that it would need a package that includes tax increases and expenditure cuts."

While Finland's at-risk-of-poverty rate is lower than the EU average, unemployment is higher than anywhere else in the bloc. In August, Finland's jobless total stood at 10.3%, just above Spain's 10%, according to Eurostat, the bloc's statistics agency. Youth unemployment reached 23.3%, against an EU average of 15.4%.

With household spending already falling, Lauri Holappa, director of the Finnish Centre for New Economic Analysis, warned that a new round of austerity would be a hard blow for the domestic economy.

"More than a quarter of the population works in the public sector, so if those workers face the threat of being sacked, the savings rate will rise and private spending will drop again," Holappa told DW.

Successive pressures ranging from the COVID-19 pandemic, higher interest rates and the global financial impact of two current wars have hurt efforts to cut the growing gap between income and borrowing. The 2027 budget proposal still counts on a spending gap of €12.4 billion.

Before the Ukraine war, Finland had already committed to buying 64 F-35A fighter jets from the United States, in a deal worth about €8.4 billion.

During more than four years of Russia's war in Ukraine, the Nordic nation raised military spending from $4.5 billion to over $8 billion per year, according to the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI).

In April, the government said it would further increase defense spending to 3.2% of GDP, close to NATO's 3.5% target.

With oil prices remaining close to $100 a barrel due to the Iran war, the government has so far resisted pressure to increase fuel subsidies. That's despite the Bank of Finland recording a 50% rise in the cost of imported energy in the early months of the Iran war.

While Finland’s shift to renewables, along with the opening of the Olkiluoto 3 nuclear power plant in 2023, has helped avoid a worse energy crisis, the central bank warned this summer that sustained high oil and gas prices would mean "lower economic growth and higher inflation" next year.

On another positive note, parts of Finland's export sector are booming, thanks to strong metal and shipbuilding orders, which Holappa described as strong as "the boom years of Nokia," when the once market-leading phone maker carried Finnish economic growth.

"There is a possibility that the export sector can steer us out of this recession," he told DW, as long as demand from the rest of Europe remains strong.

Despite the multiple roadblocks to bringing down the deficit, market reaction to Finland's debt woes has, so far, been muted. Rating agencies have kept the country's outlook stable, apart from S&P, which turned negative in April without cutting the rating.

On October 6, the premium the Finnish government pays over Germany to borrow for 10 years was 38 basis points. That compares to 128 for France and 107 for Italy. In other words, investors still treat Finnish debt as among the safest in Europe.

That is, as long as the next shock is not another extended oil-price spike, a fresh escalation from Russia, or a Europe-wide debt crisis centered on France.

Next March, Finland could still be named the planet's happiest nation for the 10th year in a row. How long the smiles last, once the bill for decades of borrowing arrives a month later, is anyone's guess.
