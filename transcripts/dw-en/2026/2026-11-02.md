## France: Petition to ban TV channel gets 500,000 signatures

https://p.dw.com/p/5OyGVPrefer DW on Google'The extreme-right kills! CNews culpable!' reads this placard during a tribute to a man shot dead near Paris last monthImage: Sameer Al-Doumy/AFPAdvertisementA petition urging the French government to ban a television news channel known for its hard line on immigration and crime has accrued more than 500,000 signatures amid accusations that its sensationalist coverage contributed to the racist killing of a Black man.

The petition to shut down CNews was launched in June, but support spiked after a Frenchman of Congolese origin was killed in a racially motivated attack in the Paris suburbs on October 23.

A 39-year-old man has been charged with murder and attempted murder over the incident in the commuter towns of Trappes and La Verriere west of Paris in which he said he was "targeting individuals who appeared to be Muslim, African, or North African," a prosecutor has said.

French media have reported that the suspect was an avid consumer of CNews, which has been described as a French version of Fox News and criticized for giving too much airtime to anti-immigrant rhetoric.

The petition to "remove CNews from French broadcasting" argues that the channel, which is owned ​by conservative French billionaire Vincent Bollore, is not an ​information channel but a"propaganda machine."

Critics have claimed that CNews' broadcasting increasingly mimics lines taken by ​Le Pen's far-right National Rally (RN), which is leading in polls ahead of next year's presidential election.

Le Pen has promised to privatize public broadcasting in France, a policy from which CNews would stand to benefit.

At a rally in the northern Paris suburb of Aubervilliers last week, protesters held up posters reading slogans such as "CNews kills."

During nationwide cost-of-living protests on October 17, protesters threw projectiles and broke windows at the channel's Paris headquarters.

Meanwhile, two Green lawmakers have urged France's lower house of parliament to withdraw accreditation for CNews to protect "democracy" from "racist hatred."

Earlier this year, France expelled Russian news pundit Xenia Fedorova, who had been accused of peddling Kremlin propaganda on CNews.

The channel has also been accused of broadcasting racist comments about Bally Bagayoko, a Frenchman of Malian descent who is the mayor of Paris' largest suburb, Saint-Denis, and who has gained prominence for his role in defending students in his constituency against police violence during recent protests.

CNews, which has previously denied allegations it promotes far-right views and racist tropes, saying it defends freedom of expression, told the French AFP news agency it would not comment on a "political petition."

At its launch in 2017, CNews had just a 1% market share but last year became France's most-watched free-to-air news channel.

Under National Assembly rules, a ⁠public ​debate can be organized in parliament ​if a petition attracts at least 500,000 signatures.

However, a parliamentary commission must ​ first review the petition and decide whether a debate can take place.

## What might Trump have planned for Iran following the midterm election?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OxtwPrefer DW on GoogleThe Reuters news agency cited three senior Iranian officials and an additional source as saying that Iran has deliberately prolonged talks to weaken Trump politicallyImage: Brendan Smialowski/AFPAdvertisementThe US Republican Party is entering the midterm elections with Donald Trump's administration seemingly unable to either win or diplomatically resolve the Iran war. If the Republicans lose control of the US Congress, the administration will have to navigate an entirely different political landscape for the rest of Trump's second term, but the course of the war is not decided just in Washington — or in Tehran.

Seeking to identify key factors that could tip the balance towards war or peace in Iran, DW spoke with Omid Memarian of DAWN, a Washington-based think tank focused on reforming the US's Middle East policy; the Finland-based political scientist Leila Morovati; and Babak Rezaeedaryakenari, a professor of international relations at Leiden University in the Netherlands.

A Democratic victory would push Trump to focus more on domestic issues and would likely expose the financial costs of the war in the US Congress, Memarian told DW. However, Trump could continue limited strikes on Iran without formally declaring war.

With Trump, Memarian said, Iran is engaged in a "big gamble with someone who does not behave like a conventional politician." This increases the risk of renewed attacks.

Rezaeedaryakenari made a similar point: "The president has broad authority and freedom of action, especially in foreign policy and the conduct of military operations. A Republican defeat, even with increased pressure from Congress, doesn't necessarily stop new attacks."

Israel's October 27 election ended in deadlock. Neither the coalition around incumbent Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, nor the camp supporting former IDF chief Gadi Eisenkot managed to secure an outright majority.

Memarian said the political uncertainty could push Netanyahu to act on major decisions while he still wields power — making the transition period the "most fragile and dangerous" for renewed escalation.

A change of government in Israel would not fundamentally alter Israel's Iran policy, Memarian said. Netanyahu's main rival, former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, "may be more deferential to America" and coordinate more closely with Washington. But ties with Trump are personal, and Eisenkot "certainly won't be like Netanyahu, who could sell any plan or scenario to Trump."

Rezaeedaryakenari said Israel's security concerns about Iran "are not limited to Netanyahu himself." A new government could change the timing of a confrontation and coordination with Washington, but the security establishment and ruling coalition would also shape the risk of an attack, he said.

Iran's nuclear program remains the biggest open issue between the governments, with the US rejecting the Iranian narrative that the program is meant only for civilian use.

But the two sides are also at odds on how the talks themselves should be set up, Memarian said.

"The US wants negotiations to start not only with a focus on Iran's nuclear program, but with concessions right at the outset," he said.

Iran seeks the lifting of sanctions and the release of frozen assets before nuclear talks begin. If the sides don't move closer, Memarian warns, the odds of a renewed war rise.

Until the sides agree on an agenda, Memarian said, they are likely to keep "climbing the ladder of tension."

Morovati, a Finland-based political analyst, said the nuclear program was "the crux" of the standoff. Iran's government sees it as a survival guarantee and bargaining chip, she said. In turn, Washington fears that a nuclear Iran would upend its security calculations and long-term strategy for the Middle East and beyond, which is why it insists on putting the issue on the table first.

Before the United States and Israel launched the war in late February, about 20% of the world's oil passed through the Strait of Hormuz.  Iran has since drastically cut on the energy exports through the naval chokepoint, using at as leverage on US and Israel.

But, Memarian said, Iran's restrictions in the strait won't trigger escalation on their own.

Should US officials decide that a broader attack is needed to change Iran's calculations, any incident could serve as a pretext, Memarian said. If Washington considers the risks too high, it may opt for an economic blockade while keeping negotiations on the table. In that scenario, "even occasional tensions in the Strait of Hormuz won't pull America into war," according to the US-based analyst.

The Houthi rebels in Yemen have also been targeting energy exports on the Red Sea — including attacks on Saudi cities and infrastructure. But Memarian notes the Yemeni rebels' roots and struggle are "entirely domestic and organic" despite their ideological closeness to Tehran.

Memarian said the Houthis made "pragmatic calculations" and their actions did not necessarily translate into an escalation between Iran and the United States.

Rezaeedaryakenari offered a slightly different assessment. Houthi attacks on shipping and on countries in the region, he says, "can both widen the war and accelerate coalition-building against the Islamic Republic." Countries that don't want war with Tehran "may turn to closer military cooperation to protect their trade and security." That doesn't necessarily mean entering the war directly, he adds, "but it could narrow the Islamic Republic's room for maneuver."

With direct talks between the governments of Iran and the United States stalled, the world turned its attention to Qatar, Oman, Pakistan and Saudi Arabia. While each nation plays a different role in the crisis, they all have a direct stake in ending the war.

"They are close to the US, but also want to maintain ties with Iran and build regional alliances to prevent future conflicts," Memarian said.

Morovati said the failure of the United States to support its Arab allies during the war could eventually push them to reconsider their policies and turn to the US's rivals.

During the campaign, Trump said he expected a deal with Iran soon after the midterm elections. Meanwhile, the Reuters news agency cited three senior Iranian officials and an additional source as saying that Tehran has deliberately prolonged talks to weaken Trump politically.

Memarian said Iran's leaders believed that a Republican defeat would increase pressure on Trump to end the war. But Trump will remain the key foreign policy decision-maker, and congressional pressure is unlikely to play a deciding role.

He said the present moment offers the "best opportunity" to end the standoff before Trump leaves office in January 2029.

"Iran's leaders need to think about their endgame," Memarian said, warning that Trump could launch a massive attack, inflict serious damage and then declare victory.

## Ice hockey officials considering options after CAS order to reinstate Russia

https://p.dw.com/p/5Oxo3Prefer DW on GoogleRussian players could soon again be seen at IIHF-sanctioned international ice hockey tournamentsImage: Kirill Kukhmar/ITAR-TASS/IMAGOAdvertisementThe president of the International Ice Hockey Federation (IIHF), Franz Reindl, announced shortly after the decision by the Court of Arbitration for Sport (CAS) on Friday, that he had called a meeting of its main decision-making body for this Monday and Tuesday.

The Lausanne-based court had ruled that the IIHF must allow Russian national teams to take part in ice hockey world championships again. They had been barred from doing so since shortly after Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022.

"Our task now is to implement this ruling responsibly," said Germany's Reindl, who was elected IIHF president just last month.

"Several championships are only weeks away, and changes to participation affect many people; players, teams, organizing committees and host cities. We will work closely with all of them to find practical solutions."

The first tournament the CAS decision applies to is the World Juniors, to be held beginning on December 26 in Canada. Just how it will affect the tournament is not clear. Hockey Canada said it was in contact with the IIHF and had briefed Prime Minister Mark Carney's government on the decision.

Foreign Affairs Minister Anita Anand told public broadcaster CBC that she is "not comfortable" with allowing a Russian team to take part in the tournament in Alberta.

"This is a very unwelcome decision given the geopolitical situation, given the actions of Russia," she said. "All options are on the table, and I will say, again, I greet this decision with great disappointment."

"Canada will maintain restrictions on the participation of Russian athletes at events hosted in Canada that receive funding from the Government of Canada and explore further options to ensure that the Russian government is held accountable," he said in a statement.

Germany is also considering its options as it prepares to host the Men's Ice Hockey World Championship in Gelsenkirchen, Düsseldorf and Mannheim next May.

"The German government takes a very critical view of the decision by the Court of Arbitration for Sport, a government spokesman said on Sunday, according to the DPA news agency.

"The German government will discuss possible consequences of the ruling, including for the 2027 Ice Hockey World Championship in Germany, with the German Ice Hockey Federation (DEB) promptly."

The DEB's chief executive, Frank Lutz, is to travel to Berlin to discuss the matter with government officials this week. Speaking on broadcaster MagentaSport on the weekend, he conceded that surrendering Germany's hosting rights for the lucrative tournament was a possibility.

"I do not want to paint a black picture. I do not want to call the World Championship into question either. But of course, we at the DEB also have to consider the option of handing back this championship," he said.

Several other national ice hockey federations have expressed disappointment at the CAS decision, including those of Ukraine, Sweden, Finland, and Czechia.

The International Olympic Committee opened the door for Russian athletes to return to international competition when it lifted its ban of the Russian Olympic Committee in July. However, the IIHF had already decided to continue to exclude Russian teams for the 2026-27 season – over security concerns. The CAS decision – handed down after an appeal by Russia – found this reasoning was insufficiently proven.

"Russia is returning to world hockey. This place rightfully belongs to us, and discrimination in hockey has been brought to an end," Sports Minister Mikhail Degtyarev told state television channel Vesti.

A boycott of the World Juniors, Men's World Championships or other tournaments by individual nations appears to be a possibility.

Latvia is prohibited by law from competing against Russian teams. Finland has said individual players would be free to opt-out of tournaments that include Russia, while Swedish Ice Hockey Association chairman Anders Larsson, who is a member of the IIHF Council, told Swedish news agency TT that "a boycott is not on the table."

However, at the same time he also noted that "our position is that Russia cannot participate in international championships in the current situation."

There has been the suggestion, that having won this victory at the CAS, Russia's next focus could be on football, where its national and club teams remain excluded from international competitions.

Russian FA general secretary Maxim Mitrofanov told state news agency Tass that there "should be one rule for all."

World football's governing body FIFA allowed a Russia boys' team to play under its national flag in the inaugural Under-15 World Cup and Festival in Azerbaijan, which kicked off last week and ends on Saturday.

## LA communities kick out Big Oil

https://p.dw.com/p/5OxeFPrefer DW on GoogleLA residents fought urban oil drilling for years — it's now been banned by the cityImage: Gissela C./STAND-L.A.AdvertisementLos Angeles has become the largest city in the US to ban oil drilling, after years of lawsuits and campaigning by residents who raised the alarm over pollution and potential health risks from nearby wells .

"Front-line communities have pushed the city for a solution for this for over a decade," said local resident Wendy Miranda, who has campaigned for years with STAND-LA, a coalition of community groups working to end neighborhood oil drilling.

The ordinance, approved by the LA City Council on October 28, puts an immediate stop to oil extraction and bans the redrilling or deepening of wells. Existing operations must be phased out within city limits over the next 20 years.

Though LA City is only part of greater Los Angeles, the move affects hundreds of active wells and thousands of inactive ones, many located in working class communities of color.

"In South LA, working-class families have had corporations drilling across the street from their homes," said council president Marqueece Harris-Dawson in a statement. "No one should have to live near an oil drilling site."

Rock Zierman, CEO of the California Independent Petroleum Association (CIPA), told DW, "CIPA believes this ordinance constitutes an unlawful taking of private property."

LA follows much smaller Culver City, also in California, in moving against urban oil production, and begins the process of ending its long, complex relationship with fossil fuels.

California's oil industry expanded rapidly in the early 20th century, when the state produced roughly a quarter of the world's oil. Lots of that so-called black gold was around LA and as the city grew, homes, schools and businesses were built around existing oil infrastructure.

Today, wells can be found beneath residential areas, close to playgrounds, along the coast and in some of the city's most densely populated and largely working-class neighborhoods. Some have been permanently sealed or plugged for decades, while others remain active or are idle.

Under California law, an idle well is one that has not produced oil for two years. Unlike plugged wells, they can potentially return to production.

Residents living near the active and inactive wells report strong odors, headaches, nausea and breathing difficulties. Another concern is air quality. Oil wells can release toxic pollutants, including benzene and hydrogen sulfide, and research has linked proximity to oil extraction with higher rates of respiratory illness and reduced lung function.

But both active and idle wells can also leak methane — a potent greenhouse gas that contributes to climate change — while residents say occasional accidents or breakages send oil flowing down city streets.

"Frontline communities have been experiencing or have experienced for decades living near these toxic sites and having to pay for that with their health," said Miranda, a policy director with Esperanza Community Housing, a nonprofit providing affordable housing services.

Miranda told DW she and her mother both have asthma. The council vote "definitely represents hope," she added.

LA City Council initially approved an ordinance to phase out oil extraction back in 2022 after successful lobbying by residents like Wendy Miranda. But oil companies challenged the measure in court, with industry groups successfully arguing that the city lacked the legal authority to impose the ban.

California subsequently passed legislation giving local governments greater power to regulate and restrict oil and gas operations, putting the possibility of a phaseout back on the table.

In an emailed statement to DW, CIPA CEO Rock Zierman said energy companies were prepared to challenge the new ordinance.

Zierman added that it would make city residents more dependent on foreign oil from countries with lower environmental standards when they are "already struggling" with high gas prices.

"It sends the jobs, investment and revenue elsewhere while leaving Californians to pay the bill," said Zierman.

The relationship between local oil production and prices at the pump is not straightforward and is influenced by a number of factors, according to the California Energy Commission, the state's main energy policy agency. Those include the cost of crude oil, refining, transportation, distribution and local taxes.

Even though the US is the world's largest oil producer, it exports and imports crude oil, as well as refined petroleum products. Because oil is traded globally, US residents are exposed to the same price fluctuations as people elsewhere, no matter how much of the fossil fuel is produced domestically.

California's oil industry and the amount of oil it produces have been steadily declining since the 1980s. In the coastal lowlands known as the LA Basin, output is 43% lower than eight years ago, with the amount of oil extracted falling more rapidly than the number of open wells.

When wells stop producing, it is up to the oil operators to remove equipment and plug them. The state agency that regulates oil and gas, CalGEM, and the LA fire department oversee plugging operations, and also seal wells for which no-one takes responsibility.

But many have not yet been plugged — some 80% of idle wells in LA have been inactive for more than eight years, and about a third for over a century.

The cost of plugging a well also varies widely, with estimates ranging from hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars. California law requires operators to submit plugging plans or face annual fines that increase with the amount of time the well has been idle.

But those fines aren't always paid. According to CalGEM's own data, the agency collected just $1 million out of $36 million (€32 million) in fines in 2025.

A report by Carbon Tracker also found that if all future profits from oil companies operating in California went to decommissioning wells, the state would still face a shortfall of $7 billion. The cost would by default fall to taxpayers, according to Carbon Tracker.

Miranda told DW that the next step for community activists is to reduce the 20-year timeline and have the oil sector "pay for this cleanup."  For now, she is says both she and her mother are hopeful that future generations "can have better health and breathe cleaner air."

## Germany&apos;s chancellor called for Korean unity. Does Seoul want that?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OxSRPrefer DW on GoogleThe South Korea government officially still pursues reunification, but the younger generations' attitudes have shiftedImage: Kay Nietfeld/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementThe story of South and North Korea — two nations that share history, language and much of their cultural heritage — sounds familiar to Germans.

Their own country spent decades divided by opposing regimes until geopolitical winds shifted and allowed East and West Germany to reunite peacefully in 1991.

Just two years later, German Chancellor Helmut Kohl visited the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) on the Korean Peninsula and expressed hope that South and North Korea would also be reunited by the end of the decade.

That never materialized. Some 35 years since Germany achieved its dream of reunification, Seoul and Pyongyang are still bitterly divided by political systems, economy, and their geopolitical alliances.

Overlooking the DMZ, which separates the two Koreas, on Friday Chancellor Friedrich Merz could only repeat the hopes stated by Kohl in 1993.

"We Germans know how painful the division of a country is," Merz stated in a message shared on social media. "We also know that whoever stands firm in their values and has the courage for dialogue can tear down walls."

To analysts following the day-to-day inter-Korean politics, this sounds like a distant dream.

As the Koreas continue to eye each other warily across the DMZ, their dramatic differences in wealth and political freedom are clearly on display.

"The feeling has changed in the last couple of decades," Choo Jae-woo, a professor of foreign policy at Kyung Hee University, told DW. "Younger South Koreans just see the North as another country that happens to be next to us, is poor and threatening, and they have no sentimental attachment to it, like older generations do."

A study by Seoul National University's Institute for Peace and Unification Studies in September indicated that 39.1% of South Koreans believed reunification was impossible, the highest proportion since the survey was started some two decades ago.

Many were concerned about the vast amounts of money that it would take to build the North's infrastructure, including roads, hospitals, schools and all other requirements for a modern functioning society.

Officially, South Korea's government has not abandoned reunification as a national objective. Its constitution still calls for peaceful union based on freedom and democracy, but Choo points out recent governments are promoting the idea far less frequently than in the past.

"In December 2023, North Korean leader Kim Jong Un announced that the constitution was being changed and that peaceful reunification was no longer possible," Choo said. "Instead, South Korea would be seen as a hostile rival and that he no longer had any intention of bringing the two together."

The revised North Korean constitution was approved in March 2026 and Kim's regime has moved aggressively to scrub all references to reconciliation and national unity.

"I do not think the North can make it any clearer that it has no intention of getting any closer to South Korea," said Mason Richey, a professor of politics and international relations at Hankuk University of Foreign Studies in Seoul. He added that analysts, officials and ordinary Koreans in the South "roll their eyes" when the issue of Korean reunification crops up.

Beyond the lack of desire on both sides, there are a number of additional challenges that would need to be overcome, he said.

North Korea's regime wishes to protect its own power and prestige, which would inevitably be under threat if a democratic system were introduced for a unified nation.

Bridging the enormous gap in economic development would be a huge undertaking that would put immense pressure on the South, Richey said, even if outside assistance were forthcoming. And while a united Germany has been able to bring its regions to relatively comparable levels, it is worth noting that East Germany in 1990 was still substantially more developed than North Korea is today.

Professor Choo said reunification would not just be a domestic issue. The two nations have divided strategic and security loyalties, with the South allied to the US and the North tied to China and increasingly, Russia.

"Those superpowers would not want to give up their ally for reunification as it would be a major strategic issue for them, as well," Choo said. "The US would not want a united Korea that leaned towards China, and Beijing would resist reunification if it meant the US could have its military on the Chinese border in what is presently North Korea. https://p.dw.com/p/5OxApPrefer DW on GoogleIliana Yotova (center) addresses the press after winning the Bulgarian presidential election runoff on Sunday, garnering 57.6% of the voteImage: BGNESAdvertisement"We are choosing between war and peace," Bulgarian Prime Minister Rumen Radev told voters ahead of Sunday's presidential election runoff between incumbent President Iliana Yotova, who opposes military aid to Ukraine and favors dialogue with Russia, and former caretaker Prime Minister Andrey Gyurov, who is regarded as staunchly pro-European and pro-Ukrainian.

Now that the official results are in and it is clear that Iliana Yotova won the election decisively by 57.6% to Gyurov's 39.5%, Radev claims that Bulgarians have chosen peace.

When the presidential election campaign began in Bulgaria on September 25, there did not seem to be any doubt about the outcome: Iliana Yotova was well ahead in the polls on 44.5%. Gyurov, her main rival, trailed far behind on 26.1%.

Yotova was backed by both the prime minister and his ruling Progressive Bulgaria party, which won an absolute majority in the parliamentary election on April 19.

While Yotova got 34.8% of the vote in the first round, Gyurov was close behind on 31.95%, which meant that a runoff between the two was necessary.

While Yotova won the runoff convincingly, both the campaign and the outcome showed that the Bulgarian electorate is divided.

This division is both generational and geographical, explains Dimitar Ganev, sociologist and political scientist at the Trend market and electoral research agency.

"Younger voters — those aged between 18 and 40 — favor Gyurov. Those over 40 tend to prefer Yotova," Ganev told DW. "It also appears that Gyurov dominates in the capital, Sofia. In smaller regional cities, other towns and villages, Yotova dominates decisively," he said.

While Andrey Gyurov relied mainly on social media for his campaign, Yotova chose to speak mostly to supporters of Progressive Bulgaria in closed venues.

Dimitar Ganev, on the other hand, sees no threat to Bulgaria's commitment to the EU and NATO. "Over two-thirds of Bulgarians do not question the country's geopolitical orientation, and around two-thirds have a positive attitude towards the EU," he told DW.

But the positions of Radev and Yotova, which many view as ambivalent, mobilized both the pro-European camp and Eurosceptic voters, who, according to Ganev, make up around a third of the country's population.

## Will renewed conflict derail Ethiopia&apos;s economic growth?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OrSePrefer DW on GoogleService and financial sector growth has transformed Addis Ababa's skyline. Could the new conflict halt the boom?Image: Solomon Muche/DWAdvertisementEthiopia's economy is growing strongly. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) forecasts real growth of 9.2% for the 2025–26 fiscal year. But renewed fighting in the north, high reconstruction costs and climate-related crop problems are threatening the country's economic recovery.

London-based financial analyst Abdulmenan Mohammed warns of the consequences of further escalation. "If there is a major conflict [...] the current economic growth will not be sustainable," he told DW.

The war that raged in Tigray and other parts of Ethiopia between 2020 and 2022 inflicted extensive damage. Infrastructure was destroyed, businesses lost production capacity and agricultural land could no longer be cultivated in some areas.

A World Bank-backed damage and needs assessment puts the damage caused by several conflicts in Ethiopia between November 2020 and December 2021 at more than $22.6 billion (€20.2 billion). Economic losses amounted to around $6 billion. The estimated bill for reconstruction and economic recovery stands at approximately $19.7 billion, which covers the total funding needed, not annual costs or damage caused by the current escalation.

A reconstruction plan covered the period from 2023 to 2028. However, it remains uncertain whether the work can be fully funded and completed within that timeframe.

Financial analyst Abdulmenan Mohammed believes the economic consequences of the Tigray war were even more severe. In addition to the direct destruction, he points to lost economic output, high inflation and foreign currency shortages at the time. International trade conditions also deteriorated: in 2022, Ethiopia lost its benefits under the US African Growth and Opportunity Act (AGOA), which had allowed certain African exports to enter the US market duty-free.

Following the Pretoria peace agreement in November 2022, the economy began to recover. According to the IMF, it grew by 7.2% in 2022–23 and 8.1% in 2023–24. The organization puts growth at 9.2% for 2024–25 and forecasts a further 9.2% for 2025–26. Agriculture, mining and electricity generation are among the main drivers.

This does not contradict the economic damage caused by the war. Gross domestic product (GDP) measures the total economic output of a country. If major sectors outside conflict-affected areas continue to expand, the overall economy can grow even while individual regions are still suffering the consequences of violence.

However, high growth rates do not mean that the country's economic problems have been resolved. Reconstruction remains a major challenge, inflation continues to put pressure on households and the government has to service its debt. In late 2023, Ethiopia defaulted after missing an interest payment on a Eurobond. Reforms supported by an IMF program launched in 2024 are intended to address structural weaknesses in the economy.

A renewed escalation could put additional pressure on public finances. The government would have to spend more on military personnel, equipment and logistics. At the same time, economic activity and, consequently, tax revenues could decline in the affected regions.

"A substantial share of government resources would be tied up in military spending that would otherwise be available for infrastructure, education and social services," warned Abdulmenan Mohammed. The extent of this impact would depend on the scale and duration of any renewed conflict.

A prolonged war could also prompt businesses to postpone investments. International lenders and donors might become more cautious, while the government would need additional funds for security and humanitarian assistance. This could leave less money available for roads, energy infrastructure and other development projects.

The IMF forecasts an average inflation rate of 11.7% for 2025–26. Prices are therefore expected to continue rising, although at a slower pace than in previous years.

Abdulmenan Mohammed expects a further escalation to intensify inflationary pressures. "If the conflict escalates further, inflation is likely to rise," he said. To finance additional spending, the government might resort to more domestic borrowing. This could crowd out private businesses by making it harder for them to access credit and finance investments.

The military also needs foreign currency to purchase equipment from abroad. If this demand increases, fewer foreign exchange reserves could be available to private businesses for imports. This would put further pressure on the birr exchange rate and drive up the cost of imported goods, fuel and industrial inputs.

The extent of these effects on the BRICS member state would also depend on export earnings, monetary policy and developments in international markets. A new surge in inflation is therefore a risk, but not an inevitable consequence of the escalation. A combination of rising prices and uncertain incomes would be particularly difficult for poorer households.

Abdulmenan Mohammed sees agriculture, as well as the tourism and hospitality sectors, as particularly vulnerable. "Agriculture is especially exposed because much of the fighting takes place in rural areas where farmers work," he said.

Insecurity around farmland, disrupted transport routes and shortages of agricultural inputs can undermine production. At the same time, parts of the country are experiencing insufficient rainfall and poor harvest prospects. Failed harvests reduce farmers' incomes, while lower food supplies can push prices even higher.

Tourism is also highly sensitive to security risks. Travel advisories and reports of fighting can deter visitors, reducing revenues for hotels and tour operators.

When making investment decisions, international investors consider not only market size but also political stability, security and the reliability of state institutions. A further escalation could therefore delay investment decisions or prompt companies to consider alternative locations.

However, there is currently no concrete evidence that companies are already withdrawing investments from Ethiopia because of the fighting and relocating them to neighboring countries such as Kenya. Abdulmenan Mohammed also sees no demonstrable economic advantage for Kenya at this stage. Beyond security, factors such as tax administration, public services and governance also play a decisive role.

This aligns with analysis from nonprofit news organization Inside Climate News showing that the Democrats have dramatically reduced mentions of climate change in their press releases and campaign messaging.

"We've entered an interesting space where climate policy doesn't need to be called climate policy to be climate policy," Max Frankel, executive director of the SEEC Institute, a nonprofit that helps guide climate policy in Congress, told DW.

Most forecasts project Democrats winning the House and a narrow majority in the Senate in the November 3 election. But without control of the White House, passing any legislation will be difficult and almost any climate policy will have to be a bipartisan endeavor, say analysts.

Given that and the Democrats' messaging-shift to affordability over climate, few experts expect a fully resurrected Inflation Reduction Act. Introduced under the Democrats to create incentives for building clean energy projects, the legislation was largely decimated by President Trump's major tax law.

But Democrats may try to bring back some climate-friendly IRA provisions, particularly measures aimed at lowering household energy costs, analysts told DW. Those include incentives for home energy-efficiency upgrades and appliances like heat pumps to replace oil and gas heaters that typically help bring down household energy bills.

Politicians could also expand an IRA manufacturing tax credit for solar, batteries, and critical minerals that largely survived. One bipartisan bill proposes extending it to include nuclear fusion components.

"It's not going to be anything at the scale of the IRA," Adrian Deveny, founder of Climate Vision, a policy firm dedicated to decarbonization, told DW. "It's like singles and doubles as opposed to a home run, which is still worth doing."

In a rare area of agreement between the parties, some Democrats and Republicans are already backing a reform bill they're hoping to pass before a new Congress is sworn in January.

If enacted, the bill could make it significantly easier to build major infrastructure projects in the US, including long-distance transmission lines that carry electricity between regions but that currently take years to build because of complex permitting rules.

"The biggest beneficiary of building more transmission will be wind and solar power, because the best and cheapest way to generate wind and solar energy tends to be far away from cities," said David Spence, a political scientist at the University of Texas who researches the politics of energy regulation. "And so, they need transmission to get their product to market."

But environmental groups say the bill could also speed up the approval of fossil fuel projects.

If Democrats win big in the midterms there might be some "peeling off their support for that bill because they think they can do better in the next Congress," said Spence.

Major new federal climate legislation is a long shot before the 2028 presidential election. And afterwards it would likely require Democrats winning the White House, the Senate and the House. While no 2028 platforms have been released yet, policy experts expect a few ideas to feature prominently.

One is data centers. Opposition to the energy-guzzling infrastructure being built en masse to power the AI boom is mounting across the country. Half of Americans are now against data centers being built close to their homes, according to a pollfrom news outlet Politico.

"I'm sure every candidate is going to have a platform with a focus on data center constraints on some level, as a way to address affordability," Deveny, who led negotiations on the IRA, said. "And that's the entry point for climate policy."

There are several ideas currently circulating — including a proposal by Democrats Bernie Sanders and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez to pause data center construction — as well as ending tax breaks for data centers and imposing new taxes on their electricity use.

Democrats may also seek to restore the IRA's clean energy tax incentives. This year, more than 150 legislators co-sponsored the so-called Energy Bills Relief Act designed to reinstate IRA clean energy tax incentives, reform how transmission permits are granted, and make data centers pay for the grid upgrades they need. The bill is unlikely to pass anytime soon but offers a window into what Democrats might support.

"By far, the biggest consensus — to the extent there's anything remotely resembling a consensus — is restoring a lot of the clean energy tax incentives from the IRA," Deveny said. "Not everybody supports that but it is a highly supported idea amongst most congressional Democrats."

Support for other proposals include a new federal program to help insurers cover climate-driven disasters and bring down hefty premiums, as well as so-called climate superfunds. This would require fossil fuel companies to contribute to the costs of damage caused by extreme weather and adapting to the risks of climate change.

Vermont and New York both passed legislation to create funds, although a federal judge blocked New York's.

The 2028 presidential race is expected to begin a few weeks after the midterms end, bringing a fresh round of policy ideas and debates. A new president typically gets to drive the legislative agenda in the beginning of their term if their party also wins Congress.

"As long as the consensus is that clean energy is the cheaper source of energy, and it's going to provide stability over the long term for high energy costs — there's a million ways you can propose solutions through that lens," Deveny said.

"We will invest 21.3 trillion won (€14.3 billion, $15.97 billion), nearly twice ​this year's amount, in AI and the three megaprojects of semiconductors, physical ​AI and ‌AI data centres," Lee said on Monday.

He added that 4.7 trillion won will be invested to bolster "frontier AI development projects, with the aim of achieving world-leading performance for our own AI model, a national strategic asset."

The Lee administration has unveiled a record budget proposal for next year, featuring a 12.8% increase in government spending to 820.9 trillion won (€551.5 billion, $615.62 billion).

The proposal needs parliamentary approval, and Lee's party, the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), currently holds the majority.

South Korea is Asia's fourth-largest economy and home to two of the world's biggest memory chip makers —  Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix.

South Korea recorded faster-than-expected economic growth in the third quarter, driven by chip exports and investment.

Exports totaled $930 billion from January to October and are on track to surpass $1 trillion this year, making South Korea the fourth country to achieve the milestone after the United States, China and Germany.

The previous recordholder was Germany's Sebastian Vettel, who first became a world champion at age 23 in 2010.

"I'm speechless. I’m trying to process what has just happened," said Antonelli after finishing the race.

"It's been an incredible season, and to share this podium with Max ​and Lewis is truly incredible. And looking forward to some more battles in the future," the 20-year-old said.

Antonelli, who secured the title with four races remaining, said the final lap was complicated by the excitement of what he had achieved.

"I think I lost two seconds because I could not see ahead, I think I saw two Ferraris," he said. "It was very special, a lot of memories went through my mind at that moment, and I let it all out after achieving one of my biggest dreams."

Antonelli has now risen to 380 points with four races remaining. Verstappen (261) snuck past Hamilton (259) in the standings, but they will be racing for second the rest of the ​way.

Antonelli started the race in ​fourth and needed to finish ‌ninth or better to secure the championship on Sunday no matter what his peers did.

He had a relatively smooth run, though the race was interrupted by a red flag around Lap ​45 when Valtteri Bottas crashed.

Verstappen, meanwhile, claimed his sixth victory at the Mexico City Grand Prix, further cementing his affinity with the circuit.

The final two Grands Prix are scheduled to be held in Qatar and Abu Dhabi. But it remains uncertain whether they will take place, given the geopolitical instability in the region.

## German police investigating after needles found in Halloween candy

https://p.dw.com/p/5Ow8pPrefer DW on GooglePolice are investigating reports about foreign objects in Halloween candyImage: Polizei Hannover/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementNeedles or other sharp metal objects have been found in sweets that children collected while trick-or-treating for Halloween, German police said Sunday.

Police believe most or all of the objects, which also included metal screws, were placed intentionally to cause harm.

Incidents were reported in Thuringia, Baden-Württemberg, Bavaria, Lower Saxony, and Rhineland-Palatinate, among other locations, police said.

In one of the cases in the southern city of Nuremberg, police said a child had bitten down on a needle while eating a piece of chocolate. The child was unharmed.

In two separate cases in the southwestern state of Baden-Württemberg, police said the needles had been concealed as such that they were not visible from the outside.

In several of the cases, police have opened investigations into attempted aggravated assault.
