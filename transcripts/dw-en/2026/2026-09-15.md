## China tightens travel rules over tech security

https://p.dw.com/p/5MbntThe restrictions come amid a technological arms race between China and the USImage: Wang Yu/Xinhua/picture allianceAdvertisementSweeping travel restrictions on Chinese citizens came into effect on Tuesday, including exit bans for people considered to have endangered China's "industrial or technological security."

The new exit-and-entry rules, which were passed in July, apply to citizens deemed to have violated technology import and export regulations, particularly in the field of artificial intelligence.

The State Council in Beijing said the new rules were formulated to "safeguard national sovereignty, security, and development interests."

The regulations apply not only to people within China but also to Chinese citizens deemed to have committed criminal acts abroad.

In such cases, citizens could be "prohibited from leaving the country for a period of six months to three years from the date of their return to China," said the State Council.

The restrictions come as China remains locked in a technological arms race with the United States, with both countries accusing each other of stealing locally developed AI models in recent months.

And analysts warn that the full scope of the new rules will only become clear once they start being implemented.

Security and criminal activities "can be vaguely defined and easily adjusted," said Chong Ja Ian, an analyst from the National University of Singapore, telling the AFP news agency that Beijing has given itself a "wide remit."

Under the new regulations, foreign nationals could also be denied entry to China for up to five years if found to have made false statements in ​visa applications.

Foreign governments have often reported instances of citizens being denied permission to leave China due ⁠to security-related ​investigations.

Analyst Chong highlighted the recent case of US citizen Min Zin who the US government said was detained improperly in Yunnan province in June.

"The case of Min Zin is a reminder that the range of people [that Beijing] could restrict from exiting could be quite wide – and potentially arbitrary," he said.

The new rules could have particular implications for Taiwanese nationals who work in the tech sector and who travel between Taiwan and China.

Authorities in Taipei have warned citizens to take extra care on visits to China, which considers people from Taiwan to be Chinese citizens.

Beijing has dismissed any such concerns, claiming the rules provide better legal protection for Taiwanese.

Dylan Loh, an associate professor at Singapore's Nanyang Technological University, told AFP the travel restrictions "send a message" that China will take action to prevent "leaks in security or national interest-sensitive areas."

## Netherlands: Suspected sabotage disrupts train services

https://p.dw.com/p/5MbdwThe disruption began during Tuesday's morning rush hourImage: Vincent Jannink/ANP/IMAGOAdvertisementTrain services in various parts of the Netherlands have been severely disrupted, with ProRail, the company that maintains the nation's rail infrastructure, saying that sabotage was possibly at play.

National railway company NS said that train services around the central cities of Zwolle, Deventer and Amersfoort have been affected since the morning rush hour.

In a statement, rail authority ProRail said that "[m]aterials deliberately placed on the tracks have been found at several locations. This is causing track-circuit failures. It appears to be a case of sabotage."

The ‌objects caused failures ‌across ​the network, affecting several level crossings, which disrupted ​road traffic, Pro Rail said.

Though the company itself did not say what the "materials" were, a ProRail spokesperson told the daily de Volkskrant that "pipes had been found attached to the line" in several places.

It remains unknown whether the incident has to do with so-called Prinsjesdag, the day the Dutch government presents its annual budget to parliament, where the reigning monarch gives a speech.

## Why top AI researchers are warning about human extinction

https://p.dw.com/p/5MZxqSome researchers argue that artificial general intelligence could pose unforeseeable risksImage: Marc Asensio/NurPhoto/IMAGO AdvertisementDramatic warnings about the existential risks posed by artificial intelligence to humanity are nothing new.

As early as in the 1940s, the renowned scientist and science fiction author Isaac Asimov explored potential risks and unintended consequences of intelligent machines.

In 1991, Hollywood blockbuster "Terminator 2" imagined a dystopian 2029 in which an out-of-control military AI called Skynet directs killer robots in a war against humanity.

If the warnings of several of the biggest names in AI are to be believed, a similar scenario could become a reality — even before 2029.

"The people building AI earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade," AI researcher Jacob Coxon posted on X last week, without exactly explaining how it might happen.

Coxon also revealed that he had quit his job at Anthropic, a US-based AI company, in protest against what he saw as an irresponsible race among AI companies to develop an ever better, self-improving superintelligence.

He warned that algorithms could begin to analyze and improve their own source code independently, which could shrink development cycles from several months to just a few minutes and thus make them uncontrollable.

Coxon is not an isolated voice. Nobel Prize-winning physicist Geoffrey Hinton, often called the "Godfather of AI," has been warning for years about the serious consequences of artificial superintelligence.

In 2023, he left his job at Google to publicly warn of the dangers of AI technologies he helped pioneer. Yoshua Bengio, a pioneer of artificial neural networks and deep learning, has also expressed concern about current developments and called for government regulation.

As recently as July 2026, more than 1,000 employees of leading AI companies warned in an open letter of an imminent loss of control if the race for "superintelligence" were not subject to significantly stricter regulations.

Despite the growing warnings, US President Donald Trump's administration has clearly shifted its AI policy toward minimizing regulatory hurdles and accelerating AI development.

In his X post, Jacob Coxon indirectly criticized the consequences of this policy: "At OpenAI, many have not deeply internalized the civilizational stakes. At Anthropic, the stakes are well-understood, but they are locked in a race to get there first — they believe no one else will act responsibly, so they must do it themselves, despite the risk."

Coxon, who had moved from OpenAI to Anthropic in early 2026, is familiar with both corporate cultures.

In an interview with The Wall Street Journal, he said that under the "most aggressive scenarios," things could spiral out of control as early as the end of 2027.

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, for his part, is currently calling for a coordinated, industry-wide slowdown in development to buy time to address the risks and prevent any misuse of AI.

In a lengthy essay shared on X over the weekend, he wrote that in six to 12 months, AI agents "could be capable ​of taking over the entire internet" and potentially cause "hundreds of billions of dollars in damage" without proper guardrails.

In recent years, numerous experts have voiced concern about the rapid AI advances, particularly the development of artificial general intelligence, which refers to systems that not only solve individual tasks but also match or exceed human intellectual capabilities in nearly all areas.

Sophisticated AI models, for instance, may pursue goals in ways that diverge from human intentions, something AI security researchers refer to as the "alignment problem."

On top of that, even developers can't always fully understand how AI makes certain decisions.

Among the most prominent critics of the so-called "AI doomers" is Meta's chief scientist, Yann LeCun. In October 2024, he dismissed such concerns as "complete nonsense."

Still other experts note that developing AI systems requires enormous amounts of computing power, energy, data and infrastructure.

They argue that an AI model cannot simply take over the world because it would need to rely on servers, power grids, communication systems and physical resources that are controlled by humans.

In fact, many experts see the short-term risks not so much in the extinction of humanity as in the numerous indirect consequences of AI systems that already exist even without superintelligence.

These include disinformation campaigns on an unprecedented scale and the related manipulation of democratic processes, an expansion of mass surveillance, and job losses across numerous industries.

In August, Meta, Anthropic and OpenAI caused a stir when they announced that their AI systems had unintentionally carried out autonomous hacking activities.

Following reports of further AI hacking attacks during testing, even top US AI frontier labs are now expressing a willingness to slow down the development of the technology.

"Just 42 days after the crime, the Attorney General's Office of the Republic of El Salvador (FGR) obtained a life sentence for aggravated femicide," authorities announced in a post on X.

## Iran and America: A tale of two lands and the pistachio between them

https://p.dw.com/p/5MPrSPistachios like these sold across the US trace their roots back to IranImage: Svetlanasf/Pond5 Images/IMAGOAdvertisementHand-width cracks crisscross Armin's pistachio orchard in southeastern Iran, damaging irrigation pipes and swallowing precious water as the ground beneath him sinks.

"You look, and the earth has opened its mouth, and the water just pours in," Armin says. He has seen the cracks spread rapidly over the past five years, and worries his children may not be able to work the land as his father and grandfather did before him.

Armin, whose real name DW has chosen not to reveal for security reasons, lives in the arid region of Rafsanjan. For generations, farmers there have cultivated the pistachio tree for its small green edible seed used in everything from ice cream to the viral Dubai chocolate.

The region has long covered much of the world's demand for pistachios, but a combination of drought and groundwater extraction for irrigation has made Rafsanjan one of the fastest-sinking areas in Iran. One study found parts of the ground are dropping by up to 37 centimetres a year (14 inches) annually. And that is impacting production.

It's a similar picture in California, where some pistachio farmers are also seeing their ground sink. Joe Coelho's family has cultivated the crop since 2000, and he says while his farm in Fresno is not directly affected, "growers are very aware of the risk."

Some areas nearby are facing serious water problems, he says, adding that "subsidence is absolutely a serious issue in parts of the San Joaquin Valley."

The link between growers in the two countries goes back to the 1960s when US famers started building a pistachio industry. They did it using an Iranian variety — the pistachio that now dominates most Californian orchards is called Kerman, named after a city near Armin's farm.

California has recently overtaken Iran as the world's largest producer, with most of the crop grown in the Central Valley, where Coelho has his orchard. And though it is one of the world's most productive farming regions, there are places where the ground is sinking by as much as 20 centimeters a year.

Like in Iran, the problem is that more water is being pumped from underground aquifers for crop irrigation than is flowing back in.

"As surface water reliability has declined, growers have relied more heavily on groundwater," Coelho told DW, adding that he has witnessed the land change. "In certain areas, the soil does not have the same structure or water movement it once had."

Pistachio orchards in the Central Valley use around 4% of the valley's crop water. In Rafsanjan, more than nine in ten liters of groundwater go to pistachio orchards.

As groundwater levels drop from overpumping, spaces within the aquifer collapse and the earth above begins to sink in a process called land subsidence.

"Land subsidence in Iran is a man-made hazard, not a natural one like an earthquake," says Ali Beitollahi, who leads subsidence research at Iran's Road, Housing and Urban Development Research Center. "Much of the damage cannot be undone. When an aquifer collapses, it can never hold as much water again as before."

Older farmers in Rafsanjan remember reaching water at depths of just 20 meters, says Armin. Today, many have to drill down to 300 meters. Some farmlands near Armin's are dead or dying, he says, while other orchards have been abandoned as wells dried up.

It's another blow to the struggling farmers, who says the boom driven by food trends like Dubai chocolate never reaches them because international sanctions and global competition have stripped Iran of the markets it once held as the world's biggest pistachio producer.

In California, the Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA), passed in 2014, requires water agencies to balance pumping and recharge in groundwater basins by 2040.

Managed aquifer recharge is one method the state is using to do so. It entails allowing water to recharge aquifers in rainy years, by flooding farmlands for instance, as a store for drought. Coelho, who is also the director of sustainability at American Pistachio Growers, is taking part in a pilot project that does just that.

SGMA has seen some progress. According to California's Department of Water Resources, groundwater levels have risen in four out of 10 monitored wells. But large parts of the Central Valley are still sinking.

Helen Dahlke, professor of integrated hydrologic sciences at the University of California, Davis, says recharge alone is not likely to offset decades of overexploitation of groundwater.

"Agriculture is by far the largest consumptive use of water in the Central Valley, so bringing groundwater basins into balance will likely require aligning crop water demand with the long-term water available under a warmer and more variable climate."

In Iran, awareness of groundwater management and subsidence has grown over the past decade among scientists but remains limited, says Ali Beitollahi. He worked on draft subsidence regulations that are currently awaiting government approval. Pointing to the example of Tokyo where subsidence rates fell dramatically after authorities restricted groundwater pumping, he believes the problem could be managed if the state were to act quickly. Though given the current political situation in Iran, swift action is unlikely.

Another urgent step, he says, "is changing how farmers water their fields immediately, and teaching them why it matters."

Most of Rafsanjan's orchards are still flood-irrigated, meaning large amounts of water are lost to evaporation before it reaches plant roots. Armin says he is trying to learn from US growers, but that many older farmers are set in their ways.

Across California's Central Valley, the pressures of a warming climate, drought, and diminishing groundwater are hard to ignore.

"We live with it every day," Coelho says, adding that California's pistachio farmers also need better water policy as well as better infrastructure and protection of water rights.

"Financial sustainability, water sustainability and environmental sustainability are connected," he told DW.

Armin doesn't see a future in pistachio farming. But when he looks at a single pistachio, he sees more than a crop competing for water or a snack on a shelf half a world away.

"This pistachio sometimes comes from a tree a hundred years old," he says. "It has paid for a wedding, for a child's education, for someone's happiness. Its money comes, and it creates a life."

## Europe launches two climate observation satellites

https://p.dw.com/p/5MbPVThe two satellites were carried by a Vega-C rocket, seen here lifting off in French GuianaImage: Ronan Lietar/AFPAdvertisementTwo satellites designed to enhance the monitoring of extreme weather events linked to climate change have been successfully sent into orbit by the European Space Agency (ESA).

The Vega-C rocket carrying the Sentinel-3C and Flex satellites lifted off from the Kourou space center in French Guiana at 10:21 p.m. Monday (0121 GMT Tuesday), with the two spacecraft entering sun-synchronous orbit within two hours of lift-off.

The Flex (Fluorescence Explorer) satellite aims to provide data on the impact of heat waves on plant matter by monitoring the very faint fluorescence emitted by plants when they carry out photosynthesis.

That fluorescence gives an indication of how efficiently vegetation is converting sunlight and carbon dioxide into energy.

The measurements provided by the satellite will thus "help scientists assess plant health, ecosystem productivity and the effects of environmental stress and climate change," according to ESA.

The satellite will orbit the Earth 14 times a day, revisiting particular locations every 27 days.

The Sentinel-3C satellite is to collect data from the marine environment,  including temperature, wave height and ocean currents.

It will also monitor land use and land cover with the aim of optimizing yields to prevent food crises.

Sentinel-3C is part of Copernicus, the Earth observation component of the EU’s space program.

Simonetta Cheli, the director of Earth Observation Programmes at the ESA, described the missions as "essential" in helping with the "climate change we are experiencing."

## US scraps greenhouse gas rules for power plants

https://p.dw.com/p/5MbKTPower plants are responsible for about a quarter of the greenhouse gas pollution produced in the USImage: J. David Ake/AP Photo/icture allianceAdvertisementUS President Donald Trump's administration has scrapped limits on carbon emissions from coaland gas-fired power plants, in its latest move to dismantle regulations aimed at curbing climate change.

"Today, we are cutting the red tape to deliver the largest power sectorderegulatory action ever," Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Administrator Lee Zeldin said at a press conference. Zeldin made the announcement during a G20 energy ministers' meeting in Houston.

"We are not going to bend the law to climate alarmism and climate radicalism at all at the Trump EPA," he said, while accusing journalists of trying to "scare" the public.

The EPA has repealed Biden-era standards, introduced in 2024 under the Clean Air Act, to control the emission of planet-heating gases from existing and new power plants. The rules had required power plants to reduce carbon dioxide emissions by up to 90% or shut down by 2039.

The latest move not only rolls back the initiatives introduced under former Democrat presidents, but also proposes to stop future greenhouse gas regulations affecting the power sector.

"For over 15 years, the Obama and Biden administrations implemented a war on coal to destroy reliable and affordable energy," Zeldin was quoted as saying in an EPA statement. The Trump administration would change that, he said. "Americans will see a decrease in electricity prices."

Emission as a result of power generation accounts for about a quarter of the country's greenhouse gas emissions.
