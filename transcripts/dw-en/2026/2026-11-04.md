## Kosovo: Protests and outrage after a woman was killed despite repeatedly seeking assistance from the authorities

https://p.dw.com/p/5P4NiPrefer DW on GoogleProtesters poured red paint onto a flight of steps outside the seat of Kosovo's government on the day after Arlinda Zekaj's murder. The banner behind them reads 'The state killed Arlinda' Image: Vjosa Cerkini/DWAdvertisementArlinda Zekaj repeatedly asked the Kosovo police for protection. They believed she was exaggerating.

On October 25, the 49-year-old once again reported her husband to the police. This time, he was arrested. But Zekaj also asked the police for additional protection — this time against her husband's family. But to no avail.

The police drove Arlinda Zekaj home, where her killer was waiting. At 8:58 p.m., Zekaj got out of the squad car. A short time later, the police received an emergency call. When officers arrived at Zekaj's house at 9:10 p.m., they found only her body.

The 49-year-old had been brutally murdered in her bedroom with an axe. By that time, the alleged perpetrator — her brother-in-law, Albert Sylaj — had fled.

The murder, which took place in the village of Vitomirica near the city of Peja in western Kosovo, has sparked numerous public protests and demonstrations involving thousands of people across the country.

More so than almost any other femicide in the country in recent years, Zekaj's death showed that one of the things that made her killing possible was the fact that police and prosecutors ignored the victim's pleas for help .

One of the first protests took place the following day, on October 26, when a group of feminists emptied buckets of red paint on steps outside the seat of Kosovo's government in the capital, Pristina.

"The state killed Arlinda," read a banner the activists hung at the entrance to the building.

In the days that followed and on November 1, public anger brought thousands of people onto streets across the country. Many carried photographs of murdered women. One slogan appeared again and again: "The government has blood on its hands."

The protesters are demanding that charges be brought against the police officers and prosecutors responsible for handling the Zekaj case.

They are also calling for the resignation of Interior Minister Xhelal Svecla and Justice Minister Donika Gervalla, whom Kosovo Prime Minister Albin Kurti appointed national coordinator for protection against domestic violence in March.

For his part, however, Kurti acknowledged that certain Kosovo state institutions had "failed."

As far back as the summer, Arlinda Zekaj had reported her husband to the police for making threats against her. A court in Peja subsequently issued a protection order, granted her temporary custody of their son and gave her the right to remain in the family home.

The protection order allowed her husband to continue living in the same house, provided he did not come within two meters (about 6.5 feet) of her. Outside the home, he was required to keep a distance of at least 20 meters.

Zekaj had decided against moving to a women's shelter because she wanted to continue living in her own home with her son.

Even after the protection order was issued, she repeatedly made complaints to the police, among other things about threats from her brother-in-law Albert Sylaj.

Three days before the killing, on October 22, Zekaj reported that members of her husband's family, including Sylaj, were in her home against her wishes. According to her account, he threatened her with the words: "Leave or I'll kill you."

Since 2010, 55 women in Kosovo have been killed by partners or family members, often after years of documented abuse and ignored complaints.

In recent years, successive Kosovo governments have adopted a number of laws and regulations aimed at better combating violence against women.

These measures include the possibility of life sentences for perpetrators in femicide cases.

Activists argue, however, that the authorities responsible for enforcing these measures — including police, social services, prosecutors and courts — have failed to implement them consistently and in a coordinated manner.

Writing in an article for the news portal Nacionale, lawyer Njomeza Zejnullahu said that "After every murder, we hear the same word: failure. 'The institutions have failed.' I disagree. Failure is when something happens once, when a system that normally works breaks down at one point. When the cases keep repeating themselves, when the victims are almost always women and each of them had previously knocked on the door of an institution, it is no longer a failure. It is a collapse of the state and society in relation to women in general."

In the aftermath of Arlinda Zekaj's killing, several senior police officers from Peja and the surrounding area have been suspended. A prosecutor who had handled Zekaj's complaints has also been suspended.

"All those involved within the chain of responsibility must not only be removed from office but also criminally prosecuted for failing to fulfil their official duties," said Liridona Sijarina, a political scientist and feminist activist with Kosovo's Kolektivi Feminist (Feminist Collective).

"That includes not only police officers, prosecutors and judges, but also Mayor of Peja Gazmend Muhaxheri and, ultimately, government ministers," she told DW.

Sijarina went on to say that feminist activists are also calling on the Interior Ministry to create a dedicated unit that would treat women's safety as a matter of national security and focus specifically on preventing femicides.

She also said that the government should make its draft legislation on affordable social housing a top political priority and ensure that women who have experienced domestic violence are eligible for social housing.

"In our view," said Sijarina, "the behavior of the institutions so far has deepened women's distrust of the state and, as a result, allowed violence against them to escalate. The government now has the opportunity to show whether it truly takes women's lives seriously by clearly responding to our demands."

## Gold for Palestine: Meet junior karate champ Mariam Bsharat

https://p.dw.com/p/5P36MPrefer DW on GoogleMariam Bsharat has won back to back world titles in karateImage: Fabienne Abu Wahib/Karate Insights/PS Images/IMAGOAdvertisement"After the referee lifted my hand to show I'm now a two-time world champion, it was a very overwhelming feeling," Mariam Bsharat said. "Making history once is something, but making it twice is really something else. I felt very happy and I felt a big relief. Only I know how many sacrifices I made and how many hardships I needed to overcome."

Themes of relief, joy and sacrifice are commonplace in the reflections of athletes after a big win. In Mariam Bsharat's case, it's about more than just dedication to training and taking care of the body. The 17-year-old won her second under-21 World Karate Championship gold medal competing for the Palestine national team in Poland in October.

"It was everyday life, seeing people get killed around me and houses getting bombed," she told DW of her upbringing in the occupied West Bank.

"I faced challenges in transportation," Bsharat said. "I wasn't able to go from one city to another to be able to train with other people, so that I can actually prepare for competitions. I needed to travel to other countries like Jordan or Egypt ... which was very hard for me. And, financially, the support wasn't really the support an elite athlete needed."

Growing up in Nablus, Bsharat had a narrow path to the top. But initially, it was her own reluctance that threatened to stop a champion career before it started.

"When I was a little kid, I did not really love it," said Bsharat, who competes in the division for fighters who weigh under 66 kilograms (145 pounds).

"I started it because my family was in that sport," Bsharat said. "My father had a dojo, and my mother also did that sport. My siblings and my aunts and my uncles — it's a family thing. I started it because they were all doing it. In time, I started getting into it."

Family has been a constant source of comfort and inspiration for Bsharat. Her father and uncle have acted as manager and coach respectively. While her mother and siblings moved with Mariam to Hamburg, Germany, last year, her father remained in order to take care of his business, the dojo, though he visits and tries to be with his daughter in competitions. Integration has been made easier by her mastery of the language, taught to her by her German mother.

"I'm thankful to be here because being in Germany opened lot of doors for me, and I think I have a brighter future here," Bsharat said. "But also being in Palestine was just my everyday life, and I loved it there. My family was around me, my friends were around me, and I just felt comfortable, which was a big change coming here to Germany. I think every change comes with hardships."

As well as the obvious difficulties of living in an area occupied by Israel, there are particular challenges for sportspeople living in the West Bank and the Gaza Strip, with access to facilities, travel and constant security fears among them. As such, very few people from the Palestinian territories become successful on the international sporting stage. That, Bsharat said, gives her responsibilities that are unusual and heavy for a person of any age, let alone a 17-year-old.

"Being an elite athlete in Palestine is something very special because not everybody in Palestine has that opportunity. There aren't really a lot of athletes that are elite and who win international medals,” Bsharat said.

"So all that pressure from all the country and all the people, the family, the coaches are on me because I'm representing them, raising their flag,” she said. "I sometimes even forget to fight for myself and actually think about myself."

That has not yet proved a barrier to Bsharat, who is is ranked first in the world in her category by the World Karate Federation (WKF). With Palestine recognized by the International Olympic Committee, Bsharat would likely have been a contender to raise that flag in Los Angeles at the 2028 Olympics. But the sport was dropped from the Games in 2024.

"The global karate community remains steadfast in our belief that the natural place for karate is within the Olympic Games, and we sincerely hope that the incongruence we suffered in Paris 2024 and LA 2028 will be corrected for Brisbane 2032, if not sooner," the WKF wrote in response to the decision in 2024. The sport was also taken off the slate for the recent Youth Games in Senegal.

Should karate return in Brisbane, Bsharat would likely be approaching her prime. In the meantime, she is looking forward to starting her journey as a senior when she turns 18. But she won't be hanging around.

"My goal is it to be a world champion at seniors, which is the hardest and the best thing you can achieve in karate," she said.

"But winning an Olympic medal, being called Olympic champion or just representing my country with that flag [is a thought that] is just full of happiness and pride."

## Africa&apos;s shifting role in the global drug trade

https://p.dw.com/p/5P22SPrefer DW on GoogleOnce seen mainly as a transit route for drugs, parts of Africa are now emerging as production hubsImage: Olympia De Maismont/AFPAdvertisementFor years, Africa was primarily known as a transit point for international drug trafficking networks.

But recent drug seizures and laboratory discoveries show that illicit drugs are no longer only passing through African countries — they are increasingly being manufactured there.

Recent discoveries of methamphetamine laboratories in countries such as Nigeria, South Africa, Kenya and Mozambique have fueled concerns that Africa is becoming a significant production hub for synthetic drugs.

According to the UN Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC), synthetic drug production increasingly involves transnational criminal networks and clandestine laboratories capable of supplying both local and international markets.

The debate gained new urgency this year after Nigeria's National Drug Law Enforcement Agency (NDLEA) dismantled what the agency described as the largest clandestine methamphetamine laboratory ever uncovered in the country.

The operation in Nigeria's southwestern Ogun State led to the arrest of 10 suspects, including three Mexican nationals whom the NDLEA said had been brought into the country to help manufacture methamphetamine.

NDLEA's chairman, Mohamed Buba Marwa, said officers seized more than 2.4 metric tons of methamphetamine, precursor chemicals and other materials with an estimated international market value of about $363 million (€312 million).

"It is no longer something you only see in movies," said DW correspondent Olisa Chukwumah. "The discovery raised serious questions about the level of local consumption if production is now happening in Nigeria."

Chukwumah said Nigerian authorities have uncovered meth laboratories before, but the size and sophistication of recent operations suggest that drug manufacturing networks are becoming more deeply established.

Dokun Adedeji, co-founder of the Compassionate Care Recovery Initiative, said Nigeria's drug landscape has evolved significantly over the past decade.

"We used to think we were only a transit nation," he explained. "Then we became a consumer nation. Now we are discovering that we are also a production nation."

He noted that authorities have uncovered dozens of smaller laboratories over the years, with meth production sites identified in multiple states.

According to the UNODC, increasing local drug consumption and the expansion of illicit drug markets have coincided with the growth of synthetic-drug production in West Africa.

He said substance abuse is contributing to school dropouts, family breakdowns, mental health challenges and rising criminal activity. Many families struggle to seek help because of stigma and fear of public judgment.

"We see families suffering every day," he said. "Many people are unable to speak openly about addiction because of concerns about reputation and discrimination."

The UNODC says young people are increasingly affected by drug-use disorders, with 34% of people receiving treatment for such disorders in Africa under the age of 25.

In addition to traditional drugs such as cannabis, users are increasingly turning to synthetic substances, including methamphetamine, often known on the streets as "ice." There is also growing misuse of prescription medications and reports of experimentation with dangerous inhalants and other toxic substances.

Nigeria's National Drug Law Enforcement Agency has intensified enforcement efforts in recent years, leading to a series of high-profile drug seizures and arrests.

NGOs have increasingly stepped in to fill the gap through awareness campaigns, particularly on university campuses where drug use is becoming more visible.

Chukwumah said there is still no coordinated, top-down policy approach and that much of the awareness work is being carried out by civil society groups.

Adedeji argued that policies based solely on punishment often drive users underground, making it harder for authorities and healthcare providers to identify and support people struggling with addiction.

UNODC and civil society groups advocate a broader response that extends beyond law enforcement.

Among the proposals being discussed are stronger national policies on substance abuse, expanded rehabilitation services, workplace drug prevention programs and increased regional cooperation across West Africa.

## Harry Potter films turns 25 ahead of new TV series&apos; release

https://p.dw.com/p/5P4E6Prefer DW on GoogleRose to fame as Harry Potter: Daniel Radcliffe at the age of 12Image: Warner Bros/Everett Collection/picture allianceAdvertisementOn November 4, 2001, London's Leicester Square became a world of witches and wizards. The occasion was the world premiere of "Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone" (released in the US and India as "Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone").

"Thousands of excited fans, some dressed up in wizard hats and cloaks, gathered to catch a glimpse of arriving celebrities," the BBC reported at the time.

Daniel Radcliffe, who played Harry Potter, was just 12 years old and clearly impressed by the spectacle. The BBC quoted him as saying, "It is so cool. I have never heard my name shouted so much in an hour."

The first Harry Potter book was published by Britain's Bloomsbury in June 1997, with an initial print run of just 500 hardcover copies.

Today, first editions are prized collectors' items, with hardcover copies commanding high prices at auction. In mid-October, one sold for $400,000 through US auction house Heritage Auctions.

At first, the book's popularity grew largely by word of mouth among schoolchildren. Media attention followed when it won the prestigious Nestle Smarties Book Prize later that year.

With an estimated 120 million-plus copies sold worldwide, it is the best-selling book in the series and ranks among the best-selling novels of all time.

For years, J.K. Rowling's public image was defined by her extraordinary rise from a single mother on welfare to a billionaire.

But in recent years, debate over her views on gender and transgender identity has increasingly overshadowed that success. It's an issue Rowling herself frequently weighs in on.

In an essay published on her website on October 21, 2026, Rowling reiterated her opposition to what she calls the "current, dominant strain of trans activism." She argues that this movement "holds that a man who declares himself a woman is, literally, a woman, and is therefore entitled to be treated as a woman in all contexts." Rowling argues that allowing trans women into single-sex spaces poses a risk. She describes biological sex as an indisputable biological fact.

Such statements have led critics to accuse Rowling of transphobia, while others regard her stance as a defense of women's rights and safety.

The controversy has also divided her fans. Some stand by Rowling, while others try to draw a distinction between the books and their author.

Others have left Harry Potter behind altogether. One former fan is German YouTuber Coldmirror, known for her Harry Potter parodies. The media artist from the Bremen area, whose real name is Kathrin Fricke, told the Süddeutsche Zeitung newspaper: "I don't have any love left for Harry. I'm still somewhat interested, but I can no longer watch it in good conscience." She cited Rowling's campaigning against transgender people as the reason.

The "Harry Potter" film cast is divided as well. The three main stars — Daniel Radcliffe, Emma Watson and Rupert Grint — have publicly spoken out in support of transgender people. Meanwhile, Ralph Fiennes, who played Lord Voldemort, and Helena Bonham Carter, who portrayed Bellatrix Lestrange, have defended Rowling's right to express her views.

Despite the controversy surrounding Rowling, Warner Bros., which holds the Harry Potter film rights, is bringing the books back to the screen — this time as a series for HBO. The premiere is scheduled for December 25.

The eight-episode first season is an an adaption of the first book. Even though the episode lengths haven't been announced, the scope of a TV series gives each book much more room than the original films did. That means more time to explore subplots, character development and everyday life at Hogwarts. While many fans are looking forward to a more detailed adaptation, others remain skeptical. They find it hard to imagine the familiar characters being played by anyone else.

The new trio consists of Dominic McLaughlin as Harry Potter, Arabella Stanton as Hermione Granger and Alastair Stout as Ron Weasley.

J.K. Rowling is an executive producer on the show. HBO says, however, that her views on gender identity won't be reflected in the storyline. Several people involved in the new production have publicly distanced themselves from her views. Paapa Essiedu, who has been cast as Severus Snape, signed an open letter supporting transgender people. Rowling subsequently said she had neither the power nor the desire to have him removed from the show because of it. John Lithgow, who plays Dumbledore, and Nick Frost, who portrays Hagrid, have also spoken out against Rowling's views.

Meanwhile, the Harry Potter merchandising machine is still going strong. On November 4, Britain's largest Harry Potter store to date is set to open in London. According to Warner Bros., the two-story shop covers more than 1,950 square meters.

## What the Democrats&apos; midterms win means for the US economy and Trump

https://p.dw.com/p/5P3bAPrefer DW on GoogleSoaring gas prices played a key role in the outcome of the midtermsImage: Patrick T. Fallon/AFPAdvertisementFor the millions who cast their ballots in the US midterm elections, one issue ranked above all others: the economy.

So what does the Democrats' stunning capture of both the US Senate and House of Representatives mean for the economy and economic policy, given that President Donald Trump still has two years of his term to go?

Markets have rebounded after initially trending negatively. Global stocks rallied as investors appeared to take heart from the Democrats' victory.

With Democrats now holding comfortable majorities in both houses of Congress and Trump still in the White House, it could lead to gridlock in Washington. Trump has already said he would become "Mr Veto," meaning he would block any legislation the Democrats advance.

"The magnitude of the gains for the Democrats in the midterms does not change too much for the near-term policy outlook, given the party still lacks a filibuster-proof majority in the Senate and President Trump has veto power to block legislation," Stephen Brown, chief North America economist with Capital Economics, said in a note.

"I think we are looking at two years of complete 100% gridlock," Jacob Funk Kirkegaard, nonresident senior fellow with the Peterson Institute for International Economics, told DW after the results came in. "Nothing will get done of any kind."

Some investors appear worried that the Democrats could force through AI regulation, especially if they also win the White House in 2028. Rising opposition among large majorities of the US population to data centers — the key infrastructure behind AI — helped secure victories for many Democratic candidates.

Kirkegaard, who is also a senior fellow at Bruegel, a European economics think tank, said the vote showed the extent of that opposition.  Even if the Democrats can't force regulation, he thinks local opposition will make it harder for data centers to be built, which could hit the valuation of big tech firms that have benefitted from the AI boom.

"This election shows that the political underpinning of the rapid, aggressive AI growth story is shaky," he said. "It doesn't mean it's going to stop or be made illegal, but the dynamic of the growth story has shifted."

If the Democrats were to win the presidency in 2028, Brown said, they would be perfectly placed to make major policy changes. "That includes the potential for tax hikes on corporations and high-income individuals as well as regulation that could delay the AI rollout," he said.

According to polling conducted in early October by the Pew Research Center, 56% of Americans say Trump's tariff policies will have mostly negative impacts on the country, with only 20% saying mostly positive.

That sentiment clearly drove many Democrats to victory in the midterms — particularly in states where tariffs have had a particularly negative impact. That includes those with sectors reliant on exports, such as Texas, Michigan, Iowa and Illinois, where counter-tariffs have hit and those that have high trade volumes with Canada.

Yet, barring an unprecedented climbdown from Trump — who has been ideologically wedded to tariffs since he first became a public figure almost 50 years ago — it's not clear how the Democrats could force substantial change to the president's signature economic policy.

"Trump will not be moderated," Deborah Elms, head of trade policy at the Hinrich Foundation in Singapore, told DW earlier this year. "Expect him to continue his trade disruption and tariff policies for as long as he remains in the Oval Office."

Though the US Constitution gives Congress the power to collect and organize tariffs, various laws passed over the years have given presidents power to implement tariffs when they cite certain conditions, such as national security or economic emergencies.

Trump has already used such avenues for his tariffs and can continue to do so, despite some legal rebukes. However, while Congress can't necessarily force an end to Trump's existing tariffs, it can now take further legal action against them to prevent greater escalation.

Though Trump and the Democrats are clearly not going to make common cause, Kirkegaard said, the scale of the Republicans' defeat might force Trump to be less erratic in his policymaking, from escalating his trade war with Canada to his Iran policy.

Trump said in the aftermath of Tuesday's defeats that he would "work with" the Democrats and told the Financial Times in the run-up to the vote that he was unlikely to escalate the war in Iran.

Were a deal to be struck with Iran, soaring energy prices — a pivotal election issue — could finally fall, helping voters with their cost-of-living concerns.

"You've got to get a deal with Iran, and you've got to bring down gas prices," Kirkegaard said. "From a political point of view, that's the most important thing now."

George Saravelos, an analyst with Deutsche Bank FX Research, thinks the markets are beginning to become more optimistic in that area. "With oil flows through the Middle East having materially recovered, the market is pricing a large imminent risk premium of re-escalation and energy supply disruption," he said in a note.

Despite the scale of the Democrats' victory, the fact that Trump remains in the Oval Office will complicate all efforts to force a change to signature economic policies and issues.

Yet Congress can now exert considerable pressure. Another issue vital to voters was corruption and the belief that the Trump administration has engaged in corruption and personal enrichment while in office.

That raises the prospect of the new Democratic-controlled Congress launching legal investigations and potentially pursuing another impeachment of Trump, which could force the president to negotiate on some economic issues to secure his political survival.

"Can you find 10 or 12 Republican senators who, in the face of overwhelming corruption, would be willing to vote for articles of impeachment? I wouldn't rule it out," Kirkegaard said. https://p.dw.com/p/5P332Prefer DW on GoogleJanosch standing next to his beloved characters, Bear and Tiger, in 2010Image: Roland Weihrauch/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementJanosch, the pen name of German author and illustrator Horst Eckert, won hearts around the world with his warm and simple sense of humor. Janosch was born as Horst Eckert on March 11, 1931, in Hindenburg (now Zabrze, Poland) in what was then Upper Silesia. After World War II, his family had to flee to Germany.

In 1953, Janosch started studying at the Academy of Art in Munich, thinking that it would be easy to become an artist. He was however dismissed after just a few terms for "lack of talent."

But luckily for his later readers, Janosch did not give up. He continued to work as a freelance artist, soon discovering his second great gift, namely writing.

Although Janosch became famous primarily for his children's books, he also wrote books for adults that focused on his own childhood.

Even though he wrote cheerful books for children, Janosch hardly had any positive memories of his own childhood, as his father was an alcoholic. Later on, Janosch tried to overcome the burden of his childhood memories by turning to alcohol as well.

Janosch's animal figures are endearing but not always well-behaved. They love all kinds of nonsense. But even if they lie to each other or put their own interests first, the true winner at the end of stories is always friendship and solidarity.

Janosch achieved his major breakthrough with "The Trip to Panama," a story about two friends, a little tiger and a bear, who set out to find their paradise in Panama. After touring around in circles, they finally end up where they started out; they realize that their journey was the destination. Janosch received the German Youth Book Prize in 1979 for the work.

Janosch's protagonists have become familiar to generations of children all over the world. They come up over and over again in the various stories carefully illustrated with water colors.

There is the honest lion Hans from the toy chest who fails to catch a nutcracker with his lies. There is Schnuddel and a ridiculous bragger Günter the Box-Frog. And then there is a tiger that collects mushrooms while looking out for his devoted friend, a bear, who always stands up for him even when the tiger temporarily leaves him because of a little pig in one of the stories.

The "Tigerente," literally "Tiger-Duck," a wooden duck on wheels with yellow-brown stripes, appears on numerous pictures by the illustrator. At times, the animal has been tossed into a corner, at times it is hanging from the ceiling, or the Box-Frog pulls it across the drawing.

From 2013 to 2019, he wrote a column for the German weekly Die Zeit, taking on the role of Mr. Wondrak, who commented on world affairs. Shortly before his 90th birthday in 2021, the complete collection of his Mr. Wondrak columns were published as a book in German.

Like the protagonists in his stories, Janosch always tried to content himself with few things. As he wrote in one of his books: "He who needs almost nothing, has everything."

## What US midterm elections mean for Ukraine, Iran and elsewhere

https://p.dw.com/p/5Oz6FPrefer DW on GoogleThe US midterms might not select a new president but they will affect US policy both at home and abroadImage: Jacquelyn Martin/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementThe outcome of the midterm election will shape policy in the United States for years and have repercussions around the globe. Decisions made in Washington affect both US adversaries such as Iran and countries such as Ukraine, which is seeking more US support.

President Donald Trump does not have the final say when it comes to deciding where the United States goes to war or which countries get financial or military aid. While the president wields more power in the US than in parliamentary democracies such as Germany, he does not govern alone — even in the land of opportunity.

The House of Representatives and the Senate, the two chambers of Congress in which the Democratic Party secured a clear majority on Tuesday, wield considerable influence. They decide on legislation and government spending and also serve as a check on the president and the intelligence agencies.

The president can veto legislation passed by Congress. Congress can override that veto with a two-thirds majority in both chambers. The legislature also handles impeachment proceedings. In addition, the president can only appoint Cabinet members, US ambassadors and federal judges from a list of nominees the Senate approves.

In the 2024 US election, the Republican Party secured not just the White House through Trump's reelection but also both chambers of Congress, which made it easier for the US president to govern.

The Democrats' resounding midterm victory has now flipped control of both chambers, which could shift the direction of major foreign policy issues.

Joe Biden, a Democrat, was in the White House when Russia launched its invasion of Ukraine. Alongside Europe, the US stood out as a reliable partner, supporting Ukraine with financial aid, military expertise and, above all, large-scale weapons deliveries.

The situation changed abruptly under Trump. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy  famously received a notably frosty welcome during his 2025 visit to the White House. Trump now openly admits that he gets along better with Russian President Vladimir Putin than with Zelenskyy.

The US has suspended sanctions against Russia and reduced financial and military aid to Ukraine. Now the Democrats have majorities in both chambers of Congress, they could put increased support for Ukraine on the agenda.

"Democrats criticize the Trump administration for not doing enough for Ukraine," Brandon Conradis, a former DW journalist and now an editor at Bloomberg Government, told DW before Tuesday's election. "They can push legislation that would provide more aid to Ukraine."

If Trump vetoed such legislation, the outcome would depend on the balance of power in Congress. But a two-thirds majority could override his veto with support from some Republicans.

"I don't think Congress will have huge powers here," said Michael O'Hanlon, director of research for foreign policy at the US think tank Brookings Institution, told DW before the midterm elections, but added that Congress could perhaps prevent a major escalation by refusing to fund it.

## From rotten food to political opponents: Researchers explore how disgust relates to our beliefs, and why the connection is complicated

https://p.dw.com/p/5OYctPrefer DW on GoogleFrom rotten food and sexual behavior to political beliefs, researchers want to know how different forms of disgust relate to our attitudesImage: picture allianceAdvertisementSome studies have linked disgust sensitivity to socially conservative attitudes.

But as Americans vote in the US midterm elections, it's time to think about how disgust shapes our politics, or whether our beliefs shape what we find disgusting?

The smell of rotten food can make us recoil. Certain sexual practices can provoke a similar reaction. And in today's polarized political climate, even political opponents can become objects of disgust.

Psychologists have long investigated whether sensitivity to disgust helps explain political beliefs. A review of 24 studies found that people who reported being more easily disgusted tended to hold more socially conservative views.

"We think that it evolved in part to reduce exposure to things that could make you sick," psychologist Yoel Inbar, of the University of British Columbia in Canada, told DW.

But disgust is not a single emotion. The Three-Domain Disgust Scale, developed in 2009, distinguishes three types:

One theory is that people who are especially wary of germs and disease may also prefer traditional social rules.

Inbar explains that avoiding potential sources of infection can also mean missing out on new experiences.

"Some people are more on the cautious conservative side, and some people are more on the exploring risk-taking side when it comes to pathogen exposure," he said.

Indeed, the language of contamination also appears in political rhetoric. During his 2024 presidential campaign, Donald Trump repeatedly described immigrants as "poisoning the blood of our country." Trump has also described himself as a "germaphobe."

Inbar acknowledges that the evidence linking sensitivity to disgust with conservative political beliefs remains uncertain.

"We have hypotheses. I don't know that we have as strong of evidence as I would like," Inbar said.

Disgust is not exclusively conservative. And what people find revolting can depend on their values.

A 2020 study titled "Is Disgust a "Conservative" Emotion?" found that liberals and conservatives were disgusted by different things.

Conservatives, generally aligned with the Republicans in US politics, reported greater disgust at examples involving sexual behaviors and drug use, while liberals, generally aligned with the Democrats, reported greater disgust at animal abuse and environmental pollution.

However, when asked how easily disgusted they were in general, there was no clear difference across the political spectrum.

Some early studies suggested conservatives showed stronger physical reactions to frightening or disgusting images, measured partly through changes in sweating.

But subsequent research, including a 2020 study by political psychologist Bert Bakker at the University of Amsterdam, struggled to reproduce those findings.

Studies based on questionnaires, however, have generally found that people with more conservative attitudes report being more easily disgusted.

"If bodily reactions don't differ across political groups, why do feelings?" Manos Tsakiris, of Royal Holloway and the Centre for the Politics of Feelings at the University of London, told DW.

Tsakiris and colleague Mariana von Mohr wondered whether the answer might lie in how people perceive and interpret signals from their bodies.

Scientists call the perception of internal sensations, such as hunger, breathing or a racing heart, "interoception." It's sometimes called our eighth sense, alongside the familiar five, plus our senses of balance and body position.

"A bodily signal doesn't arrive with a label attached," Tsakiris told DW. "We have to notice it and make sense of it."

The researchers surveyed 632 US adults, asking how much they noticed and trusted signals from their bodies — a measure known as "interoceptive sensibility."

They found that people who were more easily disgusted by sexual behavior tended to be more conservative, regardless of how aware they felt of their bodies.

But for moral disgust, the link to conservatism was found only among people who reported lower bodily awareness.

What particularly surprised Tsakiris was that perceived bodily awareness alone had no link to political views.

"People who are highly attuned to their bodies were neither more liberal nor more conservative," he said.

Inbar questions whether all forms of disgust are equally useful for studying political attitudes.

He and Joshua Tybur, one of the creators of the Three-Domain Disgust Scale, generally prefer measuring pathogen disgust — reactions to potential sources of disease — rather than sexual disgust.
