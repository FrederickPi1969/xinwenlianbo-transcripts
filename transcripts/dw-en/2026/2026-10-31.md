A "narrative of violence" was being used "to divert attention from our core demands," spokeswoman Veronika Dinter told the German broadcaster Deutschlandfunk.

She stressed that "GenZ regelt" — roughly meaning "Gen Z takes charge" — called for peaceful demonstrations, pointing to its published principles. Anyone who shared those principles was welcome, she said.

10/31/2026October 31, 2026What are the rules governing protests in Germany?Timothy JonesGermany guarantees its citizens the right to protest, as here in Berlin on August 8 against the far-right AfDImage: Michael Kuenne/PRESSCOV/ZUMA/picture alliancePeople in Germany have the fundamental right, enshrined in the constitution or Basic Law, to assemble in public provided they remains peaceful and are unarmed.

However, several states have adopted their own assembly acts, which means some of them have additional restrictions, such as a ban on protesting on highways in the state of North Rhine-Westphalia.

Federal law itself imposes the obligation on organizers of protests to notify the local authority entrusted with approving public assemblies at least 48 hours before they invite people publicly to take part.

This aims to allow authorities to put in place security measures and to lessen the impact of any assembly on others by, for example, instigating traffic control measures.

Spontaneous assemblies are, however, also allowed in cases where a topical issue of urgent importance is at stake, and there is no notification requirement for indoor assemblies.

Protests or other assemblies can be banned beforehand or dispersed while they are underway, but this is considered a means of last resort.

Some locations are also subject to restrictions on protests, such as the area around the Reichstag building in Berlin where the German parliament sits, and around the Federal Constitutional Court in Karlsruhe.

Demonstrations around the Reichstag are, for example, permitted only when the parliament is not in session.

Post-war Germany has seen several massive demonstrations, including one in 1983, when 250,000 people — according to police estimates — gathered in the then West German capital, Bonn, to protest against the decision by NATO to station American nuclear missiles on German territory.

That was, however, surpassed in size by a protest in Berlin in 2003 against the invasion of Iraq by US-led forces, which drew a crowd of around half a million people.

It has explicitly called for peaceful protests without the violence that marred recent demonstrations in France.

## Bollywood and the underworld — together in the spotlight?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OrDdPrefer DW on GoogleIndian actor Tiger Shroff (center) was recently shot at in Mumbai [FILE March 17, 2017]Image: Amal KS/Hindustan Times/IMAGOAdvertisement"In the entire film industry, whoever works without aligning with us, or works with Salman Khan, will be killed."

This warning was delivered in an audio message by presumed attackers on Indian actor Tiger Shroff in Mumbai earlier this month, as they claimed responsibility for the shooting.

The apparent perpetrators said they were connected to Lawrence Bishnoi, a student-politician-turned-gangster who has been in prison since 2014. Notably, police have not confirmed the gang's involvement.

The shots fired at Shroff, which injured his driver, were only the latest in a string of attacks targeting prominent figures from the Mumbai-based film industry, popularly known as "Bollywood".

Since 2022, gunmen fired shots at actor Salman Khan's apartment and at the home of filmmaker Rohit Shetty in Mumbai, as well as actor Disha Patani's family home in northern India. Actor Ranveer Singh also reportedly received an extortion demand of nearly $1.1 million (around €980,000).

The attacks have spread to the other side of the globe, with a cafe owned by comedian Kapil Sharma in Surrey, Canada, also hit three times.

For an industry that once lived in the shadow of organized crime, the attacks have revived old fears, harking back to the crime bosses of the 1990s. But experts say the threat facing Bollywood today looks very different from the one it faced three decades ago.

"The mafia was always fascinated with the movie industry," said S. Hussain Zaidi, crime journalist and author of "Dongri to Dubai: Six Decades of the Mumbai Mafia," who has covered organized crime in the city for over three decades.

Gangsters like Dawood Ibrahim, Abu Salem and Chhota Shakeel "were totally obsessed" with Bollywood, he told DW, much like mafiosi who sought a foothold in Hollywood.

To the gangsters, films offered glitz and glamor, but also a way to launder their profits. They invested in films "so that they could control the industry and have a legitimate way of making money," Zaidi added.

When producers didn't cooperate, the consequences could be fatal. In 1997, music mogul Gulshan Kumar, founder of the label T-Series, was shot dead. In 2000, filmmaker Rakesh Roshan, father of Bollywood star Hrithik Roshan, was shot and wounded.

Mumbai's streets were "made red with blood" in 1998, Zaidi said, describing the year as the pinnacle of gang killings.

D. Sivanandhan, the former Police Commissioner of Mumbai, who led the crackdown, says 101 businessmen were shot dead in 1998 alone, or one "every third day," mostly over unpaid extortion demands.

Police efforts were reinforced by a tough new organized crime law passed in 1999, which Sivanandhan calls the "ultimate weapon." Security forces used electronic surveillance and preventive detention, and seized some 2,500 illegal weapons.

"A few encounters also happened, but that is not a policy nor a strategy,” he told DW, referring to police shootouts.

By the early 2000s, the gangs' hold had been broken, and a new Mumbai seemed to be emerging.

Bishnoi, 33, comes from a landowning family in the northern state of Punjab and was studying in Chandigarh when he was drawn into violent student politics. Then, he was arrested, incarcerated and eventually turned to crime, says journalist Jupinderjit Singh, author of "Gangs of Punjab."

Bishnoi went on to build what Singh describes as one of the first criminal syndicates spanning several northern Indian states. Investigators say he still directs operations from prison using concealed phones and messaging apps.

Currently, Bishnoi is held in a jail in Prime Minister Narendra Modi's home state of Gujarat.

His network stretches beyond India's borders. In September 2025, Canada designated the Bishnoi gang a terrorist entity. Last month, the FBI added Satinderjeet Singh, popularly known as Goldy Brar, to its Ten Most Wanted list, offering up to $1 million for information leading to his arrest. US prosecutors described him as the alleged leader of Bishnoi's group in North America.

While Bishnoi comes from an affluent landowning family, his shooters are mostly poor. The gang usually recruits them online.

"There are so many youngsters who want to do something… they are just pulled by the lore attached to Lawrence Bishnoi," Singh explained. "Recruiting is no big issue… They offer money, protection, weapons."

In 1998, Salman Khan was accused of killing a blackbuck, an antelope revered by Bishnoi's community. Bishnoi vowed revenge, and many of the attacks targeted people close to the actor, including the 2024 killing of politician Baba Siddique, which police have linked to the gang.

Singh sees the feud as a bid for respectability. Criminals "always try to find something that makes them a moral guardian," he says, and this case was Bishnoi's "chance to redeem himself."

"Bollywood has a lot of money," Singh added, pointing that the previous generation of gangsters was no longer in position to take advantage.

"With Dawood Ibrahim and his counterparts away, there was a vacuum, and Lawrence Bishnoi is trying to exploit that."

Sivanandhan explained the old Mumbai gangs ran a whole "basket" of illegal businesses, from smuggling to protection rackets and cricket match-fixing. Their members were "local boys," while the Bishnoi gang has “nothing to do with Mumbai,” he added. It targets Bollywood because the industry is "loaded with money" and a "soft underbelly."

Meanwhile, Zaidi sees the attacks mainly as publicity. "It's a way to grab headlines," he told DW. Within hours of the recent attack on Shroff, someone claimed on social media that he was targeted for being close to Khan, a logic Zaidi called "absolutely illogical and absurd."

But publicity is useful to gangs that live off extortion. "The base material of extortion is: create fear," a security professional responsible for the protection of several Bollywood stars, who asked not to be named because of the sensitivity of the issue, told DW.

Still, experts say its hard to argue that the ongoing violence is comparable to the 1990s crime wave.

## Embarrassing name? How we live with names we don&apos;t like

https://p.dw.com/p/5OVs4Prefer DW on GoogleFrom childhood bullying to workplace discrimination, awkward names can influence how people are perceived and treatedImage: Dreamstime Demidovic/Dreamstime/IMAGOAdvertisementAs a child growing up in Australia, Louise Pain hated her surname.

"People used to tease me; I was called things like 'Pain in the a*s,'" she told DW. "I remember actively hating my surname when I was a child and wanting to change it."

The jokes have followed her throughout her life, even after she moved to Germany. But one thing did eventually change: Pain's feelings about her own name.

Names are not only a fundamental part of how we identify ourselves — they can, beyond teasing, also influence how other people treat us. So, what happens if we dislike our own name, or if it is perceived negatively by those around us?

"A person's name is vital to their sense of identity," psychoanalyst Mikita Brottman told DW. "When someone has an embarrassing name, that embarrassment can be incorporated into their identity."

Research on name bias has linked negatively perceived names with romantic rejection,as well as lower educational outcomes, lower self-esteem and more frequent smoking.

This suggests that names could influence our life trajectory, namely through self-esteem and opportunity.

But a person's relationship with their name is not necessarily fixed. Pain's lived experience, and that of another woman with the first name Tiny, shows how people can find ways to reclaim, and even come to value, names that once triggered ridicule and rejection.

Names can act as "signals" when you first meet someone, personality psychologist Jochen Gebauer told DW. Those signals may lose importance as people get to know each other, but Gebauer said their effects could also accumulate over a lifetime, potentially contributing to "real measurable advantages and disadvantages."

Research led by sociologist Anthony Lee found that a name can evoke inaccurate assumptions about intelligence, competence, trustworthiness, sociability and attractiveness.
For Pain, other people's reactions have extended beyond jokes. In high school, a teacher refused to believe that 'Pain' was her real surname and reprimanded her in front of the class.

"Your name couldn't possibly be Pain. Write your real name!" she recalled the teacher saying.

In Lee's UK-based research, less familiar names tended to fall along a dimension partly characterized as "weirdness," which may help explain why difficult-to-pronounce or culturally unfamiliar names receive more negative evaluations.

Tiny, now 54, encountered the same questions about her unfamiliar name growing up in Greece.

Her parents had adapted "Tiny" to "Tinie" to make it sound more Greek, but people would repeatedly ask what her real or baptismal name was.

Even relatives have refused to accept it. Recently, two elderly aunts introduced her to someone as "Haritini," a conventional Greek name.

"The jokes [from others] got repetitive and boring pretty fast, so at least I was left with the sense that I have something unique with my name going on."

Pain, now 40, discovered how much cultural context could change the reaction to her surname when she began learning her father's native French as a teenager. In French, 'pain' means bread.

Later, after moving to Germany, the jokes became mostly bread-related rather than the more scathing ones she had experienced in Australia. "That helped me to feel better about my name," she said.

As they got older, both Pain and Tiny developed coping strategies to pre-empt people's reactions to their names.

Tiny sometimes introduces herself with: "Hi, I'm Tiny. It's my name and not a statement," or, "I'm Tiny. Not a difficult name to live up to."

"I was convinced that I should become either a doctor or a dominatrix, or perhaps both, to become either 'Doctor Pain,' or 'Mistress Pain.'"

"Names can conjure that whole spectrum of shame and pride," psychoanalyst Melissa Daum told DW.

Pain now finds that people tend to see her surname as "funny and endearing… it's become something that is more of a funny talking point."

But the childhood discomfort has not entirely disappeared. When Pain thinks about having children, her feelings about the name become more complicated.

But Pain also believes her father would be deeply hurt if future children had a different name. The surname she once hated connects her to him and to her French heritage.

"Something that once feels like an inconvenience or a source of self-consciousness can become a marker of distinctiveness, history, or belonging," Daum said.

"My parents seemingly made an unfortunate choice, but by now I have embraced both the name and the sense of uniqueness that comes with it."

That does not mean she uses Tiny everywhere. She avoids it on dating apps because "Tiny might give off the wrong message," and does not use it in her email address when she wants to appear professional.

Pain, meanwhile, uses a Germanized version of the French word for bread as her social media surname.

## UN peacekeeping mission in Western Sahara to expire amid US, Russia vetoes

https://p.dw.com/p/5OtM2Prefer DW on GoogleFriday's failed US draft resolution would have rebranded the peacekeeping force from MINURSO to MINUSO, dropping the 'R' which refers to referendum on the territory's self-determination.Image: FADEL SENNA/AFPAdvertisementThe fate of the United Nations peacekeeping mission in Western Sahara has been left in a limbo after Russia and the United States vetoed each other's draft resolutions on extending the mission that is over three decades long — just one day before the mandate expires.

The peacekeeping mission, known as MINURSO, was established in 1991 to oversee a settlement plan and ceasefire between Morocco and the pro-independence Polisario Front in a territorial dispute spanning half a century.

The Polisario Front, which is supported by Algeria and represents the indigenous Sahrawi people, seeks to establish an independent state called the Sahrawi Republic.

On Friday, the US tabled a draft resolution at the UN Security Council (UNSC) that would have extended the mandate for one year, while making a Moroccan autonomy plan for Western Sahara the only basis for negotiations to settle the conflict, and cutting the mission's military component.

Last year, the UNSC backed Morocco's autonomy proposal as the "most feasible" solution for the disputed territory.

Friday's failed US draft resolution would have gone further and rebranded the peacekeeping force from MINURSO to MINUSO, dropping the 'R' which refers to referendum on the territory's self-determination.

The US proposal was vetoed by Russia, which slammed it as a "unilateral approach."  A total of 11 of the 15 UNSC members voted in its favor and three abstained.

"We stress the fact that the Security Council cannot automatically pre-determine matters relating to sovereignty,"  Russia's permanent representative to the UN, Vassily Nebenzia, said.

"The Security Council must not give a blessing to such a one-sided approach. Therefore we were compelled to block the adoption of ⁠the draft," ​he added.

The US, with its own veto, then blocked a Russian draft that proposed renewing the mission's mandate for another year while preserving the same terms as before.

"Unfortunately, some members of this Council have used their veto to preserve the letter 'R' for referendum in the mission's name, even though the United Nations has not been preparing to hold such a vote for decades," the US representative to the United Nations, Michael Waltz, said after the vote.

Morocco's royal palace welcomed the failure to renew the peacekeeping mission and said that this "should mark the end of the Security Council's involvement and the removal of this issue from its agenda."

The Polisario Front's UN representative, Sidi Omar, thanked Russia for vetoing the US resolution that he said "would have undermined the free exercise by the Sahrawi people of their inalienable right to self-determination and independence."

## European diplomats shun Russia at G20 gathering in the US

https://p.dw.com/p/5OtFNPrefer DW on GoogleSergey Lavrov, under US sanctions for Russia's war on Ukraine, is photographed while he takes his seat at the G20 meetingImage: Erik S. Lesser/AFPAdvertisementA Group of 20 foreign ministers' meeting in the US city of Atlanta got off to a rocky start on Friday after top diplomats from European nations refused to take a "family photo" along with Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov.

US Secretary of State Marco Rubio, who is hosting the gathering, decided to scrap the photo before the meeting got underway because Washington didn't want it to become a spectacle or distraction.

German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul told reporters before the first session on Friday that there wouldn't "be a family photo with European partners and Mr. Lavrov together because ... there is no normalcy with Russia."

The two-day gathering of the G20 grouping, which includes the EU, Russia, Germany, India, China, Brazil and other major countries, is meant to be a precursor to a G20 leaders summit later this December.

That G20 leaders meeting in December is set to be hosted by US President Donald Trump at his golf club in Miami.

Rubio still opened the talks by stressing unity on major issues, telling his counterparts that they were in a "critical moment."

He pointed to disruptions to global energy and trade and said many were posing "severe threats to essential foundations of the global economic order."

But several countries, including the United States and Russia, are involved in separate wars. Wars in Iran and Ukraine have resulted in a spike in food prices for people around the world.

While the message is about unity and finding solutions to problems like freedom of navigation in the Black Sea and the Strait of Hormuz during the G20 meeting, the reality is that finding common ground will likely be much tougher.

Given a renewed momentum to revive talks to end the war in Ukraine, there was also interest in getting Lavrov to talk about Russia's stance on future talks with Kyiv.

The US has invited Russian President Vladimir Putin to the G20 meeting in Miami, but it's unclear whether Russia has accepted the invitation.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has previously said that he would be open to traveling to Miami to hold talks with Putin if he were also there.

The Kremlin has so far declined direct meetings with Zelenskyy, with Russia's Sergey Lavrov telling DW on the sidelines of the meeting that Putin is willing to meet with the Ukrainian leader if he travels to Moscow.

"President Putin said if he [Zelenskyy] is willing to meet, let him come to Moscow," Lavrov said. "We will guarantee his safety."

Putin has not attended a G20 leaders meeting since 2019 and only attended a session virtually in 2023.

Russia has also continued with its attacks on Ukrainian cities in recent weeks, stepping up drone strikes on water and energy infrastructure.

The European Union's top diplomat, Kaja Kallas, said ministers at the G20 meeting were able to call out Lavrov for his "lies" at the table.

"We were talking about the big geopolitical crises, including Ukraine, and we were able to address Sergey Lavrov head-on with this," Kallas said on the sidelines of the meeting.

"Of course, he put forward a lot of lies, a lot of false narratives that we were also able to then rebuke," she said.

British Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband also called out Lavrov, saying, "If we are serious about our values and our interests, we must also work harder to secure just and lasting peace in Ukraine."

Lavrov, who is under international sanctions for Russia's war in Ukraine, was invited to the US at a time when the two countries have appeared to have fostered warmer ties.

Trump announced a diesel deal with Russia earlier this month and also waived new sanctions on Russia's energy. The US announced the move just weeks away from the midterm elections on November 3.

Lavrov said on Friday that despite those efforts, "in practice, no real progress in relations with the US has so far been seen."

The Trump administration has been embroiled in disputes with South Africa, having imposed visa restrictions on South Africanswho it said were complicit in discriminating against white people.

Ukraine, which is not a G20 member but was invited to attend previous such meetings, was not invited this year because US officials said they made a conscious choice to include only formal members of the group.

But US officials didn't extend that invite to South Africa, a G20 founding member and the only African country in the group.

"We have consistently maintained that our nonparticipation is not voluntary," said Phindile Kunene, spokesperson for South African Foreign Minister Ronald Lamola.

With this many issues on the table, it appeared less likely that there would be a joint consensus at the end of the talks.

Instead of a final declaration, the AFP news agency reported, the meeting is expected to end on Saturday with a chair summary drafted by the US.
