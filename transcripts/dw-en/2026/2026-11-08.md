## At least 21 killed after migrant boat sinks off Senegal

https://p.dw.com/p/5PDGdPrefer DW on GoogleThousands of people attempt to reach Spain's Canary Islands from West Africa through the perilous Atlantic route every yearImage: Jesus Cabrera/REUTERSAdvertisementAt least 21 people were killed after a small boat carrying migrants sank off the coast of northwestern Senegal, Senegalese authorities said.

Thousands have died in recent years after embarking on boats from the Senegalese coast in order to reach Spain's Canary Islands through the perilous Atlantic route.

According to the Senegalese Interior Ministry, only one person was rescued from the capsizing, and 21 bodies were recovered.

The ministry added that the toll was "provisional" and that search operations were still ongoing.

The incident occurred near Kebemer, some 180 kilometers (around 112 miles) north of the capital, Dakar.

On Saturday, the ministry stressed the government's "determination to combat people smuggling networks, notably through strengthened cooperation with neighboring countries."

Senegalese authorities said they intercepted 142 migrants on a similar boat aimed at reaching Spanish territory on Friday. The boat included nationals of multiple West African countries.

Authorities on El Hierro Island in the Canary Islands declared an emergency on Saturday after a marked increase in migrant boat arrivals.

El Hierro officials say that over 3,300 people have arrived on around 30 boats since the start of the month.

"We are very concerned... by this sharp increase in arrivals," Alpidio Armas, head of the El Hierro regional council, told Spanish television.

"This is precisely what has led us to declare an emergency in order to ensure a framework of cooperation with higher-level institutions," he added.

Migrant arrivals reached a record high of 46,000 in 2024. Afterwards, arrivals have fallen sharply due to increased surveillance and cooperation with African countries.

According to the US Central Command (CENTCOM), the cargo vessel "tried to depart regional waters after visiting an Iranian port," before its stern was struck by "precision munitions" shot from a US military aircraft.

In a video posted by CENTCOM, an explosion can be seen on the M/V Huda's stern as a result of the shooting, with the ship's civilian crew ignoring "repeated warnings."

The authorities in the UAE said 22 people had been rescued due to a fire on a vessel in the Gulf of Oman.

The US naval blockade on Iran, intended to pressure the country into lifting its own blockade on the Strait of Hormuz, has been in force for months.

Iran's own blockade of the waterway, through which a fifth of the world's oil and gas supplies flowed before the war started in late February, saw energy prices spike around the world.

Iran's Revolutionary Guards said three "non-compliant" oil tankers caught fire while going through the Strait. Tehran has regularly attacked vessels going through the Strait of Hormuz without its approval.

Elsewhere in the Middle East, the escalation between Iran-backed Houthi rebels in Yemen and Saudi Arabia continued to rage.

Saudi Arabia said it had intercepted incoming missiles shot in the direction of the capital, Riyadh, on Sunday.

Debris from the interception damaged a building belonging to state-owned oil company Aramco. No casualties were reported, but some of the building's floors were heavily damaged, witnesses told AP.

Saudi Arabia accused Yemen-based militant group Houthisof launching two ballistic missiles and two cruise missiles.

While Houthis carried out such attacks in the past, the rebels have not claimed responsibility for the attack yet.

According to the Houthis, 33 people were killed in Saudi-led attacks on Yemen's rebel-held capital, Sanaa, on Saturday.

## Gaza: Rains add misery to humanitarian catastrophe

https://p.dw.com/p/5PCvcPrefer DW on GoogleGaza's displaced people saw heavy rains that soaked tents and mattressesImage: Omar Al-Qattaa/AFPAdvertisementHeavy rains sweeping across parts of the Middle East this week have flooded tens of thousands of tents in Gaza's displaced persons' camps.

According to authorities in the enclave, at least 70,000 tents have been flooded over the past day, where tens of thousands of people took shelter.

Some of the tents have been washed away due to the weather, with mattresses and blankets soaked with water.

More than 1.6 million residents of Gaza's 2.1-million population now live in tents or other makeshift shelters due to Israel's war in the enclave.

UN data says some 80% of the enclave's structures have been damaged during the war, with two-thirds destroyed, leaving Gaza's residents — many of whom were displaced multiple times during the war between Israel and Hamas — to either live in tents or risk their lives and seek shelter in buildings that could be on the verge of collapse.

The Higher Presidential Committee for Church Affairs in Palestine released a statement appealing for humanitarian aid for the Gaza Strip due to the situation.

According to the committee, some 950,000 people are living in areas "vulnerable to flooding," with more than 400,000 living in areas of "high levels of risk."

"Heavy rainfall is compounding an already catastrophic humanitarian situation, leaving displaced families and those who have lost their homes exposed to the elements and deprived of the most basic necessities of life," the statement said.

Some 74,000 people have been killed in the Gaza Strip since October 7, 2023. The war was triggered when Islamist Palestinian organization Hamas and its affiliates launched a terrorist attack in Israel's south, killing some 1,200 Israelis, most of whom were civilians. The militants also kidnapped 251 hostages.

A UN commission of inquiry, as well as many leading human rights organizations, have accused Israel of committing genocide in Gaza. Israel has rejected the accusation, saying its army is acting in accordance with international law.

## &apos;Never Again!&apos;: Holocaust remembrance under pressure

https://p.dw.com/p/5P0GMPrefer DW on GoogleA mural at the Bonn exhibition shows people moments before being sent to the gas chambersImage: Oliver Berg/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementYoung people pose inside a crematorium oven at the Buchenwald concentration camp memorial, laughing while they give the Nazi salute and take pictures of each other. "In a sense, they're trophies to post on social media for them to boast about — breaking the ultimate taboo," Jens-Christian Wagner, director of the Buchenwald and Mittelbau-Dora Memorials Foundation, told DW.

"Incidents like this have become more frequent in recent years. We have seen memorial trees planted in honor of concentration camp victims cut down. When something like this happens — and it happens regularly — I find it particularly despicable because, because it ultimately kills these people a second time," added Wagner.

Wagner is part of the curatorial team behind the exhibition "Never Again! Against Forgetting the Crimes of the Nazi Regime" in Bonn's Bundeskunsthalle, or the Art and Exhibition Hall of the Federal Republic of Germany. Wagner said Holocaust remembrance is under pressure, and the subject of intensifying attacks.

Critics have dismissed remembrance as a "cult of guilt" or "national masochism." With right-wing populism on the rise and historical revisionism becoming more common, Wagner worries about how Germany will remember the victims murdered by the Nazi regime. He is also concerned about the future of democracy. And soon, there will be no Holocaust survivors left to tell the true story of what happened.

This new exhibition tackles this concern head-on with numerous works of art and historical artifacts.

"I hope that perhaps even some people who don't already believe it's important to confront Nazi crimes will find their way to the exhibition," Wagner said. Memorial sites also attract visitors who aren't necessarily convinced of the need to confront these parts of history. Many of them are students.

"Let's not kid ourselves: When a teacher says 'We're going there,' it's always a mandatory visit," he said.

For today's young people, the Nazi era seems like the distant past, Wagner explained. "When you tell them to remember something that their grandparents didn't even experience, it naturally feels like an emotional burden. They can practically see the finger being wagged at them, and that doesn't exactly encourage learning."

A two-hour lecture won't have much of an impact if pupils aren't even paying attention. Instead, Buchenwald organizes full-day programs that allow students to explore historical sources together and make sense of history for themselves.

The next step, Wagner said, is to show young people that this chapter of history isn't closed. Visitors are encouraged to make connections between the past and current events like migration, for example.

"For the longest time, people have been discussing migration almost exclusively in terms of threats to public safety and crime. This broadly marginalizes certain groups — a mechanism that also worked for National Socialism," he said.

Eva Umlauf, 83, knows firsthand what it means to experience exclusion in its most extreme form. She is a patron of the Bonn exhibition.

In November 1944, Umlauf was just 2 years old when she, her pregnant mother and her father were deported by the Nazis from the Novaky labor camp in Slovakia to Auschwitz-Birkenau, the notorious Nazi concentration and extermination camp. She was saved from the gas chambers by mere days; the Nazis stopped using the chambers as the Red Army advanced, hoping to hide evidence of their crimes.

The camp was liberated on January 27, 1945. Three months later, Umlauf's sister Nora was born. But her father, her mother's siblings and her grandparents were all murdered. Eva barely survived herself. She suffered from tuberculosis, rickets and whooping cough, as well as edema caused by starvation. "Forget about the child. She won't survive," a doctor told her mother. But he was wrong.

Her mother returned to what was then Czechoslovakia with her two daughters, settling in Trencin. Eva Umlauf has no conscious memories of Auschwitz, but she carries her family's trauma deep within her.

Her mother never talked about what happened. Even so, Eva knew from an early age that she was a survivor. Her family rarely mentioned the camp by name, but one sentence came up again and again: "They stayed there." Eva knew that meant "they" were dead. And then, there was the number tattooed onto her arm: A26959.

Her mother taught her daughters not to draw attention to their Jewish identity — antisemitism was widespread throughout Czechoslovakia at the time. The small family managed to avoid harassment.

Eva Umlauf wanted to become a pediatrician, so she studied medicine. In the 1960s, she met her future husband, Jakob, who had also survived the Holocaust. He was an architect, active in Munich's Jewish community. They married in 1966, and she moved to Germany. She could hardly have imagined that she would one day live in the country of the perpetrators. "I don't hate Germans," she said. "But I cannot forgive. How can you forgive the deaths of 6 million people?"

It wasn't until much later that she first spoke publicly about the horrors of her early childhood. In 2011, she delivered a commemorative speech in Auschwitz on the anniversary of the camp's liberation. Since 2025, she has served as chair of the International Auschwitz Committee. A heart attack in 2014 motivated her to write her first book. "I was in intensive care. I thought to myself: If I get out of here healthy, I'll write a book."

Her memoir, "The Number on Your Forearm is Blue Like Your Eyes," was first released in 2016. The English translation followed in 2024. Since its publication, hardly a day has passed without someone asking Eva Umlauf to share her story.

She is appalled by the disrespectful behavior of young people at former concentration camps and by growing ignorance about the Holocaust. Sometimes, people even ask if she's too old to have a tattoo. But she also recalls a young man on the Munich subway who noticed the number on her arm and told her: "I want to apologize to you for what my ancestors did to you."

Umlauf never imagined that antisemitism would once again become so widespread in Europe and the United States. Nor did she expect the rise of Germany's far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) party.

"I go into schools. I'm so overwhelmed by the number of requests asking for my availability. And yet, the AfD is growing faster than I can reach students. It makes me think all this work is for nothing," she told DW.

Germany must never again fall under a right-wing dictatorship, she stressed. "'Never again' sounds nice, but all I can say is: It's happening again! Wouldn't 'Here We Go Again: Never Again' make a good title for the exhibition?"

She also shared her message to young people in the exhibition catalog, "Never Again": "You are not responsible for the crimes of your grandparents and great-grandparents. But you are responsible for the future. […] You are the generation that will shape our world. Make it a place where everyone can grow up safely, regardless of their background, faith or skin color."

She continues: "You don't have to be perfect. You just have to stay alert. Take care of your country."

The exhibition "Never Again! Against Forgetting the Crimes of the Nazi Regime" in the Bundeskunsthalle in Bonn runs through May 2, 2027.

## As COP31 Antalya kicks off, can world move beyond big promises?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OFYkPrefer DW on GoogleFinding a path away from polluting fossil fuels like coal, oil and gas will once again be on the agenda at COP this yearImage: Goksel Yapar/AFPAdvertisementWith its pulsing music, flashy images and barrage of special effects, the key message in Antalya's marketing video is clear: Turkey, the host of the 31st edition of the UN climate talks, is cool, stylish, modern — and eco-conscious.

Both President Recep Tayyip Erdogan and his wife, Emine, make an appearance, with Turkey's first lady calling climate change "one of the most visible manifestations of the burden human activity has placed on our planet."

From November 9-20, delegates from nearly every country in the world are coming together in the Mediterranean city of Antalya to chart a way out of the climate crisis. Turkey is the official host, and Australia will lead the negotiations. But the issues they'll confront during those 12 days are far more serious than the showy marketing campaign make them out to be.

Severe drought, extreme heat, wildfires and record-low water levels in Europe's main waterways — summer across the continent was dominated by climate emergencies.

And in late August, a series of flash floods triggered by a rock-ice avalanche in Nepal and Tibet washed away entire villages, killing more than 1,400 people and leaving thousands missing. Climate experts have linked the disaster to melting ice and a warming climate.

Globally, carbon emissions are still rising: the UN has concluded that it is no longer possible to keep global temperature rise to 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 Fahrenheit) above pre-industrial levels, the goal agreed at COP21 in 2015.

The global transition away from polluting fossil fuels like coal, oil and gas will be at the center of attention in Antalya — once again. To date, there's been no consensus on whether and how to transition rapidly away from this main source of global warming.

Despite the setbacks, the world has made some progress thanks to the 2015 Paris Agreement.

David Victor, a climate expert at the University of California San Diego, pointed out that before then, the planet was hurtling toward an average temperature rise of between 3-3.5 C.

If current climate commitments are fully implemented, the latest UN projections show a rise of up to 2.6 C. It's a significant step forward, even though warming of that magnitude will still have worldwide consequences.

"There's no question that we could be doing more," said Victor, though he cautioned against pinning too much on this year's summit.

All agreements at COP need to be unanimous, meaning individual states can quash any sweeping resolutions. Added to that challenge are the ongoing wars, trade conflicts and other economic problems swirling around this year's negotiations.

"It's like a giant Christmas tree in which everybody's hung their different ornaments, and the tree is groaning and more ornaments are being put on it," said Victor. "We have to look at it for what it is: It's a place where people talk about stuff."

Instead of waiting for all COP member states to come to an agreement, Victor sees a better chance for change in what he called "clubs" — smaller alliances of governments and companies working together to advance actual solutions, whether it be in aviation, shipping or electromobility.

"The Paris process is important. It has to go forward," he told DW. "But all the work is going to be done in clubs."

An example of this approach was last April's first ever International Conference on Transitioning Away from Fossil Fuels in Santa Marta, Colombia.

Colombia and the Netherlands invited representatives from 57 countries and key sectors to discuss ways to move the world away from coal, oil and gas, after an agreement on a similar plan failed at the 2025 climate summit due to opposition from several oil-producing states.

With an eye to the ongoing trade conflicts and energy crisis, hosts Turkey and Australia have included green industrialization and the clean energy transition among their aims. One goal is to increase the share of electricity in global energy consumption to 35% by 2035, up from around 20% today.

A recent analysis by the International Energy Agency found accelerating electrification could cut fuel-importing countries' energy bills by more than $400 billion (€357 billion) by 2035, compared to 2025 levels. The idea could appeal to regions dependent on fossil fuel imports — among them Germany and the European Union.

Ahead of COP31, German Environment Minister Carsten Schneider said Germany and the EU wanted to spark new momentum in the "global phaseout of fossil fuels."

It's wouldn't just be good for the climate, but a chance for many countries to boost their economies and reduce dependence on energy imports, added Schneider in a statement.

Oil-producing states like Saudi Arabia, however, aren't interested in deals that will undermine their business model. At recent UN climate conferences, they've repeatedly blocked any final agreements including phaseout plans.

Even China, despite its massive expansion of wind and solar energy over the last 20 years, could be against the idea. The economic superpower traditionally rejects any outside demands, proposals or interference concerning its national decisions on energy policy.

Every country in the world is affected by climate change, with loss and damage calculated in the hundreds of billions from heat, drought and storms. Worst hit are vulnerable countries in the Global South and island nations, who are at risk of disappearing as sea levels rise.

That's where money comes into play, with developing countries calling for more support to protect their citizens from the harms of global warming. At the 2024 UN climate talks in Baku, Azerbaijan, industrialized nations agreed to free up at least $300 billion annually in climate financing by 2035.

But where exactly all those funds will come from remains hotly debated. At the same time, several major developed countries — most notably, the US — have cut their development aid.

The German government only paid out €4.7 billion from its 2025 budget for international climate financing, down from €6.1 billion in 2024.

"The level of financial support for developing countries today is far from what's needed," said Jan Kowalzig, a climate expert at Oxfam Germany.

Breaking financial promises like this comes at the expense of losing the trust of developing countries and, as a result, the diplomatic power, on which Germany and other industrialized nations rely to shape policy to fight climate change.
