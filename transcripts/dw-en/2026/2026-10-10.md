## &apos;Ragebait&apos; is the 2026 German Youth Word of the Year

https://p.dw.com/p/5O1iY'Ragebait' has been selected as the 2026 German Youth Word of the YearImage: Udo Herrmann/imageBROKER/picture allianceAdvertisementThe 2026 Youth Word of the Year, "Ragebait," was announced on Saturday at the Frankfurt Book Fair by publisher PONS Langenscheidt.

The "Jugendwort des Jahres," or German Youth Word of the Year, is an annual campaign that documents the popularity of new slang expressions.

"Ragebait" (in English: rage bait) refers to social media content that is deliberately designed to make people angry, frustrated or morally outraged.

It aims to drive engagement, since the algorithms of platforms like Instagram, TikTok and X reward high-energy interaction. A post that generates a heated debate through angry comments, or that is widely shared due to the emotional reaction it provokes, counts as high engagement, which leads the platforms to push the content to more feeds.

"Ragebait" was on a shortlist of three words for the 2026 Youth Word of the Year, alongside "Peak" and "Crashout."

All three terms are borrowed from English, demonstrating once again the language's significant influence on German youth culture.

Around one million young people, aged 11 to 20, have voted since August 28 for their favorite term.

"Ragebait" obtained 36.5% of the votes, followed by 33.8% for "Peak" and 29.7% for "Crashout."

"The close result shows that all three terms have a firm place in the vocabulary of generations Z and Alpha," said Artur Raiser, marketing manager at PONS Langenscheidt, in a press statement. "'Ragebait' held a slight lead from the start," but only by a narrow margin throughout the voting period, he added. "It remained an exciting race for us at the publishing house, too, until the very last moment."

"Crashout" (used by young Germans as "crashout gehen," or "going crash out") originally comes from the English "to crash out," which traditionally means to fall asleep very quickly due to exhaustion.

But in newer internet slang, popularized on gaming and social media platforms, "crashing out" means having an intense emotional breakdown, losing control of one's temper or reacting with sudden, uncontrolled anger or stress. It is often used with a touch of humor, to emphasize that the reaction might be somewhat exaggerated.

As for the other shortlisted word, "Peak," it refers to something that is the absolute best. In German youth slang, it functions like an adjective: "That was peak football" or "that is peak comedy."

PONS Langenscheidt, a publishing company that specializes in language reference works, has been selecting the German Youth Word of the Year since 2008 — originally via a jury, and since 2020 through an online vote. Offensive, racist or discriminatory terms are not permitted. The multi-stage public nomination and voting process for next year's word will start in the summer of 2027.

## Operations Plan Germany: How the Bundeswehr trains for NATO collective defense

https://p.dw.com/p/5NnZhThe Bundeswehr conducted exercises in HamburgImage: Norbert Schramm/BundeswehrAdvertisementIn a residential district of Hamburg at midnight, the streets are quiet and deserted. Suddenly, the silence is shattered: Several helicopters circle the night sky, and flashing blue lights illuminate the darkness. A long convoy of vehicles moves through the streets. Soldiers stand atop armored Bundeswehr vehicles, their machine guns at the ready. For local residents, it is an unusual, perhaps even unsettling sight in an otherwise peaceful port city.

"Red Storm Charlie" is the name of the exercise that will bring parts of Hamburg, northern Germany's largest city, to a standstill for three days. The Bundeswehr, police, and civilian organizations are jointly testing how well prepared they are to respond in a crisis.

The scenario centers on a deteriorating security situation on NATO's eastern flank. NATO troops, represented in the exercise by Bundeswehr soldiers, arrive at the port of Hamburg, Germany's largest seaport, before moving eastwards through the Hanseatic city.

Acts of sabotage and demonstrations, also included in the scenario, hamper their advance. With around 1,700 participants, including 500 soldiers, the exercise held in late September 2026, was the largest of its kind to date.

"Red Storm Charlie" forms part of a classified strategy known as the "Operationsplan Deutschland" (Operations Plan Germany). Through this document, the Bundeswehr is preparing for the possibility of a Russian attack on NATO territory. The Operations Plan Germany is an integral element of NATO's broader defense planning and is closely aligned with the Alliance's overall defense framework.

In the event of war or a major crisis, not only the city of Hamburg but Germany as a whole would assume a pivotal role as a logistics hub for hundreds of thousands of NATO soldiers, weapons, and pieces of heavy equipment. "This is a highly detailed and strictly classified plan that specifies exactly where NATO allies would arrive in Germany, including which ports they would use and which routes would be required for their transit across the country," security expert Aylin Matlé of the German Council on Foreign Relations (DGAP) told DW.

The Baltic states are widely regarded as the region where NATO considers a Russian attack most likely. In such a scenario, the alliance would concentrate large troop contingents from its 32 member states there. To reach the region, soldiers from southern and western Europe would need to pass through Germany. "In concrete terms, this means that around 800,000 soldiers and 200,000 vehicles would have to move through our country within six months. Those are the calculations," says Marcel Bohnert, a Bundeswehr officer who contributed to the first version of the Operations Plan.

Speaking to DW in a personal capacity rather than as a representative of the Bundeswehr, Bohnert explained: "We must ensure that soldiers can use our roads and ports safely. That is why we take care of all their needs: We arrange accommodation, provide food, ensure their security, and guide them through our transport network."

The plan also covers transport in the opposite direction, from east to west, including the evacuation of wounded soldiers and civilians fleeing the conflict. Its development was commissioned by Bundeswehr Inspector General Carsten Breuer in the wake of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. As Germany's highest-ranking military officer, Breuer is far from alone in his concern that Russia could target additional countries. Germany is already confronting a wide range of Russian hostile activities, from cyberattacks to acts of sabotage directed against the Bundeswehr.

A new dimension of the threat emerged with the attempted attack at Leipzig/Halle Airport in early August. An explosive-laden drone was deployed there and failed to detonate only through luck. The German government holds Russia responsible. "The threat is becoming more tangible. It is serious, acute, and increasingly visible. This is exactly what the Russian president wants. Moscow is testing our response," Defense Minister Boris Pistorius of the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) told the Bundestag on September 23.

The Bundeswehr is also addressing this threat through the Operations Plan Germany, which is continuously being refined and updated. Lessons learned from exercises such as "Red Storm Charlie" are incorporated into the plan. According to Bohnert, the plan also serves as a deterrent: "We hope to discourage Russia from further aggression by demonstrating our strength through the rapid build-up of military capabilities and the highly organized, swift deployment of forces to the borders."

How well prepared is Germany for its role as a military logistics hub? Matlé identifies one major vulnerability in the country's ageing and, in some cases, deteriorating infrastructure. Germany, she argues, must address the maintenance and repair of the bridges, roads, and transport routes required for troop movements "far more quickly and comprehensively." "Some of this infrastructure dates back to the Cold War and was not designed to accommodate heavy loads or military equipment."

A further challenge lies in striking the right balance between confidentiality and public involvement. To guard against acts of sabotage, sensitive details such as the precise transit routes for NATO troops must not fall into Russian hands. At the same time, the plan can only succeed through close cooperation between the Bundeswehr and civilian society, including local and regional authorities, the police, and relief organizations. "Parts of the plan must be publicly accessible because we need support from society, as well as from ministries and governments, to make it work," said Bohnert.

The debate over the confidentiality of the more than 1,000-page document recently resurfaced after the far-right Alternative for Germany(AfD) emerged as the strongest party in recent elections in the eastern German state of Saxony-Anhalt.

Security experts expressed concern that the party, which is classified in part as right-wing extremist and is regarded as Russia-friendly, could pass information from the plan to Moscow if it were to assume the office of state premier. The Defense Ministry rejected these concerns, noting that no federal state is given access to the complete document. Instead, the sensitive sections of the Operations Plan are shared only in excerpts and strictly on a need-to-know basis.

According to observers, however, the growing number of hybrid threats is underscoring the need for secrecy. During the "Red Storm Charlie" exercise in Hamburg, accredited journalists were not informed of routes or locations until the very last moment because of concerns over potential disruptions and acts of sabotage.

## US midterms: Women candidates could make history

https://p.dw.com/p/5O695The US is holding midterm elections on November 3Image: Framed/Pond5 Images/IMAGOAdvertisementAround 51% of the population of the United States is female, but that is not reflected in the make-up of the US Congress, where only 28.4% of the members are women. Nevertheless, with 152 women in total sitting in the House of Representatives and Senate, the number is higher than it has ever been in the history of the United States. The number of female governors of the 50 US states is also currently higher than ever before: Some 14 of them — 28% — are women.

There are a number of reasons why there are still comparatively few female representatives in the US Congress. "Some of them are structural: There are rules and processes in place in our political system that make it harder for any newcomers to break in," says Kelly Dittmar, director of research at the Center for American Women and Politics (CAWP) at Rutgers University. "Historically, women were newcomers."

Seats in the Senate only come up for election every six years. Members of the House of Representatives serve two-year terms, but there is no limit to the number of times they can stand for reelection, so in theory they can stay in office for decades.

Also, as Dittmar points out, election campaigns in the US are very expensive. Women have historically been less able to access networks of rich donors — who, like many party leaders, are still most likely to be men. Leading male politicians are also more likely to recruit men to stand as new candidates. Finally, cultural biases also play a role: "Stereotypes and expectations about what women can and should do."

The elections on November 3 will decide whether more or fewer women will sit in the US Congress over the next two years. All 435 seats in the House of Representatives and 35 of the 100 seats in the Senate are up for election in the midterms. And in 36 states, the citizens will also decide who will move in to the governor's villa.

125 of the 435 representatives in the House are women. As in the Senate, and also with the gubernatorial posts, they are not evenly distributed between the two parties: There are more women among the Democrats than among the Republicans. At present, there are 95 women sitting as representatives for the Democrats, and 30 for the Republicans.

"There is a far less robust support infrastructure for women within the Republican Party," says Dittmar. She comments that organizations that specifically recruit, train, and fund women candidates are almost all either on the Democrat side, or non-partisan. Besides, she adds, "A lot of the [Republican] party leaders don't see an electoral incentive or pressure" to recruit more women. Diversity is more important to Democrat voters, who place greater value on seeing women among their elected representatives.

The Republicans currently have a majority in the House of Representatives, which means that they also get to appoint the Speaker or majority leader. At the moment, the position is held by Mike Johnson. Only one woman has held this powerful post to date: Nancy Pelosi, who was the Democrats' Speaker from 2007 to 2011, and again from 2019 to 2023.

Women currently constitute 27% of the US Senate – 16 Democrats and 11 Republicans. Each of the 50 states is represented by two senators. The term of office is longer in this chamber of the US Congress, and the majority of the officials here represent many more voters than the representatives in the lower house.

According to Dittmar, it's even harder for women to win a seat in the Senate. "With the Senate, it's an opportunity and timing issue," she says. Each seat comes up for election every six years. There are 100 in total, and every two years only a third of them are available. Little opportunity, then, for women who have not previously sat in the Senate to advance. A state-wide senatorial election campaign is also more expensive than the equivalent campaign for a seat in the House of Representatives — and studies indicate that female candidates are generally less wealthy than their male counterparts.

For one senatorial candidate, the November midterms could prove historic. If the Democrat Peggy Flanagan wins in Minnesota, she will be the first Native American woman to sit in the US Senate. Flanagan is Ojibwe, otherwise known as Chippewa, an indigenous people from the Great Lakes region spanning the US and Canada.

Flanagan currently holds the post of lieutenant governor of Minnesota. Polls suggest that her chances are good. She has a slight lead over her rival, the Republican Michele Tafoya, previously a sports reporter and advisor to conservative politicians.

Ten of the current 14 female governors are Democrats, while four are Republicans. Six of the 14 are standing again, including Sarah Huckabee Sanders, the governor of Arkansas, who was President Trump's press secretary during his first term in office.

For the first time in US history, it is possible that a Black woman may be elected state governor. In Minnesota, the Democrat Tim Walz — Kamala Harris's vice-presidential candidate in the 2024 election — is not standing for reelection. The main rivals for his seat are the Democrat Amy Klobuchar and the (Black) Republican Lisa DeMuth. Meanwhile, the Black Democrat Keisha Lance Bottoms is running for governor of the state of Georgia.

Kelly Dittmar doesn't think DeMuth will win in Minnesota — the state is regarded as solidly Democrat. And Klobuchar is well known among the people there; she has represented Minnesota in the US Senate since 2007.

But in Georgia, according to Dittmar, it's a different story: "That race is considered a toss-up, 50-50." Dittmar believes that Bottoms "absolutely has a chance to be the first Black woman governor." Former President Barack Obama is her most prominent supporter, while President Trump has declared his support for the Republican candidate, Rick Jackson.

## Taiwan to boost military spending to &apos;deter war&apos; with China

https://p.dw.com/p/5O6BmPresident Lai Ching-te said his country cherishes peace but will not give up freedom in his National Day addressImage: Ann Wang/REUTERSAdvertisementTaiwan plans to boost defense spending to deter war, President Lai Ching-te said on Saturday, as China steps up military pressure to assert its claim over the self-governed island.

Lai's government has allocated a record NT$1.1 trillion ($34.5 billion) for defense spending in 2027, equivalent to more than 3% of GDP.

Taiwan's 23 million people face the threat of a Chinese attack. Beijing claims the island as part of its territory and has not ruled out using force to bring it under its control.

"Our aim in increasing investment in national defence is not to provoke, but to deter war," President Lai Ching-te said in a speech marking Taiwan's National Day.

Successive governments have increased military spending as Taiwan strengthens its defenses against a potential Chinese invasion and seeks to demonstrate to the United States its commitment to defending itself.

"We do not seek hegemony, nor do we seek to dominate others," Lai said in his annual address outside the Presidential Office in Taipei, attended by thousands of people, including foreign diplomats.

He warned that peace could not be taken for granted, accusing China of using military and grey-zone activities to change the status quo across the Taiwan Strait.

Lai welcomed talks between the United States and China but stressed that Beijing must respect "the right of the people of Taiwan to exist."

Officials in Washington and Taipei have repeatedly said US policy on Taiwan remains unchanged under President Donald Trump, although some of his remarks have raised concerns in Taipei.

Trump raised concerns in May when he suggested that US arms sales to Taiwan could be used as a bargaining chip in negotiations with Chinese President Xi Jinping, following their meeting in Beijing.

Taiwan is awaiting Trump's approval of $14 billion in weapons sales, including the US-made Patriot air defense system.

At a high-profile summit between US President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping in Washington in September, trade and artificial intelligence dominated the agenda. One of the most contentious issues, Taiwan, was discussed only behind closed doors.

Ahead of Lai's speech, Taiwanese fighter jets and two Chinook helicopters carrying giant Taiwan flags flew over the capital.

Facing China's much larger military, which continues to expand its arsenal with advanced weapons, including stealth fighter jets, aircraft carriers and a vast array of missiles,  Taiwan is increasing defence spending and modernising its armed forces.

China's Taiwan Affairs Office did not immediately respond to a request for comment on Lai's speech. Beijing has never renounced the use of force to bring Taiwan under its control, calls Lai a "separatist" and has rejected his offers of talks.

Democratically governed Taiwan faces growing military and political pressure from China, which claims the island as its territory despite strong objections from the government in Taipei.

The United States welcomed Lai's commitment to maintaining peace and stability across the Taiwan Strait. A senior US administration official said that, referring to China, "a routine speech is no justification for aggressive or coercive actions."

The United States switched official diplomatic relations from Taipei to Beijing nearly 50 years ago, but Washington is Taipei's most important security backer.

Saturday's celebrations marked the 115th anniversary of the 1911 revolution that toppled China's Qing dynasty and paved the way for the founding of the Republic of China.

The dispute between China and Taiwan dates back to the Chinese civil war, when nationalist leader Chiang Kai-shek fled to the island in 1949 after his forces were defeated by Mao Zedong's communists.

## Is China seeking to restrict Taiwan&apos;s cultural presence?

https://p.dw.com/p/5O2RX'Taiwan Travelogue' follows a young Japanese writer visiting Japanese-colonized Taiwan in May 1938 on an official government-sponsored lecture tourImage: Pete Speller/Avalon/IMAGOAdvertisementWhen Taiwaneseauthor Yang Shuang-zi's "Taiwan Travelogue" won the 2026 International Booker Prize, it marked a historic breakthrough on the global stage. But behind the triumph lay a sharp geopolitical reality.

On stage, Lin King, who translated the book into English, gave voice to the anxiety looming over Taiwan, where she spent a large part of her childhood.

"In 2022, when Russia invaded Ukraine, I made the explicit decision that rather than translating Sinophone works indiscriminately, I would only translate writing from Taiwan for the foreseeable future," she said in her acceptance speech. "I will continue to do so until the day comes that my homeland's sovereignty is no longer a provocation or a punchline — until no English speaker feels comfortable saying to me, 'I really should come visit you in Taiwan while it still exists.'"

"Taiwan Travelogue" follows a young Japanese writer visiting Japanese-colonized Taiwan in May 1938 on an official government-sponsored lecture tour. As she and her Taiwanese interpreter explore local street food together, a subtle intimacy unfolds between them. The narrative centers Taiwan's history rather than Beijing's preferred "Greater China" framework.

"Literature and politics are directly connected," Yang told DW. "Politics determines the allocation of resources, and the allocation of resources determines which works get pulled out, held up high and defined as 'good literature.' The idea that politics can just be kept out of it doesn't exist."

A few days after the 2026 International Booker Prize was announced, China's Taiwan Affairs Office reacted by saying: "Writers from both sides of the Taiwan Strait should firmly uphold a national standpoint, respect historical facts, and squarely face the immense suffering inflicted upon the Chinese people ... by Japan's war of aggression and fascism."

"From China's perspective, they do not want to see Taiwan's voice gain prominence." Yang said.

Her book demonstrates that Taiwanese culture is shaped by multiple historical strands — including indigenous, Japanese colonial and local Han influences, rather than being merely a "branch of Chinese culture."

During Yang's German book tour this October, an event at the Literaturhaus in Munich sparked controversy when the writer's interpreter significantly shortened certain statements concerning Taiwan, China, history, and politics. The interpreter later admitted that there had been oversights and shortcomings in the translation but denied deliberately deleting content due to their political stance or the situation in Taiwan.

Beijing claims Taiwan as a Chinese province and has long threatened to use force to bring the island under its control. Taiwan's democratically elected president has rejected Beijing's assertion that its laws apply to Taiwan.

Since 1949, Taiwan has functioned with de facto independence, maintaining its own government, military, and legal systems while facing ongoing military pressure from Beijing.

China's monitoring of Taiwanese international engagement reaches far further than words over a literature prize.

"The target of suppression and harassment primarily include outspoken individuals, exiled activists, diaspora community organizers, alongside their family members and close affiliates back home," said Dr. Yan-ho Lai, senior fellow of Georgetown Center for Asian Law. "From the PRC's (People's Republic of China) eyes, these groups are a threat to regime security, as they are able to amplify the dissenting voices and alternative stories of China to the West."

In April this year, Taiwanese barista Lin Shao-hsing, also known as Bala, won the 2026 World Latte Art Championship in the United States. Soon after, all mention of Taiwan as it related to Bala's win was removed from the official World Coffee Championships website.

It now says that Bala was representing "the competition body of Chinese Taipei," a term often used in international contests, such as the Olympics, to refer to Taiwan, but almost never used in Taiwan itself.

According to a 2026 poll by National Chengchi University's Election Study Center, the vast majority of people in Taiwan identify as "Taiwanese," while only 2.5% identify as Chinese.

In 2024, China allegedly planned to stage a "car collision" during Taiwanese Vice President Hsiao Bi-khim's visit to the Czech capital Prague. Czech intelligence officials told local media that the incident had been part of a much larger and escalatory plan by Chinese diplomats and intelligence officers working out of the Chinese embassy in Prague.

In early October this year, the FBI said authorities arrested a woman in California for allegedly spying on the son of Taiwan's president.

According to the US Department of Justice, the woman had traveled to Seattle in 2025 under instructions from Beijing to monitor the Taiwanese president's son and his family, capturing photos, video footage, and license plate information.

During a press conference on October 6, China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs said it "consistently opposes the hype surrounding so-called 'Chinese spies' and the smearing and vilification of China."

"It reflects the ambiguity and limitation of international law, that the PRC using soft power to restrict the visibility of Taiwan may not directly fall into international law arena, as Taiwan is not recognized by international bodies as a sovereign state party." said Dr. Lai.

## How the Houthi-Saudi conflict is affecting countries on Africa&apos;s Red Sea coast — and beyond

https://p.dw.com/p/5NwjQA Yemeni soldier pictured patrolling the Red Sea in AprilImage: Abdulnasser Alseddik/AP Photo/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementIn September, one of the major conflicts in the Middle East came within sight of Africa, as Iran-backed Houthi rebels captured Yemen's Perim Island.

The island forms the narrowest point of the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, which connects the southern entrance of the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden. It is roughly 20 kilometers (12 miles) from Djibouti and 20-minutes by air from Ethiopia.

In early October, Yemen's armed forces and their Saudi allies launched a counteroffensive. The Iran-backed Houthis have been firing on merchant ships since the start of the Gaza war in 2023.

African economies are already suffering from crisis in the Strait of Hormuz, which is making fuel and fertilizer scarce and more expensive.

If the Bab el-Mandeb Strait also becomes impassable, the situation could deteriorate further. Shipping companies would have to make detours via the Suez Canal, which is controlled by Egypt, or the southern tip of Africa, as some already do. The additional costs would be passed on to consumers.

The human cost of the escalation in Yemen hit Djibouti almost immediately. According to the UN Refugee Agency (UNHCR), at least 3,700 refugees have crossed from Yemen into Djibouti since September. Most of those who made it to the country's northern Obock region are women and children.

The UNHCR is preparing to assist another 10,000 people who have not yet crossed into Djibouti. But Obock, which is on the border with Eritrea, is extremely dry and this is causing other issues.

"We don't have much water here. There's no groundwater at all," Alessandra Roccasalvo, the UN Development Program (UNDP) representative for Djibouti, told DW. "Everything has to be desalinated."

Despite its small size, Djibouti — a Muslim-majority country at the Horn of Africa with a population of just over a million — is a major player in the region. Its position at the entrance to the Red Sea makes it an attractive commercial and military shipping hub.

The United States, China, France, Italy, and Japan all operate military bases in Djibouti. The German Bundeswehr was also present until 2021, as part of a naval mission to combat maritime piracy.

The country's civilian ports serve as transshipment, or intermediate hubs for global shipping cargo, goods and containers. So far, the Houthi advance has not led to empty docks in Djibouti.

But there are still potential consequences, said Roccasalvo: "Basically, there's an economic upswing here — which means, as in any country experiencing an upswing, building materials are needed: cement, raw materials, and other things."

Less of that money is now coming in because ships have to pay nearly twice as much for insurance and reinsurance as they used to. "Many ships no longer want to pass through the Suez Canal. Many no longer want to pass through the Red Sea."

Ethiopia — the world's most populous landlocked country — handles about 95% of its imports and exports through Djibouti's ports. Price hikes resulting from higher shipping insurance premiums to cover for potential war-related loss are affecting the country greatly.

Shortly after the Houthis seized Perim, Ethiopia's economy faced another, even more immediate threat: On September 23, fighting broke out in the country's northern Tigray region.

Since then, the Ethiopian National Defense Forces (ENDF) have reportedly made gains against the Tigray-led rebel coalition that aims to overthrow the national government. The rebels briefly advanced toward the main corridor used for transporting goods between Djibouti's ports and the Ethiopian hinterland.

According to certain experts, the ENDF's advances have since reduced the threat of yet another trade blockade — but as long as the conflict remains unresolved, the situation could potentially deteriorate again.

For months now, Ethiopia has suffered more than almost any other African country from the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz and has already had to ration fuel at times. In the long term, the country aims to reduce its dependence on imports, for example by powering vehicles with domestically generated electricity instead of imported gasoline.

Ethiopia and its northern neighbor, Eritrea, have severed diplomatic ties; other regional powers such as Sudan, Egypt, and Somalia may find themselves siding with Eritrea rather than with Ethiopia.

Eritrea sees itself as facing a threat that Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed has reiterated on several occasions: With Ethiopia deprived of access to the sea since Eritrea's independence, Abiy has often alluded to rectifying this perceived injustice — either through a political agreement, as was once sought with Somalia's breakaway region of Somaliland, or by force.

The port of Assab is located on the edge of Eritrea's panhandle — and is often described as the most likely target should Abiy choose the path of aggression. If the fighting within Ethiopia further escalates into a full-blown regional war, this could also lead to additional restrictions on safe passage through the Bab el-Mandeb Strait and the surrounding waters.

Experts have also raised concerns about the Shiite Houthis increasingly collaborating with the Sunni terrorist group Al-Shabab, which is based in Somalia. Although their ideologies are fundamentally different, they are particularly united by their hostility toward Israel and the US.

The three African non-permanent members of the UN Security Council — the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Liberia, and Somalia — held an emergency meeting in mid-September, jointly calling for free shipping lanes and endorsing the demand that the crisis in Yemen be resolved under the auspices of the UN.

The situation at Bab el-Mandeb and in northern Ethiopia once again demands flexibility from Africa. In fact, some African economies could also benefit from relocation activities: Ports inKenya and Tanzania, for example, are becoming increasingly attractive for transshipment.

And while many countries are suffering from high oil prices, Nigeria's Dangote Group has announced an initial public offering and a new refinery in Kenya.
