## Despite Russia impasse, Germany still hoping to host Men&apos;s Ice Hockey World Championship

https://p.dw.com/p/5PAJcPrefer DW on GoogleSkoda has long been the main sponsor of the Men's World Ice Hockey ChampionshipImage: Vit Simanek/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementA German government spokesman has said that despite talks with the German Ice Hockey Federation (DEB), the International Ice Hockey Federation (DEB) and others, the impasse caused by last week's Court of Arbitration for Sport (CAS) ruling remains.

Since last Friday's decision, several leading ice hockey nations, including Finland, Sweden and Czechia have said they would refrain from playing against Russia in any tournament in which it is allowed to take part. Several key sponsors have also threatened to pull out, should the Russians be allowed to play.

The first tournament to be affected would be the Men's World Juniors, which opens in Canada late next month, followed by the IIHF's premier event, the Men's World Championship, to be hosted by Germany in May.

German government spokesman Stefan Kornelius told reporters in Berlin on Friday that talks held with various stakeholders had made clear that Russian participation would render the hosting of the World Championship "practically impossible, both from a sporting and an economic perspective."

However, he also said talks would continue and the government still hoped for a diplomatic solution that would allow Germany to host the World Championship – as planned – in Gelsenkirchen, Mannheim and Düsseldorf next May.

"Our goal must be to hold the tournaments as scheduled. However, given the boycotts that would follow Russia's readmission, that would effectively be impossible. For this reason, we intend to explore—through dialogue—how we can still ensure the tournament takes place," he said.

Following last Friday's CAS decision ruling that the IIHF must allow Russia to return to international play, DEB chairman Frank Lutz had conceded that Germany may be forced to consider withdrawing as World Championship host.

Asked at Friday's press conference whether Germany could issue visas to a Russian team for the tournament, a German interior ministry spokesman declined to answer categorically, but did indicate that this would be difficult due to restrictions currently in place.

"At a time when Russia is waging a war of aggression against Ukraine, there can be no 'normalcy'—and we will certainly not pretend that there is," Martin Giese said.

Earlier this week, Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney categorically ruled out Russian players taking part in the World Juniors in Alberta.

"While Russia and Belarus are supporting this illegal, unjustified ​war in Ukraine, as long as that's going on, there will be none of those athletes competing for their countries in Canada," he said.

"So, ​if you're asking specifically about the World Juniors, the Russians will not be there."

The IIHF Council had originally called two days of meetings, on Monday and Tuesday to try to reach a consensus on how to respond to the CAS decision – before extending discussions for a third day, on Thursday. By Friday, its members had evidently still not reached a consensus.

The aircraft came down at around 2:48 p.m. local time (1348 GMT/UTC) between the town of Burg and the Krahenberge highway rest area.

## Fact check: Three viral Iran war posts debunked

https://p.dw.com/p/5P797Prefer DW on GoogleDisinformation narratives are often triggered by real news events, such as the latest Iran war escalationImage: U.S. Central Command/Handout/REUTERSAdvertisement"The reason we lost is the war," US Vice President JD Vance said, shortly after Republicans suffered major losses during the US midterm elections in both the House and Senate. He was referring to the US-Israel war against Iran.

Hours before the US midterms, the US reportedly struck Iran's Qeshm Island, despite Trump previously saying that there would be no new strikes before the elections.

Among fears of a renewed regional escalation, social media has been flooded with dramatic images and purported battlefield updates. Not all of them are genuine.

Claim: In this post on X, a user claims that Iran launched a massive missile and drone attack on a US aircraft carrier, resulting in fatalities. It is accompanied by a video showing an explosion and its aftermath on what appears to be an aircraft carrier. The post has garnered over 643.000 views.

Although there have been reports of Iranian forces attempting to shoot down a US aircraft, there have been no credible reports of a successful attack on a US aircraft carrier which resulted in fatalities.

The AI video also predates the latest US strikes — it has circulated at least since May this year. At that time, it was shared alongside a different claim unrelated to the current situation.

Frame-by-frame analysis reveals several inconsistencies. At approximately three seconds in, the feet of a running person visually glitch. The sea in the background appears uniform and almost video game-like. At five seconds, several people appear to pass through one another, and their limbs also glitch.

The voices in the audio sound synthetic and appear closely synchronized with the visuals. Both AI Detection tools by Meta AI and Google Gemini identified strong indicators that the video was AI-generated. It is important to note, however, that such tools provide an assessment of likelihood, rather than definitive proof that something was created using AI.

The video is considerably shaky and short, two characteristics that warrant closer scrutiny of possible AI use.

The visual nature of supposedly chaotically filmed videos can cover up glitches that still happen frequently in AI-generated content.

Claim: This user on X shares a video with the claim that "Iran attacks the US bases in the Gulf." A burning building is visible with large dark plumes of smoke rising from it. It has been viewed over 55.000 times.

A reverse image search shows that the images are recycled and show a fire in South Korea in 2025.

News reports indicated that a fire broke out at a Fashion Logistics Center in Cheonan. The same building is visible in the street-level view of South Korea’s Naver Maps street imagery, and its location matches the red building in a mountainous area which is shown on Google Maps satellite imagery.

Visual cues that match include the building itself, the white lettering "E-Land fashion" on the right side and the mountains in the background.

The reverse image search also reveals that the video has repeatedly circulated. It made rounds in July this year attached to Iran war posts in Tamil and English with one post on X reaching 1.4 million views. Other posts linked it  to the war in Yemenand other conflicts, unrelated to the fire in South Korea.

Claim: "Iran has launched a major hypersonic missile attack targeting multiple Israeli military bases," an X account  writes. By the time of writing, the post had 2.5 million views.

Iran claims to have  hypersonic missiles and has previously claimed to have fired Fattah-1 missiles at Israel, most recently in June 2025.  There are no credible reports about Iranian hypersonic missile attacks during the latest escalation.

The account that has published the claim has over 100.000 followers and posts with high frequency, often over ten times a day. Most of the posts reach hundred thousands of views, often using urgent "Breaking" headlines and repeating similar posts. In one post for example, it claims "the war has officially started" reaching more than 30 million views.  These are all hallmarks of a so-called "content farm."

In a comment to DW, analyst Charlene Lin from the internet watchdog NewsGuard writes that this account is a South-Asia based content farm. The account has already been flagged in relation to other disinformation narratives.

Lin writes that such South Asia-based content farms "seem to spread false claims about geopolitics by typically posing as pro-China/Russia/Iran users."

She adds that the account amplifies false claims alongside other accounts that appear to be part of the same network, typically within a short period of time.

Victoire Rio, an expert on social-media monetization and information integrity, wrote to DW that "it's pretty common to see financially-motivated actors from South and Southeast Asia leverage prominent news issues to generate a financial profit off the engagement on platforms like X."

## Protests and reforms: France&apos;s rising debt costs threaten stability

https://p.dw.com/p/5P6hiPrefer DW on GoogleThe French government is facing not only protests over the rising cost of living but is also grappling with mounting debt costsImage: Lisi Niesner/REUTERSAdvertisementDeeply indebted, politically trapped in a standoff between extremes and led by a government without a parliamentary majority: These are the defining features of France's current predicament.

If, in response to nationwide protests, the government plans to provide €4 billion ($4.48 billion) in low-interest loans to renovate schools by 2030 and allocate a further €1 billion to universities by 2029, it is promising money the state simply does not have.

France's public debt stands at around 120% of gross domestic product (GDP). By comparison, Germany's debt-to-GDP ratio is roughly 65%, despite its €600 billion investment package for infrastructure and defense.

Germany still enjoys the highest possible AAA credit rating. France, by contrast, is rated AA, A+ or Aa3, depending on the credit agency. While Germany's outlook is consistently rated as stable, several agencies have assigned France a negative outlook.

As a result, the French government must pay higher interest rates on its borrowing than Berlin. The risk premium over German government bonds, known as the spread, is now at its highest level since the financial crisis. France pays nearly 1.6 percentage points more in interest than Germany on 10-year bonds.

The spread acts as a kind of fever gauge for bond markets, explains Friedrich Heinemann, an economist at the Leibniz Center for European Economic Research (ZEW) in Mannheim.

"This fever gauge then forces a change of course. If the spread rises too sharply, France's financing conditions come under pressure, and anyone in France who needs a loan has to pay higher interest rates. Banks then get into trouble, and the crisis escalates further," Heinemann told DW.

"This could then slide towards a financial crisis, which would finally send a clear signal to everyone: Things cannot continue like this unless you are prepared to risk a genuine economic collapse," he explained.

Landry Charrier, a researcher at the University of Bonn's Center for Advanced Security, Strategic and Integration Studies, however, does not expect France's debt-fuelled spending spree to end anytime soon.

"French people are aware of the country's high public debt. But debt remains an abstract concept if its consequences are barely felt in everyday life. According to INSEE (the French national statistics agency — the ed.), 56% of the population receive more in government benefits and services than they pay in taxes and social contributions. The majority therefore have a tangible interest in maintaining the status quo," Charrier told DW.

For more than half a century, France has regularly spent more than it collects in tax revenue. The last time it balanced its national budget was in 1974.

France last paid a similarly high-risk premium at the end of 2011, during the eurozone debt crisis. What is particularly striking is how quickly it has risen: Since June 2026, the spread over German government bonds has more than doubled.

The main driver is not so much the general level of interest rates. Rather, it's the growing risk premium investors demand in response to high debt levels, political instability, large budget deficits and the recent unrest.

The higher the interest rates, the larger the share of the national budget that must be devoted to debt servicing alone. France is expected to pay more than €79 billion in interest on its total public debt in 2026. That figure is projected to exceed €91 billion in 2027 and could reach between €125 billion and €132 billion by 2030.

These figures include the costs incurred by social security funds and local authorities. The central government's interest bill alone is currently estimated at €62.6 billion by the Agence France Tresor, a French Treasury agency.

In the coming years, France will have to refinance large amounts of outstanding bonds that were issued when interest rates were at historic lows. At the same time, the country's substantial budget deficit means it must take on additional debt.

The trend of rising interest costs is also affecting Germany. The ZEW's Heinemann has calculated that German interest payments are likely to increase sharply by the early 2030s.

"For the federal government alone, which has so far paid around €30 billion, the figure will triple within a few years as both debt and interest rates rise. And it won't stop there — it will exceed €90 billion. Reaching €100 billion in annual interest payments in the federal budget is then no longer far off," warns the Mannheim-based economist.

In France, however, economic realities seem to have little impact on political debate. Both the far-right National Rally (RN) and the far-left La France insoumise (LFI) are campaigning on costly promises.

RN leader Marine Le Pen, for example, wants to reinstate the retirement age of 62 while promising €140 billion in savings. Jean-Luc Melenchon, LFI's leader, has even suggested that government bonds held by the French central bank should be "thrown into the fire." "Nobody will ever notice they've disappeared," he claims.

For Heinemann, this is "a good example of radical parties on both the right and the left simply telling economic fairy tales." Their common message, he says, is that "there is a way out of this mess that requires neither fiscal consolidation nor painful cuts."

Charrier is convinced that many French people have yet to grasp the seriousness of the situation.

"The euro has made it easier for France to access cheap financing and has long softened the pressure to adjust," the expert points out.

"In the past, currency devaluations (of the French franc — the ed.) made economic imbalances immediately felt through declining purchasing power and rising prices. Today, that warning signal is missing. This has created the illusion that necessary adjustments can be postponed indefinitely," Charrier said.

## Czech arms makers set sights on space defense in bid to sustain Ukraine-driven arms boom

https://p.dw.com/p/5P9P9Prefer DW on GoogleRussia's war in Ukraine has driven major growth across the Czech defense industry as a wholeImage: Dalibor Gluck/CTK Photo/IMAGOAdvertisementThe Czechoslovak Group (CSG) arms maker announced in mid-October that it has acquired a stake in Canadian space technology manufacturer MDA Space.

This space mission is part of the company's bid to continue riding the boom that has transformed its fortunes in recent years.

When it listed on the Amsterdam Stock Exchange earlier this year, CSG soared to become the most valuable company in Central and Eastern Europe, with a market capitalization of €14.6 billion ($16.4 billion), propelling its owner, Michal Strnad, to the top of the region's rich list.

CSG began life as a 1990s startup, selling military surplus amid the collapse of the Warsaw Pact, the military alliance between the Soviet Union and Europe's socialist Eastern Bloc nations, which was founded in 1955 and disbanded in 1991.

It has since grown to become the second-largest producer of ammunition in Europe after Germany's Rheinmetall and now produces across Europe and in the US.

The main driver of the company's rising fortunes has been Russia's war in Ukraine, which has driven a dramatic boom across the Czech defense industry as a whole.

In 2021, the sector's exports totaled €600 million. In 2025, these exports had expanded to €4.7 billion, powered by orders placed by Kyiv and its European allies.

By the end of that year, European states had procured approximately €11 billion worth of military support for Ukraine from Czech defense firms.

Orders to the value of roughly €4 billion have arrived via the Czech Ammunition Initiative, a program that coordinates the transfer of artillery shells to Ukraine.

Amid the avalanche of orders sparked by Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, CSG and Czech peers such as STV, Colt CZ and PBS have been busy expanding production to pump out military vehicles and planes, guns, grenades, shells and bullets.

"The war in Ukraine, particularly in its early stages, was especially well suited to Czech firms," Krzysytof Debiec of the Centre for Eastern Studies (OSW) told DW.

Debiec says that this was largely due to the major role that the former Czechoslovakia played in supplying Warsaw Pact countries.

"Specialization in the production of ammunition — including Soviet calibers — the repair of Soviet-era equipment and the manufacture of relatively unsophisticated systems that could be rapidly introduced and were compatible with the Ukrainian army's existing stocks," have all been key, he explained.

At the same time, European states eyeing the threat from the east and broader geopolitical instability are boosting their own defense budgets.

"The shift in US policies vis-a-vis NATO is forcing European nations to increase their defense spending dramatically, and that's another factor" in the Czech defense industry boom, Jan Jires, a former deputy defense minister and now a senior advisor at the International Institute for Strategic Studies, told DW.

However, since launching on the stock exchange, CSG's share price has performed erratically, illustrating the shifting sands on which Czechia's booming defense industry now stands.

The government in Prague has responded to the sector's surge by transferring the defense industry portfolio from the Ministry of Defense to the Ministry of Industry and Trade to align its growing role in exports with national economic and trade policy.

At the same time, the populist ruling coalition that took power a year ago is made up of parties that have either promised to lower support for Ukraine or — in the case of the far-right Freedom and Direct Democracy party (SPD) — are openly pro-Russian.

As a result, the coalition has halted contributions to the Czech Ammunition Initiative and is dragging its feet on raising defense spending to meet NATO targets.

This only limits an already small domestic market, leaving the industry dependent on exports for about 90% of its revenue — the vast bulk of which is still linked to Ukraine — and lacking a stable base for expansion.

"The Czech industry has outgrown the country's financial means," said Jires. "Unlike German, French or British companies, Czech manufacturers cannot rely on domestic demand to make them globally relevant."

Increased global defense spending looks set to continue, but an end to Russia's war in Ukraine, a change in US foreign policy or a calmer geopolitical environment would hit Czech arms manufacturers hard. For this reason, they must ponder what the future of war, and its funding, might look like.

The conflict in Ukraine offers some indicators, having become increasingly focused on swarms of drones and a technological race between Kyiv and Moscow to improve the performance of the unmanned aircraft.

Major questions must now be asked regarding "the size and role of the military, how it is equipped, what should be purchased and when new weaponry can arrive" Andor Sandor, a defense analyst and former commander of Czechia's Military Intelligence Service, told DW.

For a company like CSG, for which bullets and shells currently account for around 60% of sales, these are vital questions.

"Czech companies — many of which still focus on relatively unsophisticated military production — must invest more in innovative manufacturing," said Debiec, who notes that most are well aware of the challenge. "They have reinvested their extraordinary profits and are already more technologically advanced and diversified in their production than they were in 2022."

The company, which already held interests in British and German space technology companies, pointed to the close link between the space and defense industries when it announced the deal.

Noting that global government spending on space defense accelerated to €65.83 billion last year, CSG estimates that the overall space economy will triple in size to €1.6 trillion over the next decade.

Developing capabilities in space is "a natural extension" of its activities, the company stated, noting plans for further development in the segment, including a "dedicated space team."

To power such missions, Czech defense companies must make sure they're in a position to tap into funds for defense and space under the next EU budget, which could rise as high as €130 billion, said Jires.

## Zambia&apos;s civil service overhaul raises questions

https://p.dw.com/p/5P9MqPrefer DW on GoogleOpposition lawmakers have questioned the retirements and dismissals taking place across public institutionsImage: Han Xu/Xinhua/picture allianceAdvertisementMany people living in Zambia seek careers in the civil service because of the stability and security traditionally associated with government employment. But recent retirements, dismissals and restructuring exercises across several public institutions have unsettled that perception and sparked a national debate about the government's use of "national interest" provisions.

Among those affected is former public relations officer Godfrey Chilabi, whose departure from the Zambia Police Service was announced in October. Choosing not to publicly discuss the circumstances surrounding his exit, Chilabi simply told media that he had been retired in the national interest.

"Yes, I have been retired in national interest," Chilabi said. "I can't give any reason, but know I'm in retirement."

Chilabi's departure came amid a series of changes affecting public institutions in Zambia.

Charity Munganga, a Zambia Police Service deputy inspector general, was dismissed, and all staff members at the Zambia Medicines and Medical Supplies Agency were let go as part of a "reset" following a government audit.

Additionally, all directors in senior management at the Zambia Environmental Management Agency (ZEMA) were put on administrative leave to facilitate an institutional realignment exercise.

The affected ZEMA directors will be assessed for their competence, performance and suitability for their positions before a final decision is made on whether they will be reinstated or separated from the institution, according to an October 20 statement by the Green Economy and Environment Ministry cited by the news portal Mwebantu.

The issue moved to the center of public debate in October, when opposition lawmakers questioned the government in parliament over retirements and dismissals taking place across public institutions following Zambia's general election in August.

Governance and political commentator Andrew Nguvu told DW that retirements carried out in the national interest raise important questions about transparency, fairness and the protection of a professional public service.

"If at all, there are legitimate reasons for retiring any particular civil servant, then obviously the government should clearly explain," Nguvu said.

He said public institutions should operate according to established procedures and the law rather than political considerations that could undermine institutional independence.

Zambian law allows service commissions to retire established officers in the national interest under provisions governing public service employment. Previous governments have also used the measure. However, the current exercise has generated significant scrutiny because it is occurring alongside broader restructuring efforts affecting multiple institutions.

Critics argue that the issue is particularly sensitive because Zambia's ruling United Party for National Development (UPND) previously opposed the use of retirements in the national interest while in opposition.

As a result, opposition lawmakers, led by Stephen Kampyongo, have criticized the measures, which they view as inconsistent with the ruling party's earlier position.

Kampyongo, speaking during Vice President's Question Time in the National Assembly on October 17, asked what assurances the government was giving public servants, who are "working in fear," and what measures were being taken to ensure that government workers retired in the national interest did not become destitute.

Responding on behalf of the government, Vice President Mutale Nalumango rejected suggestions that the exercise amounted to a political purge and described it as part of a lawful process of institutional realignment.

Nalumango acknowledged the concerns surrounding the changes but defended the government's authority to make personnel adjustments within the public service framework.

"As provided for in section 39 of the Public Service Pensions Act, there are situations that arise and people fall by the way side," Nalumango told the parliament. "[It is] unfortunate that a life was lost, but maybe it is an issue that public servants/public service workers must brace themselves for."

Nalumango's comments have been cited by critics as evidence that additional changes could occur as the government continues to restructure public institutions.

The debate has been amplified by comments from UPND National Youth Chairperson Gilbert Liswaniso, who said governments naturally sought to work with people aligned with their vision.

"Every political party [that] comes to power has to flush out!" Liswaniso said. "[It] has to work with the people aligned with the vision of that political party!"

Critics say such statements reinforce concerns that political considerations may be influencing decisions within the public service.

The civil rights activist David Chishimba rejects the notion that perceived political affiliation should determine a person's future in government employment.

"The constitution, however, is very clear on this," Chishimba told DW. "It restricts public officers from participating in partisan politics while in office. This safeguards an impartial civil service. However, it does not prohibit private political belief or otherwise."

Chishimba said civil servants served the country rather than any political organization. "Civil servants serve Zambia," he said, "not a party, the president or a campaign."

Nguvu said the shift was deeply unsettling for public workers caught in the middle of the administrative realignments.

Nguvu said abrupt personnel changes could affect morale throughout the public service and leave workers uncertain about their professional future.

"This can weaken morale and help to destroy institutional memory," Nguvu said. "Most of these decisions lack clear explanations."

Nguvu said such concerns had become more acute when explanations for personnel decisions were not communicated clearly.

## Exercise and cancer: What &quot;living longer&quot; really means

https://p.dw.com/p/5P4E9Prefer DW on GoogleGuided exercise can improve longevity after cancer by 7%Image: Pond5 Images/IMAGOAdvertisementIn 2025, an international trial found that people treated for colon cancer were more likely to be alive eight years later if they had received long-term exercise coaching.

The question facing doctors now is less whether people with cancer should exercise than how to build it into routine care.
In the US, that shift is reaching hospital standards. From January 2027, breast cancer centers must screen patients' physical function and recommend exercise or physical therapy where needed.
Yet experts caution that the trial's findings may not apply to every cancer or every patient.

The 2025 trial, called CHALLENGE, was led by the Canadian Cancer Trials Group. It began enrolling patients in 2009 and published its results in 2025. Its 889 participants came from six countries, most from Canada and Australia. All had finished surgery and chemotherapy for stage 2 or 3 colon cancer, which had not spread to distant organs.

Half received only health education materials on exercise and healthy eating. The other half additionally met a physical activity consultant for three years.

In the exercise group, the target was similar to adding 45 to 60 minutes of brisk walking, three to four times a week. The amount and type of activity varied from person to person, said Tormod Skogstad Nilsen, a cancer exercise researcher in Norway, but participants achieved lasting increases in their physical activity.

Exercise helped. People in the exercise group were less likely to see their cancer come back, to develop a new cancer or to die. In all, 93 had at least one of these setbacks, compared with 131 in the health education group.

In cancer research, living longer usually means a larger share of people is still alive at a given point. It does not promise anyone a set number of extra years.

Eight years after joining CHALLENGE, about 90 of every 100 people in the exercise group were alive, compared with about 83 of every 100 in the health education group. That is about 7% more people alive in the group who had guided exercise.

For people without cancer, the evidence comes from studies that follow people's habits and health over many years. These can show a link, but not prove cause and effect. Active people may also eat better or smoke less.

In another 2025 study, more than 85,000 adults in the UK wore a wrist tracker for a week. Over about six years, those who moved most had a 26% lower risk of 13 cancers, including breast and colorectal cancer, than those who moved least.

Even light activity, such as household chores, was linked to lower risk. The link held after researchers accounted for lifestyle, weight and other health conditions.

Earlier research, based on people's self-reporting, has led to similar conclusions. A 2016 analysis of 1.44 million adults in the US and Europe linked free-time activity to a lower risk of many cancers, largely regardless of weight or smoking.

A trial to prove prevention is unlikely: Many cancers take years to develop, so it would need huge numbers of people followed for a long time.

CHALLENGE included only people with colon cancer. But how it picked participants offers a clue: Before joining, they had to pass a fitness test.

Anna Campbell, a professor of clinical exercise science at Edinburgh Napier University in Scotland, sees that as a guide.

"I think patients with any other cancer stage II and III who have a reasonable baseline level of fitness and no major comorbidities that would prevent them from undertaking moderate to vigorous activity could be considered for similar interventions," she said. Comorbidities are other health conditions.

Nilsen noted some people decline to join exercise trials because they feel too unwell or tired, or do not want to exercise. "Hence, study populations are typically younger, 'healthier', and most times slightly more motivated compared to a clinical population," he said.

The coaching aimed to make activity a lasting habit and Campbell said that is where the trial's value lies.

"The main success of the study is that it enabled the participants to become independent exercisers staying active in a safe and effective and individualized way," she said.

Nilsen agreed. Helping people become more active "in their own environment," without depending on a well-equipped training center, "would likely lead to more sustainable activity behavioral changes," he said.

He is wary of fixed targets, however. "Exercise should be tailored to each individual and the challenges they face, rather than striving to reach a given minimum of exercise required," though health care resources may not always allow it, he added.

In a 2024 survey of 549 people with cancer, only one in ten had been referred to an exercise service. Yet almost three in four thought their oncologist should raise the subject.

"There are some excellent examples of effective pathways but these are few and far between," said Campbell. Good ones bring everyone on board, from surgeons to community fitness instructors.

"Ideally, one person in the clinical setting needs to champion the program to link all the team together."

Campbell expects CHALLENGE's regular coaching will be cut back in everyday care. Apps and live online group sessions could still work, she said, if run by properly trained staff.

One recent study of live online exercise engaged patients from poorer backgrounds, in rural areas and with advanced cancer. "I think it answered some of the barriers, such as cost, travel and lack of confidence," she said.

A training session can bring structure and company, Nilsen said. "You made it here, despite your feeling unwell or fatigued this morning, so you feel mastery based on that."

Latin America is home to around 40% of the world's 1.4 billion Catholics, for whom the pope is their spiritual leader. However, fewer and fewer people on the continent identify as Catholic.

Pope Leo's first leg of the tour takes him to Uruguay, the most secular Latin American country.

The excitement can be seen in the street vendors selling T-shirts and rosaries with his likeness, while bakeries have been selling Leo bread.
