## &apos;Star Trek&apos; star Patrick Stewart dies age 86

https://p.dw.com/p/5OqQJPrefer DW on GoogleStewart needed his agent's encouragement to take on the role of Captain Jean-Luc PicardImage: Willy Sanjuan/picture allianceAdvertisementFor Star Trek fans, he was the legendary Captain Picard; the British actor and director Patrick Stewart, longtime member of the  Royal Shakespeare Company, died on Thursday at the age of 86 following a "brief and poignant journey" with Alzheimer's disease and Lewy body dementia, according to an official statement on his social media accounts.

In Los Angeles, California, far away from the small English village of Mirfield where he was born, Stewart achieved world fame. He was 47 years old when he landed the role of a lifetime in the legendary science fiction series Star Trek: Playing Captain Jean-Luc Picard in "Star Trek: The Next Generation," he stepped into the shoes of the legendary Captain James T. Kirk, played by William Shatner.

Stewart brought a calm, measured and distinctly British presence that quickly made Picard a fan favorite and a cult figure in the Star Trek universe. He returned to the role decades later in the spin-off series "Star Trek: Picard," which ended in 2023, proving the character's enduring appeal.

As Captain Jean-Luc Picard, he led the crew of the starship Enterprise, a spaceship with the sizeable mission: "To explore strange new worlds. To seek out new life and new civilizations" — and most importantly, "to boldly go where no man has gone before!"

Stewart's interest in acting was sparked in the early 1950s when a teacher asked him to recite a work by Shakespeare. His passion for Shakespeare stayed with him until the end.

His parents were far removed from the glamor of the theatrical world. His father was a highly respected officer in the British Army during World War II, but at home, he was an abusive husband, and young Patrick had to helplessly witness repeated violence against his mother. Stewart wrote about his experiences with domestic violence in The Guardian in 2009. The stage, he confessed there, seemed to him "a far safer place" than reality; he saw acting as a way to escape his home life.

Patrick began acting at the age of 12. At 16, he left school early and, thanks to a scholarship, studied acting in Bristol, England. Along the way, he worked as a journalist. In 1965, he became an official member of the Royal Shakespeare Company, and remained with the theather company from 1966 to 1982. Such was his passion for the great English playwright and lyricist that he even shared his sonnets on his Twitter account during the 2020 coronavirus pandemic.

Stewart dazzled the theater world with his own stage adaptation of Charles Dickens' 1843 tale "A Christmas Carol." He played more than 30 roles himself. After its 1987 premiere in Stewart's hometown of Mirfield, the one-man show became a critics' favorite and a long-running hit on Broadway in New York.

In the same year,  Stewart went from being a stage actor to an international TV star. Star Trek producer Robert Justman offered him the role of Captain Jean-Luc Picard, one of the main characters in a new edition of the popular TV series "Star Trek: The Next Generation."

The successful Shakespearean hesitated. Until then, he had never worked in Hollywood. But his agent encouraged him to accept the role, Stewart recalled in a 2020 interview with the German newspaper Die Welt. "You can accept the offer as a start for now. It won't be a big deal, you can't revive a series as iconic as Star Trek anyway."

But his agent was to be proven wrong: Stewart led the Starship Enterprise through its galactic adventures for seven seasons and four full-length feature films. The TV series had astronomical success on Earth in a very short time.
At first, the producers were at odds as to whether Stewart's British accent could really fit the role of commander of a US starship in the 24th century, but it soon became impossible to imagine the series without the subtly diplomatic Captain Picard, who often quoted Shakespeare while at the helm. It seemed as if all the time he had spent as a Shakespearean actor had prepared him to command the Enterprise, Stewart once remarked with a wink. When the cult series ended in 1994, he had ruled out ever returning to the Enterprise's command center.

In 2010, the actor was knighted by the Queen of England. Stewart considered it the greatest honor of his career to stand alongside acting greats such as Sir Laurence Olivier and Sir Alec Guinness.

Beyond the screen, Stewart has long used his voice for social and human rights causes. In recent years, he has drawn attention to issues such as violence against women, age discrimination and the protection of children.

In a June 2020 interview, "Playboy" asked the New York resident whether he was considering retiring from acting. "Never! That would mean giving up my life," replied Stewart, who was approaching his 80th birthday at the time. He was filming the second of three seasons of "Star Trek: Picard." The series ends with a final episode in which the retired Starfleet admiral makes his last appearance. In the series, Picard retires to his family vineyard in France but is drawn back into space to confront new threats to the Federation.

This series also showcases Stewart's affection for his pit bull, Ginger. In "Star Trek: Picard," Picard also has a pit bull, a loyal four-legged companion named Number One — a deliberate reference to Stewart's real-life love of the breed. For his efforts to combat prejudice against pit bulls and to support their adoption, Stewart was honored by the US animal welfare organization ASPCA, among others.

In 2024, he published his memoir "Making it So." In it, he recounts his passion for theater and his love of Shakespeare.

Now, at the age of 86, the British actor has finally departed for another galaxy. Farewell, Patrick Stewart!

## Justice for Maya women in Guatemala, girls back in school in India, restored land in Mali: The UN has named its 2026 SDG Action Award winners

https://p.dw.com/p/5Oq4dPrefer DW on GoogleRokiatou Traore received the Changemaker AwardImage: UN SDG Action Campaign/Sabrina MarchionneAdvertisementA legal aid group for Indigenous women in Guatemala, a movement of village mothers in rural India, and a Malian entrepreneur who restores farmland have won this year's UN SDG Action Awards.

The winners were announced at the ninth Heroes of Tomorrow awards ceremony in Rome, Italy, run by the UN SDG Action Campaign. They were chosen from more than 7,000 entries from 190 countries.

Nine finalists were shortlisted for three categories: resilience, creativity and changemaker.

The Resilience Award went to the Women's Justice Initiative. Many indigenous women in Guatemala face high rates of violence, poverty and discrimination, made worse by language barriers and a lack of access to information. The initiative helps women and girls get access to justice and lead change in their communities. According to the organization, more than 16,000 women and girls have participated.

"I accept the UN SDG Action Award in the honour of the Maya Kaqchikel women and girls in Guatemala who are building a more just future for their communities. Ending violence against women requires all of us. Only together, we can build a future where every woman and girl lives with dignity and realizes her full potential," said Viviana Patal, the initiative's legal director.

The Creativity Award was given to Mothers of Courage, a grassroots campaign that aims to keep girls in education in rural India. Its organizers say more than 1.2 million people have joined and pledged to keep their daughters in education and to prevent early marriage.

"This award today belongs to all the mothers in those villages, all the women leaders, who are working hard to change the destinies of their daughters and their daughters-in-law," said Khushboo Awasthi, Mothers of Courage's chief curator.

"Together, let's create a world where no girl ever has to negotiate for her right to dream and to aspire and to thrive."

The Changemaker Award when to Malian entrepreneur Rokiatou Traore. Her organization, Herou Alliance, helps women and small farmers bring worn-out land back to life by planting trees alongside their crops. The trees also help farmers generate extra revenue, allowing them to sell products from moringa and other valuable trees.

"Receiving this award means a lot to me. It is a reminder of why I started this journey," said Traore. "I invite you all to join us, to plant more trees, to empower more women, and above all, to create a future full of opportunities for communities for today and for future generations."

The UN Trust Fund to End Violence against Women and Girls received an honorable mention. Speaking about the award, President of the Organization of Women's Freedom in Iraq Jannat Al Ghezi paid a tearful tribute to its founder Yanar Mohammed, who was assassinated outside her home in Baghdad in March.

UN Assistant Secretary-General Michelle Muschett said the finalists "remind us that the future is something we create together and that each of us has the power to help shape it and open doors for the next generations."

"When they tell you that one person cannot make a difference, prove them wrong," UN SDG Action Campaign Director Marina Ponti told the audience. "Change has always begun with people who refused to accept that the world could not be different. So be courageous. Be persistent. Be that change."

A panel of 20 judges from UN leadership, government, civil society, the arts, business and youth activism chose the winners. They included UN Development Programme Administrator Alexander De Croo, Oxfam International Executive Director Amitabh Behar, and Otto Group Vice President Tobias Wollermann.

The awards are run by the UN SDG Action Campaign, a special initiative of the UN Secretary-General. The campaign supports the UN Sustainable Development Goals, 17 targets adopted by member states that cover areas including poverty, hunger, education, gender equality and climate protection.

The awards are supported by Italy's Foreign Ministry and Germany's Ministry for Economic Cooperation and Development.

## Germany seeks new head of state

https://p.dw.com/p/5Op3CPrefer DW on GoogleFrank-Walter Steinmeier is set to leave office early next yearImage: Katharina Kausche/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementWanted: A new head of state — female, male, or other. Requirements: German citizen, entitled to vote, minimum age 40. Please apply as soon as possible! Availability starting January 30, 2027; job starts March 19, 2027.

This is not an official job posting, but Germany's federal government is indeed looking for someone to fill the nation's highest office. Having already served two terms, current President Frank-Walter Steinmeier is no longer eligible for another, leaving the position open for a successor. Many feel that it is time for Germany's first-ever female head of state.

"The person should have a certain level of public recognition, be politically unbiased, capable of bringing people together, approachable and a reasonably good [public] speaker," political scientist Ursula Münch told DW.

In Germany, a federal parliamentary republic, the president serves as head of state and a representative of the country as well as someone who embodies moral authority for the nation. Unlike in the US, France, or Russia, the president does not wield extensive political power or direct government affairs.

Steinmeier was nominated by his own party, the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD), in 2009. However, once elected via a convention of parliamentarians and state electors, a president-elect has to suspend their party membership in order to serve in the office apolitically. This time, it is Chancellor Friedrich Merz's center-right Christian Democrats (CDU), the senior partner in the coalition government with the SPD and the conservative Christian Social Union (CSU), which has the right to nominate a candidate.

Political scientist Münch said that "the three parties must agree on a person who is acceptable for all three." Since the convention that will select Steinmeier's successor is currently controlled by these three parties and their delegates, it is very likely that whomever Merz nominates will be elected.

"Well, I would really like it to be a woman this time. We've had 12 male federal presidents since 1949 — it's about time for a woman. But if the right man were to be nominated, I'd be fine with that too," said Münch. According to an August survey conducted by the Forsa polling institute for the RTL television network, about 74% of German respondents say they don't care whether it's a man or a woman. Another 19% explicitly want a woman, while 5% prefer a man.

Ilse Aigner, president of the Bavarian State Parliament in Munich, appears to have a good chance. Aigner is a member of the smallest party in the coalition, the Bavarian CSU, and is considered a unifying figure. She also has experience in government in Berlin, having served as agricultural minister at the federal level. She enjoys strong support within her Bavarian CSU party, whose leader Markus Söder recently said: "It would be tremendous if she became federal president."

Even members of the opposition Greens and the SPD are full of praise for Aigner's candidacy. "What are we actually waiting for? Surely Ilse Aigner has long since secured a majority across party lines?" asked SPD parliamentary group leader Matthias Miersch recently. However, Merz, who is friends with Aigner, has remained tight-lipped so far — possibly also because Aigner is not a member of Merz's party, but rather of its smaller sister party, the CSU.

Other potential successors from the CDU membership have been mentioned: Bundestag President Julia Klöckner, former Defense Minister Annegret Kramp-Karrenbauer and Education Minister Karin Prien. Hesse's state premier, Boris Rhein, is also a possible candidate. In recent days, another name has been making the rounds: lawyer Ines Claus, who serves as CDU's parliamentary group leader in Hesse and deputy party chair at the federal level — and is largely unknown.

However, rumors that she would be nominated were swiftly shot down by the chancellor. Sources close to Merz emphasized that he has so far "neither made a decision nor put forward a proposal." There are "a number of suitable" options, sources added.

The president is elected every five years by the Federal Convention, which comprises all 630 members of the Bundestag and an equal number of electors who represent the state parliaments. Among them are also prominent figures from the spheres of culture, science, sports and society. The candidate who receives more than 631 votes goes on to assume office. The governing parties — the CDU/CSU and the SPD — have a combined total of 682 votes, a comfortable majority.

The office of the president is often underestimated as merely a ceremonial role. Yet, as an authority standing above the political parties, this person wields considerable political, legal and moral influence. This is especially true in times of political polarization and international crises. While the president does not govern like a chancellor, he or she represents Germany both at home and abroad.

"From a constitutional perspective, the federal president is enormously important. For example, in a political crisis, he could decide whether it makes more sense to dissolve the Bundestag or to advocate for a minority government," explained political scientist Münch. In addition, he reviews and signs laws proposed by politicians.

The federal chancellor and CDU chairman has an interest in an orderly process, say sources close to the government. The "first milestone" will be the meetings of the CDU Presidium and Executive Board on November 9. Until then, discussions will be held with the state chairpersons of the CDU and the CSU. Sources made it clear that the SPD and the Greens will also be consulted, "since the election of the federal president should have broad support across the democratic spectrum."

That is how political scientist Münch sees it: "I still consider the question open, with a slight edge for CSU candidate Ilse Aigner." But there could still be a real surprise, she added.

The company reported an operating loss of €5.86 billion (roughly $6.53 billion) during July, August and September. One-off costs, not least a massive writedown in its stake in struggling sister company Porsche, explained the vast bulk of the losses.

The world's second-largest carmaker is battling mounting competition from and falling demand in the core Chinese market.

It is pursuing a major restructuring and downsizing program, trying to bolster falling profit margins, and executives said the difficulties highlighted the urgency of this process.

"The nine-month results make one thing clear: We cannot afford to lose any time. We must act quickly and decisively," chief financial officer Arno Antlitz said in a video statement.

The results were the latest reminder of the pressure on VW — and the wider German and Western automotive industry — to react to increasing competition in China, high costs and practical difficulties moving towards electric car production, and unpredictable and rising tariff burdens, thanks in no small part to US President Donald Trump.

Antlitz said the operating return on sales was only 3.8% in the first nine months of the year, even before the one-off writedowns hitting the bottom line.

As part of its restructuring program, the VW Group aims to restore an operating profit margin of between 8-10% by 2030.

This year, amid one-off costs now totaling in the region of €10 billion, it expects a profit margin of no more than 1%, compared to prior forecasts in the region of 5%.

## Russia shadow fleet sanctions: Can the EU act without US?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OmFfPrefer DW on GoogleTo curb Russia's oil export revenues, the US and Europe have stepped up efforts to intercept tankers accused of violating sanctionsImage: Thibaud Moritz/AFPAdvertisementSince Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, Moscow has increasingly relied on a so-called shadow fleet to bypass Western sanctions and transport its oil around the world.

Other heavily sanctioned countries, including Iran and Venezuela, have also been accused of using such vessels to export petroleum products.

These tankers often rely on deceptive practices like turning off their location transponders to evade detection and conceal the cargo's true origins or destinations.

The fleet, which consists largely of aging, uninsured and poorly maintained tankers, has raised widespread concern across the shipping industry. Yet it has expanded rapidly in recent years and now accounts for nearly 19% of global oil tanker capacity, according to estimates by financial intelligence firm S&P Global.

"The shadow fleet is a scourge," Elisabeth Braw, senior fellow at the think tank Atlantic Council, said, adding that "it is in nobody's interest" to have these ships sailing around in the world's oceans.

She pointed out that these tankers undermine the maritime order and increase the risk of accidents and environmental damage.

"Every country should use its leverage with Russia to ask it not to use the fleet," she told DW, noting that ultimately all countries suffer the consequences.

To clamp down on the fleet and curb Russia's oil export revenues, the United States and European countries have stepped up efforts to intercept tankers accused of violating sanctions.

The Trump administration, however, is reportedly considering lifting sanctions on some shadow fleet vessels and allowing them to transport oil that is not subject to sanctions.

A US official told Reuters earlier this month that the move was intended to ease record-high global freight rates.

Lifting sanctions on the shadow fleet "would decrease the effectiveness of the sanctions regime overall," said Michael Carpenter, senior fellow for Transatlantic Affairs at the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS). Such a move "rewards the aggressor and helps Moscow gain revenues to continue attacking Ukraine," he told DW.

Carpenter, who previously served as US ambassador to the Organization for Security and Cooperation in Europe (OSCE), said any sanctions relief would also put the US and the EU "strategically at odds with each other, with the EU clearly backing Ukraine and the US increasingly supporting Russia's financial goals."

This view is shared by Ben Harris, vice president and director of Economic Studies at the Brookings Institution.

Lifting sanctions on shadow fleet tankers would "make it substantially easier for Russia to trade its oil at market rates," he said, pointing out that it would "improve its government finances since so much revenue is tied to energy markets."

US Energy Secretary Chris Wright, however, rejected suggestions that the US was removing sanctions from shadow fleet tankers.

"We are converting dark tankers to legitimate tankers," Bloomberg quoted Wright as saying last week. "There is no removing of sanctions," he stressed, adding that the US government was working with investors who buy idle sanctioned tankers, refurbish them and use them to transport oil that is not subject to sanctions.

"We're going to move some of the ships from what I call the 'bad-guy fleet' to the 'good-guy fleet,'" Wright said. "They will not carry sanctioned oil anymore."

The proposal follows a deal that US President Donald Trump struck with his Russian counterpart Vladimir Putin this month to allow sales of Russian diesel onto global markets.

The agreement was seen as part of Trump's efforts to lower domestic fuel prices ahead of the US midterm elections on November 3.

The US president also waived broad new sanctions on Russia authorized by ​a recent legislation, which targeted Moscow's energy revenues and threatened tariffs against the world's largest purchasers of Russian oil and gas.

The decision was sharply criticized by Ukraine and its European allies over concerns that it could undermine the credibility of sanctions and weaken Western economic pressure on Moscow.

Harris said that "the US sanctions carry outsized weight because of the threat [for countries and businesses] of being cut off from the US financial system."

If the US steps away from its sanctions on the shadow fleet, he argued, "the responsibility shifts to European policymakers." But they have "less effective options because of the diminished threat of severing financial ties."

Still, the expert stressed that Europe has some tools, pointing to the continent's key role in global shipping and insurance services.

"They can implement measures demanding that tankers carry legitimate and established insurance when traveling through their waters," he said, noting that it "would mean that many shadow fleet tankers could not in practice legally transport Russian oil."

Harris added that European policymakers could also pressure countries worldwide to better regulate the tankers flying their flags.

Carpenter said even if US eases some restrictions, the EU will have no choice but to continue enforcing its sanctions on these vessels.

"The vast majority of the shadow fleet traverses the Baltic Sea and exits it via the Danish Strait," he noted, adding that the EU is "uniquely positioned to board and inspect vessels moving through this key chokepoint."

"European Central Bank President Christine Lagarde today informed the Governing Council and the president of the European Council Antonio Costa that she will step down from her position on 7 February 2027," the ECB said in a statement.

European Council President Antonio Costa said a process to find her successor would start immediately.

The announcement comes ahead of a key meeting of the bank this week that is likely to focus on France's deteriorating public finances.

No reason has so far been given for Lagarde's premature departure, but there has been speculation that she would leave early to enable EU leaders to choose her successor before French elections in the spring that could see a eurosceptic far-right victory influence the choice.

Her term, which began on November 1, 2019, would normally have ended at the end of October 2027.

Lagarde, a former French finance minister, was also head of the International Monetary Fund from 2011 to 2019.

Costa praised Lagarde's leadership during her seven-year stint at the ECB helm, a period in which the EU and the world faced great challenges.

"Through a global pandemic, a war in Europe, energy crises and inflation shocks, she has provided a steady and determined stewardship, helping to safeguard stability and the resilience of the euro area," Costa posted on social media.

"To ensure a smooth transition to the next leadership of the ECB, the process for the appointment of her successor will be initiated today and will proceed without delay, with a view to a timely decision," Costa said.

Recent protests, at which many demonstrators were calling for more spending on public services, were also marred by considerable violence.

There has been speculation as to whether the ECB could, for the first time, use the Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI),  a crisis-fighting tool that allows the bank to buy bonds of a eurozone member if yields rise uncontrollably and there is a risk of financial stress spreading.

## Bayern Munich head coach Vincent Kompany&apos;s health scare highlights the need for better support for elite coaches

https://p.dw.com/p/5OgQnPrefer DW on GoogleBayern fans made a banner for Vincent Kompany that read "Get well soon Vincent, we are with you!"Image: Bernd Feil/M.i.S./IMAGOAdvertisementVincent Kompany has just returned home from hospital. The Bayern Munich head coach admitted that he hadn't listened to his body, "and then at some point, my body just said, 'Enough is enough!'"

Bayern Munich's sporting director Max Eberl, who left Gladbach in 2022 due to his own health concerns, told German public broadcaster ARD that the 40-year-old Belgian had been suffering from meningeal irritation, which if untreated can become meningitis.

"They found out what it is. It is a meningeal irritation that brought on the incident last Friday," said Eberl, referring to when Kompany collapsed during training and required an emergency doctor.

"Honestly, he is doing as well as can be expected. He has been discharged from hospital today, which is the biggest and best news for all of us."

The news has been met by great concern from the German football community. Bayern fans held a banner showing their support, many German media outlets wrote opinion articles softly reminding that coaches are humans too and they too need support. Even the wider coaching community has started to ask questions.

"It’s something you take to heart and think about," Freiburg head coach Julian Schuster said after his team lost to Bayern recently.

"It's an illness. Any one of us can get it. It has nothing to do with the pressure we face in the Bundesliga," Eberl said.

Nevertheless, with Kompany admitting he hadn't listened to his body and his assistant coach Aaron Danks saying that "getting Vince to rest is a challenge", it is clear that the Belgian had been working very hard of late.

Kompany, whose father became the first Black mayor in Belgium after moving there from the DR Congo, has faced an enormous amount of adversity in his life. At just 14, his parents divorced and he told The Guardian in a 2019 interview: "I need to be fed stuff constantly, otherwise I don’t know how to function. I can't remember a lazy Sunday."

The Belgian's own history, both in his childhood and as former professional player, also plays a role in understanding the challenges coaches face in elite sport.

"I think we (coaches) have a really poor measure of ourselves within this example of like, wasn't listening to my body. I think a lot of coaches are really poor at that,"  coaching mentor Cody Royle, who has worked with teams in the NBA and the Premier League, told DW.

"And so part of my role is to enhance their attunement to that, to ask questions to that effect, to try to perhaps even impart some things that I've learned along the way or heard from others or other coaches that are going through similar things so that you can actually start to put words to what that feeling might have been or where you maybe touched burnout but it didn't quite get there and you're able to rescue something and start to really develop skill within it," he said.

"I think with Vincent's example we also need to account for the fact that this is a young man still finding his way in coaching and so he's still understanding what those triggers are within his body and what they're really telling him Maybe he had physical things that he could read when he was playing. These are more mental triggers, and he's still learning that and that's part of what he needs to do. But there should be people around that help."

It begs the question, in 2026, at a time when awareness around mental and physical health is greater than ever, particularly in elite sport, how is this still possible?

"I suspect that many head coaches aren't able to let go or delegate," Frank Wormuth, an educator for the German Football Association (DFB), told DW.

"They want to control everything because they don't feel secure when the team loses," continued Wormuth, adding that that's why coaches often bring close friends into their staff.

"You'd think professional coaches wouldn't have to worry, since they've earned enough money, but that’s not a good coach's priority—success is far more important."

A former coach, Wormuth admits that he had moments in his career when he had lost sight of how much of a role football was playing in his life.

"I never felt like I was doing too much until my kids pointed out to me—when I was at home—that I wasn't the coach anymore, but should be fulfilling the role of a father," Wormuth said.

"That's when I realized that I was truly operating 24/7 as a coach in my head, even when I was at home."

"I kind of throw my hands up a little bit at the fact that we aren't further along," Royle said.

"We're not even trying to educate this in to our young coaches as they come through. The education system probably facilitates this as much as the organizations that are individually responsible."

Clearly, if not well managed, extremely demanding elite-sports environments with high expectations on individuals makes for a dangerous combination. Both Wormuth and Royle advocate for the concept of a second set of eyes.

Having someone who sits outside the organization and doesn't belong to the politics to be able to —whether it's on the phone or come in for a visit — just say, 'You need to go home because this isn't going well.'"

"This is a game where Jock Stein literally dropped dead on the sideline, right? There shouldn't need to be any more conversation beyond that... and it was workaholism and stress that caused it," added Royle.

"So I don't know what else it's going to take, but I just don't think the game really cares enough to change anything about it. The show must go on, right?"

Even as it does when Bayern Munich play Borussia Dortmund in the Bundesliga's most marketed game this weekend, Kompany's absence will serve as a reminder that while much of the focus of modern sport is on the players, there is an equal level of support required for those on the sidelines.
