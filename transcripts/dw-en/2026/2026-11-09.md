## French officials blame Iran for supermarket fridge hacking

https://p.dw.com/p/5PGaUPrefer DW on GoogleStores in several countries across Europe recently experienced fridge and freezer issuesImage: Elsa Rancel/Ouest-France/PHOTOPQR/MAXPPP/IMAGOAdvertisementFrench Foreign Minister Jean-Noel Barrot said the attacks on French supermarkets last month came from Iran, and were carried out by the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), the elite branch of Iran's armed forces that answers directly to its religious leadership.

"This attack is unprecedented in both its scale and its level of aggression," Barrot said, adding "we will implement sanctions at the European level against all those responsible for this attack."

While the cyberattack was comparatively simple, it nevertheless had serious consequences for hundreds of supermarkets, spoiling large quantities of produce and in some cases setting temperatures so high that refrigeration units caught fire.

It was launched overnight on October 11-12, affecting around 700 of France's roughly 10,000 supermarkets.

A series of similar incidents also affected French supermarkets last week, and authorities even reported a failed attempt to hack cooling units at blood banks.

Supermarkets in other European countries have reported similar security breaches, as have US authorities. It was not clear if any of all of these instances were connected.

"A judicial investigation has been opened against the head of the Iranian group Shahid Kaveh, an offensive cyber unit affiliated with the Electronic and Cyber Center of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, against his two deputies, and against any other individuals who may be identified," Paris prosecutor Laure Beccuau said.

Barrot's Foreign Ministry said that France "condemns in the strongest possible terms the unacceptable cyberattacks carried out on its territory by the Islamic Republic of Iran."

## Russia: Independent Novaya Gazeta shuts its Moscow newsroom

https://p.dw.com/p/5PFatPrefer DW on GoogleLaunched in 1993, Novaya Gazeta often took on Russia's criminal and political elites Image: AP Photo/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementThe Novaya Gazeta newspaper closed its Moscow office on Monday, saying it was not able to continue reporting amid a campaign of state pressure in Russia.

The investigative outlet, co-founded by late USSR chief Mikhail Gorbachev, had long been a thorn in the Kremlin's side. In recent years, it ran into increasing roadblocks amid its critical coverage of the Russian invasion of Ukraine.

It was branded a "foreign agent," a Soviet-era term resuscitated by President Vladimir Putin's administration, with officials ordering it to vacate its Moscow offices.

Russian banks were also forbidden from processing donations from readers and supporters. The paper had been heavily dependent on this income stream after moving wholly into online publishing, with the transition taking place shortly after the invasion of Ukraine. Russian authorities introduced new wartime censorship rules in the wake of the full-scale invasion.

2021 Nobel Peace Prize-winning founding editor Dmitry Muratov issued an appeal for free speech in wartime Russia as journalists gathered in the Novaya Gazeta offices to witness its closure.

"Those in our country who advocate for peace have been denied the right to read what they choose, as we see happening today," he said. "We are currently at a point where the section of the Russian constitution that guarantees political and civil rights has ceased to function."

Russia's government meanwhile sought to play down the significance of the closure, with Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov saying it was part of a "rotational process" in a fiercely competitive media landscape.

Novaya Gazeta was a flagship for post-Soviet democratic Russia and the era of "glasnost" or openness, also launched by Mikhail Gorbachev. The reformist Soviet leader used some of the prize fund for his 1990 Nobel Peace Prize as seed money for the publication.

The paper has built a reputation for fearless reporting, which has also put it in the crosshairs of Russia's wealthy and powerful. Six of its journalists were murdered since the paper was launched in 1993.

They included Anna Politkovskaya, who spent years investigating allegations of abuses by Russia's military during its campaigns in Chechnya. An early critic of Putin, she was found dead in her apartment bloc on the president's birthday in 2006.

Images published in Russian media on Monday showed the paper's editor-in-chief, Sergei Sokolov, taking down photographs of Polikovskaya and other reporters murdered in what's thought to be retribution for their work.

Last month, when first announcing that the paper would close, Sokolov said the outlet had "been killed." The paper was shutting down after "the Russian authorities — the Russian special services — have forced the editorial team to end its work," he said.

Muratov called for a ceasefire in Ukraine and urged foreign observers to remember the strength of dissent that endured in Russia, even if it was being pushed underground by censorship laws and other repression or intimidation.

"We canot blame the citizens themselves for this," Muratov said, "especially the wonderful, truly remarkable younger generation."

"I urge you to distinguish between the Russian authorities and the Russian people,"  he added.

Muratov also called for the release of around 4,000 people he termed political prisoners in Russia.

Novaya Gazeta's leadership has not made it entirely clear whether its closing down in Moscow marks a definitive end to the outlet's reporting, or whether they might perhaps seek to regroup and begin operating in some form from outisde Russia, as several other publications including DW have been forced to in recent years.

One of its journalists, Irina Vorobyova, said that the paper's spirit would survive like a "good virus" and would "continue in everyone that was part of it."

## India’s demonetization: 10 years on, promises remain unfulfilled

https://p.dw.com/p/5PGVPPrefer DW on GoogleDigital payments in India have expanded rapidly since the demonetization move in 2016Image: Amit Dave/REUTERSAdvertisementOn November 8, 2016, Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced in a televised address to the nation that he was withdrawing all 500- and 1,000-rupee banknotes from circulation at midnight.

The surprise move invalidated 86.9% of the value of currency in circulation in a single stroke.

He promised that the hardship would help eliminate black money, corruption, counterfeit currency and terrorist financing. The exercise was presented as a major step towards a cleaner and more transparent economy.

Millions of Indians rushed to banks to exchange their savings. Long queues formed outside banks and ATMs, with people waiting for hours, sometimes without knowing when they would get their money.

Workers missed wages, small businesses struggled to pay employees and families found it difficult to buy everyday essentials. Reports of people dying during the cash crisis added to the distress.

"I still remember my domestic help's father," businessman Prakash Patra from Jaipur city told DW. "He panicked, collapsed, and passed away right there, standing in a long queue just trying to exchange his family's life savings. It wasn't just cash that was lost in those lines. It was human lives."

For India's most vulnerable, particularly rural women and people without bank accounts, the sudden cash shortage made savings difficult to access. Money set aside for family weddings, medical emergencies and basic survival was suddenly compromised.

Joe Athialy, executive director of the New Delhi-based Center for Financial Accountability, a financial watchdog, says the impact fell disproportionately on the poor and the informal economy.

"Demonetization destroyed livelihoods, disrupted small businesses and pushed millions into deeper economic insecurity. The poor and those dependent on the informal sector bore the greatest cost," Athialy told DW.

Arun Kumar, former professor of economics at Jawaharlal Nehru University and author of "The Black Economy in India," shared a similar assessment. The government wrongly assumed that illicit wealth was primarily held in cash, he said.

"And it penalized the cash-dependent informal sector while leaving the real engines of the black economy untouched," Kumar told DW.

The first major setback came in 2018, when the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) reported that 99.3% of the demonetized currency had returned to the banking system.

Only a small fraction of the withdrawn money had failed to come back, undermining the expectation that large amounts of undeclared cash would be extinguished.

A decade later, there is little evidence that demonetization made a significant dent in black money or corruption. Economists have long pointed out that much illicit wealth in India is held in property, gold and other assets rather than cash.

Some supporters of the policy argued that forcing cash into the banking system would help formalize the economy and widen the tax base. But the extent to which these changes could be attributed to the currency withdrawal remains contested.

Lekha Chakraborty, a professor at the National Institute of Public Finance and Policy, said research after the exercise pointed to limited success in achieving its stated objectives.

She draws on studies that examined both the immediate economic shock and its longer-term effects.

"The research points to modest gains against demonetization's primary objectives, alongside temporary but real economic costs. The impact was particularly severe in the informal sector, where cash remains central to everyday transactions," Chakraborty told DW.

"The economic shock was real, even if much of it faded over time. But there is little to suggest that demonetization produced a lasting change in the way black wealth is generated or concealed."

Athialy argues that the policy was based on a flawed understanding of how illicit wealth is held in India.

"Cash is only an insignificant part of the black economy. Much of India's unaccounted wealth is held in assets such as real estate and in tax havens, not currency," he said.

He also questions the absence of accountability for the disruption and its institutional consequences.

"Nobody has been held accountable for the economic disruption or the undermining of institutions such as the RBI."

Digital payments have expanded rapidly since 2016. The Unified Payments Interface (UPI), launched in August that year, has become a major part of India's retail payment system today.

In 2025-26, UPI processed 241.6 billion transactions, worth 314.2 trillion rupees (approximately €2.95 trillion), according to the RBI.

Yet the growth of digital payments has not reduced the amount of physical currency in circulation.

This comes after the Norwegian Ice Hockey Association (NIHF) said that the International Ice Hockey Federation (IIHF) had informed it that it had been removed from the tournament so that Russia could take part.

The move, which has yet to be publicly announced by the IIHF, comes in apparent response to last month's Court of Arbitration (CAS) ruling, which ordered it to allow Russia to return to international play. The World Juniors is the first tournament affected by the CAS decision. The IIHF barred Russia and Belarus from international play shortly after Moscow launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022.

In a statement issued on Saturday, the IIHF said it was "taking the necessary steps required by the CAS ruling and reviewing what conditions would need to be met to safeguard our Championships, protect the organization's financial stability and secure the long-term future of international ice hockey."

As of Monday, the IIHF website still listed Norway as one of the participants in the World Juniors, which is to be hosted by Edmonton and Red Deer, Alberta, beginning on December 26. Still, Swiss Ice Hockey responded to Norway's statement, saying its team would not face Russia at the World Juniors — as would be the case if it replaces Norway in the schedule.

However, it is far from certain that Russia will actually be able to ice a team in the tournament, as just last week, the Canadian prime minister ruled out the possibility.

"While Russia and Belarus are supporting this illegal, unjustified ​war in Ukraine, as long as that's going on, there will be none of those athletes competing for their countries in Canada," Mark Carney said.

"So, ​if you're asking specifically about the World Juniors, the Russians will not be there."

How this circle could be squared is unclear. Carney's pledge, coupled with the stated refusal by Finland, Sweden, Switzerland and others to face Russia in an international tournament, appears to have given the NIHF hope that its team could still play in the upcoming World Juniors. Several long-time corporate sponsors have also threatened to pull out if Russia is allowed to compete.

"This makes us hope that the last word has not been said about our participation in Canada in December," NIHF General Secretary Petter Salsten said.

"The Norwegian Ice Hockey Association is awaiting further information from the IIHF in the coming days."

Meanwhile, the German Ice Hockey Association (DEB) is still in discussions with the government in Berlin and the IIHF about how to proceed — or not — with hosting the Men's Ice Hockey World Championship next May.

"We intend to host the World Championship in Germany next year, and I am very optimistic that we will succeed in doing so," DEB chairman Frank H. Lutz told German broadcaster MagentaSport on Saturday.

While he acknowledged the IIHF's CAS-ordered requirement to include Russia and reiterated the DEB's rejection of the court ruling, he provided no indication as to how this obstacle could be overcome.

Lutz, who said he would be in Berlin for more consultations this week, had previously said handing back Germany's hosting rights was a possibility — if only as a last resort.

On Friday, government spokesman Stefan Kornelius said Berlin supports the DEB's efforts to find a solution.

"However, given the boycotts that would follow Russia's readmission, that would effectively be impossible. For this reason, we intend to explore, through dialogue, how we can still ensure the tournament takes place."

Asked at the same press conference whether Germany could issue visas to a Russian team for the tournament, a Foreign Ministry spokesman indicated that this would be difficult under restrictions currently in place.

"At a time when Russia is waging a war of aggression against Ukraine, there can be no 'normalcy' — and we will certainly not pretend that there is," said spokesperson Martin Giese.

It has been suggested that government officials could be choosing their words carefully out of concern for Munich's chances as it bids to host the Summer Olympics in 2036, 2040 or 2044.

The International Olympic Committee (IOC) recently lifted its sanctions on Russian athletes and suspension of the Russian National Olympic Committee, opening the door for their return to competition despite the ongoing war in Ukraine.

The thinking goes that the IOC would expect national Olympic committees who are bidding to host an Olympic Games — like the German Olympic Sports Confederation (DOSB) — to follow its lead.

Asked whether the debate over Russia's possible involvement in a hockey tournament could affect Munich's chances, government spokesman Kornelius said he didn't wish to "speculate on implications."

Still, with the IOC set to decide in the next few weeks whether Munich advances to the next stage of the process, one has to think that the issue could hardly have come up at a more inopportune time for the DOSB.

## Fake news in Africa: In an age of disinformation, who decides what&apos;s true?

https://p.dw.com/p/5P8sxPrefer DW on GoogleIn junta-run countries like Burkina Faso, fact-checking initiatives like Fasocheck are under increased government scrutinyImage: Boureima Salouka/DWAdvertisementMisinformation poses a threat to democracy worldwide: An exaggerated WhatsApp message, a manipulated TikTok video or an apparent breaking news alert on Facebook can all misguide people in Africa and beyond — particularly in the run-up to elections.

As artificial intelligence is making it increasingly easy to manipulate images, voices and videos, more and more countries are trying to equip their populations with skills to identify and counter fake news.

But are these efforts really about raising awareness, or are certain governments using this fight to control public opinion, silence dissident voices and misguide the populace — from both within and outside of these respective nations?

Journalism lecturer Dani Madrid-Morales at the University of Sheffield believes that African governments must significantly step up their efforts to combat sources of disinformation and misinformation.

He told DW that "there are very few countries — if any — that are actually doing a good job" to beat the influence of fake news.

Meanwhile, he points out that there also are civil society initiatives in various African nations, which help inform people about the dangers of such false narratives.

For instance in South Africa, organizations like Africa Check and Media Monitoring Africa work together to highlight the impact of fake news on elections. Similar coalitions also exist in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) and Senegal.

However, these initiative mostly focus on fighting disinformation during election periods; the rest of the time, they typically lack funding and sustained engagement with civil society to continue to make a change.

There also are deliberate efforts by certain governments not to battle fake news; some let it happen while others engage in deliberately spreading this kind of disinformation.

Madrid-Morales distinguishes between three types of disinformation in this context: There are scams and similar schemes, which some governments, he says, fail to sufficiently fight, leaving people susceptible to financial losses and other problems.

Secondly, he adds that there also are certain foreign influence operations trying to impact public opinion from outside a country and, in many instances, outside of the continent.

Finally, there are political disinformation campaings which originate from within a country that are intended to deliberately derail impartial views, he explains.

"Governments are particularly interested in the third category," says Madrid-Morales, adding that this is especially prevalent in the Sahel region.

He highlights how in nations like Burkina Faso and Mali, military governments have shut down media outlets, prosecuted journalists and expelled foreign correspondents in order to silence free speech.

In his view, these junta governments are primarily concerned with preventing negative or false information about themselves from circulating rather than actively recruiting followers.

Foreign powers meanwhile also appear to play a considerable role in shaping public opinion across the continent.

Afrobarometer, a pan-African research network, examined the influence of international actors in terms of their influence across Africa — including those suspected of sharing fake news.

The study found that over 60% of respondents viewed China's political and economic influence positively; this was followed by 52% for the United States.

Both countries have no past colonial history in Africa, although they are now competng for economic gains while focusing otherwise on furthering infrastructure projects — partly to maintain this largely benevolent perception towards them.

However, perceptions of other former colonial powers vary notably in some cases, depending mainly on factors such as the impact of past injustices: For example, in French-speaking countries, only 34% of respondents viewed France's ongoing influence positively.

Komi Amewonou from Afrobarometer  told DW that such attitudes are not necessarily the outcome of targeted propaganda campaigns for or against certain nations outside the continent but rather the result of African governments letting negative perceptions simply run their course.

In some countries, there are media development project trying to educate people and animate them to think for themselves and thus fight sources of fake news and disinformation.

In Ghana, the NGO Penplusbytes runs the "Caravan of Hope" initiative — a project supported by the German development agency GIZ and by DW Akademie.

This mobile campaign brings media literacy training to rural communities through theater, role-playing exercises, public discussions and videos in local languages. The initiative has managed to reach 3,000 people in five districts to date.

The campaign also involves trusted community figures, as studies show that locals most trust people they know personally — particularly traditional village leaders and community representatives.

Rebecca Avusu from Penplusbytes, who helped develop the project, told DW that "information must be verified before people believe it or pass it on. This is particularly important for those without smartphones or with limited formal education."

Initiatives in South Africa take a similar approach: The country is home to Africa Check — one of the continent's best-known fact-checking initiatives.

The organization visits schools and teaches classes how to identify manipulated images, verify sources, and deal with misinformation circulating on WhatsApp groups.

However, Madrid-Morales says that there can be a downside to such media literacy programs as well; pointing to research conducted in South Africa, he highlights that while some young people do become more discerning consumers of information, others eventually lose trust in almost every source, resulting in a complete lack of knowledge about important developments.

In countries elsewhere in Africa, there's greater resistance against such media literacy initiaves — chiefly by their own governments.

## Strait of Hormuz still a source of leverage for Iran

https://p.dw.com/p/5PG4FPrefer DW on GoogleLatest data from the UK's maritime trade operations center UKMTO show increased shipping traffic through HormuzImage: Atta Kenare/AFPAdvertisementSince the Iran war started, attacks on commercial ships in the Strait of Hormuz have put crews at risk of death and injury, forcing shipping companies to choose whether to attempt the passage or delay cargos and face higher costs.

Merchant vessels continue to use the strait despite the danger, but how long that can continue depends partly on Iran's ability to sustain its attacks and the United States' capacity to protect shipping and bear the cost.

Ali Mohammadi, an expert in Iranian affairs based in France, believes Iran's geography and the equipment it uses allow it to keep attacking ships.

But he sees the economic burden of the war as Tehran's main constraint.
"Iran's limitation in the Strait of Hormuz is economic rather than military, because continuing the war has a significant impact on its economy," he said.

It's unclear how long Iran can sustain attacks given the lack of reliable information on the nation's weapons stocks and its ability to replenish supplies. And sporadic attacks in the crucial waterway are also different from closing the strait completely.
"Even if a complete closure of the Strait of Hormuz were possible, it would not be sustainable because it could carry high political costs for Iran," Mohammadi told DW. "Iran cannot indefinitely disrupt its allies' access to the strait."
By threatening shipping, Iran can raise the cost of confrontation and seek leverage in negotiations, but that may not guarantee success or yield concessions, the expert said.

Data from the UK Maritime Trade Operations Center's (UKMTO) weekly report, released on November 6, show 325 reported transits through Hormuz between October 29 and November 4. That was up from 277 the previous week, an increase of about 17%.

But vessel numbers alone cannot explain what is happening to energy supplies as cargo volumes and the routes used also matter, said Dalga Khatinoglu, an expert on Iran's energy and macroeconomics.

"If we look only at crude oil, Middle Eastern exports across all routes, from the Gulf of Oman and the Strait of Hormuz to the Red Sea, have largely recovered to pre-war levels," he told DW. "In some weeks in September and October, they even exceeded those levels."

The novel "Hufeland, at the corner of Bötzow" (2019, Ullstein), for example, recounts the period of reunification from a child's perspective.

In his experience, the way films and documentaries portrayed East Germany around the time of reunification was very bleak. "But West Germany in the '80s," he said, "was also bleak and very staid."

This uneven portrayal of the two parts of Germany is an issue that deeply concerns Menner — partly because he unwittingly contributed to it in a small way. Among other things, Menner's work explores the topic of surveillance and how it works.

In the archives of East Germany's Ministry for State Security, or Stasi — which are freely accessible today as part of the National Archives — he discovered a massive collection of photographs that both fascinated and horrified him and which he wanted to bring to the attention of a wider public.

His photo book "Top Secret" (2012, from publisher Hatje Cantz) features a selection of photos that offer a unique insight into the work of the Stasi. Some are eerie, while others are curious or even absurd.

Menner also tried to look into the photo archive of West Germany's Federal Intelligence Service. "I spent a year trying to gain access — without success," he said.

According to Menner, West Germany's intelligence services in the 1960s were not exactly models of virtue either, and he believes it is "actually high time" to critically examine this part of history as well. In his view, an honest examination of Germany's past as a whole is a prerequisite for genuine reconciliation.

Streisand believes literature can help foster mutual understanding. "Literature opens a window into experiences we haven't had ourselves," she said. "You can immerse yourself in the mind of someone who has had a different life than you have and understand what happened."

Could the West German interest in this stem from the fact that full-time employment for both parents has long been less common in the West? It's possible, said Feuchtenberger.

When looking toward their shared future, the two East German women are more optimistic than Menner, from West Germany.

"In Berlin, I often don't even notice anymore whether I'm in the West or the East," said Menner. "But when it comes to sociopolitical issues, politicians should listen to older and younger people more closely," he added, referring to the recent election successes of far-right Alternative for Germany, or AfD . "Otherwise, things are going to get difficult in the near future."

He points to high local community opposition to coal projects on environmental grounds or due to lack of financial viability. According to German nonprofit climate campaigners, Beyond Fossil Fuels, some 97% of new coal projects have been cancelled in the last decade in Turkey, in what it calls "a global record."

"Turkey's coal project failure rate is the final nail in Europe's coal coffin," said Kasia Szeniawska of Beyond Fossil Fuels in a statement earlier this year. "Coal power is finished: it's financially risky, increases costs for billpayers, harms people's health, and sabotages climate goals."

But in June, Turkey announced plans to expand one of its largest coal plants and near double its capacity.

The move underlines Turkey's "conservative energy policy" and "highly insufficient" climate goals, said Umit Sahin, head of the climate change program at the Istanbul Policy Center, a global research institute.

Turkey's current climate target allows emissions to keep rising until 2038, meaning it will struggle to meet its plans to reach net zero by 2053, notes Climate Action Tracker, a climate research think tank.

Aside from climate concerns, Turkey's high dependence on imported fossil energy makes it extremely vulnerable to volatile global oil and gas markets. The nation currently imports 95% of its natural gas, 83% of crude oil and 60% of its coal — which together make up a "lion's share" of Turkey's energy capacity, said Baysal.

It is partly this dependence that has inspired Turkey's push for greater electrification at the COP31 climate conference.

In September, the country launched the "35x35" global electrification pledge, which aims to increase electrification of world economies from just over 20% today to 35% by 2035.

The goal is for electrification to replace fossil fuels, including in transport, buildings and industry, and preferably using clean energy, though this is not mandatory —heat pumps, for example, emit fewer greenhouse gases than gas boilers, even when the electricity is generated using fossil fuels.

The pledge complements existing UN climate goals of doubling energy efficiency and tripling renewables to keep temperature rise in line with the Paris climate agreement.

Turkey COP31 President Murat Kurum will promote the 35x35 pledge in Antalya on both climate and energy security grounds.

"[The war in the Gulf] has shown us, once again, that fossil fuels do not guarantee energy supply security," he said earlier this year at a UN climate meeting in Bonn, Germany. "Investment in alternative energy sources, in particular to support energy diversity, means stability, resilience and clean development."

But despite the rhetoric, Climate Action Tracker says that under Turkey's National Energy Plan, the COP31 host is only on track to electrify around 25% of total energy consumption by 2035.

Turkey is one of numerous Mediterranean countries facing extreme climate impacts like wildfires and record heat, in addition to recent severe and deadly flooding.

As it hosts this year's climate summit, the country has a great opportunity to hasten the energy transition, said Baysal, especially since it receives nearly double the annual sunlight of Germany.

Turkey is already starting to realize some of this potential, with one analysis showing the country produces more solar module manufacturing capacity than several major European countries combined.

Solar increased from around 0.1% of total installed electricity capacity in 2014 to almost 21% at the beginning of 2026, according to Turkey's resources and energy ministry.

The COP31 host can reduce its emissions 35% by 2035 if 10 GW of solar and wind is added each interim year — as opposed to the government's target of 8 GW — noted Umit Sahin. This goal will be helped by fast-expanding battery capacity, he said.

Meanwhile, the COP presidency could be a "turning point" and "a very important step for Turkey," said Sahin.

If the country wants to show climate leadership, and reduce energy dependency, it will need to embrace a "progressive climate policy" that promotes a full energy transition, he added.
