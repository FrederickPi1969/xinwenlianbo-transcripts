## Strait of Hormuz attacks spill oil into Persian Gulf

https://p.dw.com/p/5MqbEOil coats the coast of Qeshm Island, Iran, on August 13, 2026, after a spill reached the shore from the Strait of HormuzImage: Sanaz/Middle East Images/picture allianceAdvertisementA dark, muddied patch of blue, incongruent with the rest of the ocean; a winding thread of silvery white; a reflective metallic sheen — these are some of the telltale signs on a satellite image that oil may be leaking into the water.

Experts have identified these and many other visual signaturesin the Strait of Hormuz, leading them to conclude that there's an environmental crisis playing out amid the US-Iran war, as tankers are struck by missiles, set on fire or sunk.

One recent example: In June, a Russian tanker carrying about 800,000 barrels of oil stranded off the coast of Oman, causing a major spill.

Earlier in the war, there were as many as 85 large oil-laden tankers stuck in the Strait — that number has since dropped to 13, according to Greenpeace. Each stranded tanker, plus every other vessel trying to move through the waterway, poses a distinct risk for attack and thus a spill. Since the start of the war, the environmental organization has used satellite imagery to track more than 100 spills.

There are likely several other spills that we don't know of, amid widespread GPS spoofing in the region that causes ships to broadcast false positions. Greenpeace released a report this week calling the situation an "ecological catastrophe" and pinpointing a number of oil slicks that are extending into protected and fragile ecosystems.

"People should be concerned," Nina Noelle, a Greenpeace expert on environmental disaster relief, told DW. The war has taken "a very, very heavy toll on the environment," she said, with the damage now reaching new scales.

Greenpeace identified five major oil slicks earlier this week with a total visible area of 240 square kilometers (about 90 square miles) in the Strait of Hormuz. That's about four times the size of Manhattan.

These patches of oil floating on the ocean's surface are getting smaller — now about 80 square kilometers — according to more up-to-date satellite images, as weather conditions have likely made the slicks disperse. But that does not mean the ecological impact is any less severe,  Noelle said. "Oil can also sink into the sediment and impact the ecosystems for years and years."

What's concerning is where the slicks were identified. One was found near Musandam National Park, a protected area in Oman that's home to coral reefs, sea life and important breeding grounds for sea birds. Another is close to Iran's Minab River Delta and its protected mudflats and mangrove forests. This kind of damage can take decades for ecosystems to recover from.

"What we have put out is the conservative estimate," Noelle said. "It might be worse. It's definitely not better."

Greenpeace and other environmental organizations are relying on satellite images provided by the Copernicus Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 satellites. They're part of the European Union's Earth observation program and are publicly available.

There are also private satellite providers such as Planet Labs that tend to have higher-resolution images. But they've limited access to satellite imagery during the war to prevent their data from being used for military targeting. And there are limits to on-the-ground reports too.

"We don't have a lot of information coming from the country itself, both in Iran as well as the Gulf states," Wim Zwijnenburg, an environmental researcher who uses open-source methods at the Dutch peace organization Pax, told DW. "There is quite restrictive policies of people posting imagery from what's happening on the shore."

Oil can be deadly for seabirds. It coats their feathers, reducing their natural waterproofing and buoyancy, making it harder to stay afloat, fly, and hunt for food. Even small amounts of oil can damage eggs too, causing them not to hatch or to be born with deformities.

"Mangroves have aerial roots, and the oil is covering these roots like a film and actually hindering the mangroves in exchanging gases," Noelle said. "They're kind of suffocated by the oil. Most of the time, it leads to the trees dying."

The risks extend to people, as well. Coastal communities rely on fish that die off after being exposed to oil. And oil spills contaminate water used for Gulf states' drinking supply. Desalination — or converting seawater into drinkable water — is where 90% of drinking water in Kuwait comes from, along with about 86% in Oman and 70% in Saudi Arabia.

"It's unlikely that it will be addressed quickly, because of the war," Zwijnenburg said. "War in general makes it very difficult to respond fast to these kind of emergencies. There are a range of limitations because of the military movement, because of mines — so it's very difficult to get vessels and cleanup operations to start."

Environmental groups have pointed to the war in Iran — and the rising scale of oil spills it has caused — as further evidence that the world needs to move away from its dependence on fossil fuels.

"The most sustainable long-term damage prevention for things like that would be to reduce and ultimately end our dependency on oil and gas," Noelle said. "Because, as long as our energy systems and transport routes are centered around fossil fuels, there will be a continued environmental risk."

## Airport malaria in Germany remains infrequent

https://p.dw.com/p/5MsNMMalaria is only transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoesImage: James Gathany/AP Photo/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementEight people are known to have contracted malaria in the vicinity of Frankfurt Airport in central Germany since July; three of them have died. Those affected are mostly employees of the airport operator Fraport.

The Frankfurt Public Health Department believes the outbreak is due to "airport malaria," which occurs when a female Anopheles mosquito carrying the malaria parasite travels by aircraft to a country where the disease is not common from one where it is, bites a person and infects their red blood cells.

"We are dealing with a very rare occurrence, not a new frequent disease in Germany," said Achim Hörauf, the director of the Institute for Medical Microbiology, Immunology and Parasitology at Bonn's University Hospital (UKB), in a press release.

The last documented case of airport malaria in Germany before the current ones was in 2023, according to the Epidemiologisches Bulletin of the Robert Koch Institute (RKI), an online journal that regularly provides information on infectious diseases and public health.

Normally, those with the disease in Germany were infected elsewhere and brought it back during a long-haul trip.

But a mosquito can make its way to Germany as a "stowaway" if it was trapped in a suitcase or in clothing. Hörauf said that under certain circumstances, it might be able to survive in warmer areas of an airport.

According to a UKB press release, the cases in Frankfurt involve malaria tropica, which is considered to be the most dangerous and potentially life-threatening form of malaria.

To contract the disease, a person needs to be bitten by a female anopheles mosquito infected with the Plasmodium falciparum parasite. This pathogen passes to the human and then attacks the red blood vessels.

"Simply put, it can lead to many small blockages in the blood vessels," explained Hörauf. "Depending on which organ is affected, various serious complications can arise."

Fluid can accumulate in the lungs, or neurological damage can result if the blood vessels of the brain are affected. Acute kidney failure is also possible. About 20% of those affected die if the disease is left untreated.

"If a diagnosis is made in time, there can be targeted treatment," Hörauf said. "If the disease is overlooked, however, it can very quickly take a severe turn."

At first, symptoms are not specific, resembling those of the flu: sudden high fever, chills, headaches and muscle pain. Nausea, vomiting and diarrhea are also possible.

The Frankfurt Public Health Department has advised anybody who lives or works near Frankfurt Airport to seek medical attention immediately if they experience any such symptoms.

Rapid diagnostic tests can provide evidence of malaria in human blood and then a blood test can help to confirm this. The RKI says treatment should take place at a hospital, which has expertise in tropical medicine.

As summer comes to an end and temperatures cool in Germany, particularly at night, the conditions are becoming less favorable for mosquitoes and malaria parasites.

However, experts warn that there could still be other cases of malaria in Germany despite the cooler weather. Plasmodium falciparum has an incubation period of seven to 15 days, so more than two weeks can pass between a mosquito transmitting malaria to somebody by biting them and the onset of symptoms. According to Hörauf, the biggest challenge is to ensure that people think malaria could be a possibility even if they have not traveled abroad.

## Fact Check: False video alleges Berlin votes destroyed

https://p.dw.com/p/5MsE4Germany's postal voting system is a frequent target for disinformationImage: Caro Kadatz/picture allianceAdvertisementBerliners will vote in state and district elections on September 20. In the days leading up to the vote, a video circulated on social media that allegedly shows a man opening postal voting envelopes and destroying ballots cast for the far-right party Alternative for Germany (AfD).

DW Fact check reviewed more than a dozen posts sharing the footage. The posts use similar captions, presenting the video as evidence of election fraud in Germany. Several have amassed hundreds of thousands of views on X.

How widely has the video spread? And is its reach driven by genuine public interest? Or is something else boosting its visibility? DW Fact check investigated the video and the accounts sharing it.

"A video circulating in Berlin shows a man opening mail-in voting envelopes and destroying ballots marked for the AfD, while ballots for Chancellor Friedrich Merz's CDU are left intact. The footage appeared days after the AfD's landslide victory in Saxony-Anhalt. Berlin holds its state election on September 20."

At first glance, the ballot paper appears authentic. Its names and layout match those of the official ballot paper for the Spandau 2 constituency in western Berlin, which is publicly available online. However, there are several inconsistencies in the documents shown in the video.

Berlin's state returning officer, Stephan Bröchler, told DW that the documents shown in the video are not authentic election papers from Berlin.

Bröchler said the envelopes shown in the video did not match the official ones used for this election. Bröchler also noted that the way the envelopes are sealed appears suspicious.

According to official guidelines issued by Berlin's election authorities, postal voters receive a voting package containing a "Wahlschein," or a voter declaration that needs to be signed, plus two envelopes and the ballot papers for the elections. For the September 20 vote, Berliners receive two ballot papers: a grey-and-blue ballot for the election to the city-state House of Representatives and an orange ballot for their District Assembly.

In the video, however, the documents do not correspond to the official postal voting package. The person in the video opens a red envelope and removes a white envelope. The signed voter declaration is missing. Without it, a mail-in ballot would be invalid.

The person then takes out only the grey-and-blue ballot paper for the Berlin House of Representatives from the white envelope.

Although voters may choose to vote in only one of the two elections, it would be unusual for three envelopes in succession to contain only one ballot paper. However, the footage does not clearly show whether the second envelope is really empty.

Another detail raises doubts about the video's authenticity: In the background, large grey bins allegedly containing postal ballots are marked with a version of Berlin's coat of arms featuring the Berlin bear.

Bröchler's conclusion is clear: "It can be ruled out that any actual Berlin election documents are involved. The video is clearly intended to discredit postal voting."

One of the accounts that shared the video has previously come under scrutiny. An investigation by the Lithuania-based research organization Debunk linked the account to the so-called "Sprinter" network, a group of X accounts. The researchers identified what they described as signs of coordinated and inauthentic activity, including unusually high posting volumes, frequent account renaming and evidence of purchased followers. Debunk said the network was used to amplify pro-Kremlin narratives.

DW Fact check reviewed more than 15 other posts sharing the footage, many of them using nearly identical captions. Together, the posts had accumulated more than 3.2 million views by the time DW Fact check examined them.

The accounts sharing the video also showed several suspicious characteristics. Almost all of the 15 accounts examined exhibited at least one sign of potentially inauthentic behavior, including rapid follower growth, frequent username changes or limited evidence of normal user engagement.

One account, which presented itself as an independent media company based in West Asia, had more than 43,000 followers despite having been created only in April 2026. During that period, the account changed its username nine times.

On their own, these characteristics do not prove that the accounts are coordinated or that the video's engagement was entirely artificially generated. They do, however, show that the video's apparent reach should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of widespread, organic discussion on X.

The video resembles other false election-related claims that have circulated about Germany in recent months.

Ahead of the September 6 election in the eastern state of Saxony-Anhalt, DW Fact check examined a video that similarly purported to show AfD postal ballots being destroyed. The claim was false.

Among other inconsistencies, all three crossed-out ballots shown in the footage carried the same name on the signed voter declaration. The envelopes had already been opened, and the ballots lacked a hole punch required for use with voting templates designed for blind and visually impaired voters.

DW Fact check has also investigated and debunked numerous other false claims targeting Germany's postal voting system.

Julia Smirnova, who investigates influence operations as a researcher at the Berlin-based Center for Monitoring, Analysis and Strategy (CeMAS), told DW that these similarities, together with the way the new video is being disseminated, indicate that it is "highly likely" to be part of the Storm-1516 influence operation.

The operation has previously used fabricated videos, forged documents and purported eyewitness accounts to spread false narratives about elections and other political issues.

In February 2025, Germany's Interior Ministry said it had indications that two fake videos alleging manipulation of AfD ballots were connected to the Storm 1516 campaign, citing similarities in their content and distribution.

## German police probe arson attack on Wuppertal synagogue

https://p.dw.com/p/5MrgcPolice worked to gather evidence at the sceneImage: Christoph Petersen/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementProsecutors in the German city of Wuppertal said on Friday that they had taken over the lead in investigations into an attempted arson attack at the local synagogue.

They said a 37-year-old Afghan national arrested near the scene soon after made "comments that indicated an antisemitic motive" to detaining officers.

The suspect is being investigated for attempted aggravated arson. He was set to appear before a judge at an arraignment hearing on Friday.

The attack took place midafternoon on Thursday. The suspect allegedly threw three containers of flammable liquid at a stairwell and doorway serving as an entrance to the synagogue and its adjoining eatery, Cafe Negev, and then set it alight.

The fire was quickly extinguished by a staff member, meaning that nobody was hurt and the damage to the structure was minimal, according to police.

"Shortly after, police officers on security duty on site were able to provisionally arrest a 37-year-old man in the immediate vicinity of the crime scene," Wuppertal's prosecutor's office said in a statement. "After reviewing the video surveillance footage from the scene he is strongly suspected of having laid the fire."

Although "intensive" background investigations were ongoing, prosecutors said there were already clues regarding the motive.

"During the police intervention, he issued comments that indicated an antisemitic motive for the crime," prosecutors wrote.

The Wuppertal resident with Afghan citizenship had a prior criminal record for threatening a female neighbor, they said.

"His apartment was already searched ... yesterday evening," prosecutors wrote. "Alongside documents several knives and a machete were located, which had been positioned in easy reach."

Police were investigating data from the suspect's mobile phone, which they confiscated in the arrest.

Regional broadcaster WDR reported that about 30 people attended an impromptu gathering outside the synagogue on Thursday evening in a show of solidarity.

The Central Council of Jews in Germany condemned the attack in the buildup to the Yom Kippur religious holiday on September 20 and 21.

"Once again an attack on a synagogue has taken place at the time of Jewish high holidays," said Josef Schuster, the council's president.

"What disturbs me most is how coolly the perpetrator in Wuppertal carried out this attack in broad daylight. There's no sign of a sense of shame or being conscious of wrongdoing."

Wuppertal, home to around 350,000 people, lies just east of Düsseldorf in the western state of North Rhine-Westphalia.

## Canada and EU: A look at the bloc&apos;s special relationships

https://p.dw.com/p/5Mr2jMark Carney sees Canada and the EU as a natural partnershipImage: Yves Herman/REUTERSAdvertisementCanada and the EU "see the world with the same eyes," according to European Commission president, Ursula von der Leyen.

"Our commitment to the rule of law, and our unyielding defense of human dignity, individual freedom and democracy share the same deep roots, the same unwavering dedication," Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney said.

In that spirit, and with both parties grasping for an effective way to deal with Donald Trump and his tariffs, von der Leyen floated the idea that Canada could become the European Union's first associate member on Thursday.

Given the concept of associate membership does not yet exist, it's difficult to determine exactly what that would mean. Germany proposed a similar arrangement for Ukraine earlier this year, only for Volodymyr Zelenskyy to reject the idea on the grounds that the country wants full membership.

Though not officially recognized as associates, there are plenty of countries that have strong ties or agreements with the bloc. DW looks at some of them and how they work.

Under this model, the ‘European Free Trade Association (EFTA)' countries are part of the EU's internal market, so people, goods and services can move freely in the Schengen area. The three countries also follow many EU rules in areas like tourism, environment and education.

But there are some key distinctions. The countries have no part in the decision-making process, bar some input into policy preparation, such as participation in expert groups. They are also not in the customs union, do not use the Euro and are not bound by the EU's common trade policy or justice laws. Iceland held a referendum on whether to go for full membership last month, but the government's push to do so failed and they remain an EFTA country.

While it borders four EU member states (Germany, Italy, France and Austria), Switzerland has never been a full member. Instead, more than 120 bilateral deals have been agreed upon by the EU and Switzerland over the last 50 years. These relate to areas such as trade, transport, security and education. A new package of deals was finalized last year, 20 years after they were last agreed.

More than 1.5 million EU citizens live and work in Switzerland, while 466,200 Swiss have gone the other way. That is possible as Switzerland is also part of theSchengen area, allowing for free movement of people across borders.

Switzerland makes financial contributions to a number of EU member states that joined after expansion in 2004, mostly in the east of Europe, but it has no EU voting rights and adopts only some EU laws. Switzerland can also participate in certain EU programs and research projects, through individual negotiations.

More than four years after the UK voted to leave the EU in a 2016 referendum, the two parties signed a trade and cooperation agreement. Negotiations were fraught and complex, and the UK now has limited access to the EU market and is aligned with the EU on some areas of policy and regulation, for now at least.

There is no longer free movement of people from the UK to the EU or vice versa, and the UK are not in the single market or the customs union. Since the center-left Labour party replaced the Conservatives in government in 2024, talk of a return to the customs union and alignment on other selected EU policy areas has been more frequent. But any return to full member status seems unlikely for now.

The EU has not admitted a new member state since Croatia in 2013. Several nearby countries in the region are hoping to join the bloc, but must first fulfill a number of criteria under the EU's "stabilization and association process" designed for the "Western Balkans." These are: Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Kosovo, Montenegro, North Macedonia, and Serbia.

Under the plan, the EU has promised to "prepare Western Balkan partners for accession through economic reforms and investment and to bring some of the benefits of EU membership to citizens in the region."

States must first become official candidates for member state status by applying to the EU, with each of the member nations agreeing to them as a candidate country. Then follows a process of negotiation, such as that currently underway with Ukraine, Serbia and Montenegro. Finally, an accession treaty is prepared, which the European Commission, the European Council and the European Parliament must agree with. Only once that treaty has been signed and ratified by all member states and the candidate country will said country become an EU member.

## Dual citizenship means Germany now in competition for talent

https://p.dw.com/p/5MrYzYounes Ebnoutalib opened the season for Frankfurt with a hat trick in BerlinImage: Inaki Esnaola/Gonzales Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementWhen Jürgen Klopp named his oversize squad for Germany for the upcoming international break, a number of other national team coaches will have taken notice.

Among them was Mohamed Ouahbi, of Morocco, who lamented that he had already had Eintracht Frankfurt striker Younes on his radar since last season.

"He had a profile that interested us. I spoke to him on the phone and explained the Morocco project to him — without making any promises," Ouahbi said.

If the 23-year-old Ebnoutalib sees any action in the UEFA Nations League over the next couple of weeks, as expected, he will be cap-tied to Germany — and ineligible to play for Morocco in the future.

This is by no means a unique situation. A growing number of Germany players in recent years have held another citizenship through their parents and thus have other options for their international careers.

This applies to several members of Klopp's 44-man squad. Vitaly Janelt, whose father moved to Germany to play professional ice hockey, is also eligible to represent Czechia. Bambase Conte's father is from Guinea, and Josha Vagnoman has an Ivorian father. Finn Dahmen has an English mother, while fellow goalkeeper Noah Atubolu has Nigerian roots. Said El Mala's father hails from Lebanon, and Brajan Gruda's father was an Albania international.

All of them were all born and raised in Germany and have represented the country at youth levels. Vagnoman has even made two senior international appearances for Germany, though he has yet to play a competitive match.

There are also numerous established Germany players who could have chosen another nation. These include Felix Nmecha (England), Kevin Schade (Nigeria), Jamal Musiala (Nigeria, England), Karim Adeyemi (Nigeria, Romania), Jonathan Tah and Serge Gnabry (Ivory Coast), Malick Thiaw (Senegal, Finland), Nathaniel Brown (USA), Nadiem Amiri (Afghanistan), Aleksandar Pavlovic (Serbia), Waldemar Anton (Russia), Yann Aurel Bisseck (Cameroon), and Ridle Baku (DR Congo).

The men's national team reflects German society as a whole: According to the Federal Statistical Office, 21.8 million of the country's approximately 83 million inhabitants have an immigrant background.

"More than 40% of children in Germany under the age of 5 have a migration background. This means they will later have the option to play for one country or the other," Andreas Rettig, the managing director for sport for Germany's DFB football association, told DW prior to the 2026 World Cup.

This forces the DFB to compete with other countries for talent for its national teams — and it hasn't always been successful.

A prime example is Ibrahim Maza of Bayer Leverkusen. Born and raised in Berlin to an Algerian father and a Vietnamese mother, he holds not just German but also Algerian and Vietnamese citizenship.

The midfielder possesses many qualities that could help Germany, yet two years ago he opted to play for Algeria and represented the "Desert Foxes" at the 2025 Africa Cup of Nations and the 2026 World Cup.

Germany also missed out on Paul Wanner and Can Uzun. Wanner, a top prospect developed at Bayern Munich, chose Austria—his mother's home country. Can Uzun of Eintracht Frankfurt has been playing for Turkey since September 2024. Both took part in last summer's World Cup.

Had Maza, Wanner and Uzun chosen Germany instead, each of them would have stood a good chance of being named to Klopp's first squad as national team coach.

With this international break featuring four instead of the previous customary two matches — and to see as many players as possible, Klopp named two squads for this break — every player selected has a strong chance of getting playing time.

The upcoming UEFA Nations League fixtures — away against the Netherlands, home and away against Greece, and at home against Serbia — are all competitive matches. This means that, even if it wasn't the primary factor in his selection process, over the next couple of weeks, Klopp could wind up cap-tying a few more players for Germany.

## Beethoven and Andy Warhol: Rebel icons on show in Bonn

https://p.dw.com/p/5MikyThe Mona Lisa of the Beethoven House: Andy Warhol's take on the composerImage: Meike Böschemeyer/BeethovenhausAdvertisementAndy Warhol portrait of Ludwig van Beethoven offers an unprecedented visual take on the great composer.

Warhol's 1987 silkscreen dispenses with the stern face usually seen on historical portraits and statues of the musical genius. Instead, Warhol uses pop art's vivid colors and striking visual language to turn Beethoven into a modern icon.

Originally conceived as a commercial work for the pop art guru's New York City studio, known as The Factory, the image was produced in a limited edition of 60 and proved to be one of the most innovative artistic engagements with Ludwig van Beethoven in the 20th century.

Warhol created the famous Beethoven color silkscreen prints just a few weeks before his sudden death on February 22, 1987. The series is among his final works.

How did Warhol approach Beethoven as a subject? And in what context did the work emerge in its numerous colored variations? A special exhibition, "Beethoven by Warhol," on show until April 2027 at the Beethoven House in Bonn — the composer's birthplace — explores these questions and more.

The exhibition presents rare works that visitors would otherwise never get to see. Over the course of three years, the team of curators at the Beethoven House and Archive Bonn, led by Malte Boecker, unearthed these valuable pieces from the depths of archives and private collections.

Alongside Warhol's iconic depiction of Beethoven, visitors can see drawings the artist did while searching for his Beethoven motif in Bonn. There are also proofs of prints that document his exploration of the series' color scheme.

The curators highlight an elaborately crafted Beethoven print on canvas, which belongs to an anonymous collector from the Rhineland.

Curator Silke Bettermann said that, with such works, "Warhol tells us: Look here! This man, Beethoven, is completely modern! He's a superstar!"

Whenever something looks effortless and easy, there's almost always a great deal of work behind it. The same is true of Warhol's Beethoven series.

In his research process, Warhol compared numerous depictions of the composer before developing his own concept. He also did drawings based on likenesses of Beethoven, including the statue of the composer at Münsterplatz in Bonn.

Ultimately, Warhol decided to base his work on the iconic portrait by Joseph Karl Stieler, painted in 1820 on commission from the Frankfurt couple Franz and Antonie Brentano — a merchant family and patrons of the arts who were friends with the composer. The portrait sought to capture not only Beethoven's outward appearance but also his rebellious personality.

Warhol adopts the graphic design of Stieler's work, transforming its colors and overlaying it with a handwritten Beethoven score. However, rather than using the notes of the "Credo" of the "Missa Solemnis," as Stieler did, he chose music from the "Moonlight Sonata," probably Beethoven's most famous work. "Warhol's Beethoven speaks to us quite literally through and by means of his music," curator Silke Bettermann told DW.

Bonn-based Warhol gallerist Hermann Wünsche played a key role in the creation of Warhol's Beethoven works. He not only visited the artist in New York, but also brought him to the Rhineland several times. It's thanks to Wünsche that the artist created numerous Germany-related pieces, including the portraits of Chancellor Willy Brandt from 1976 and soccer star Toni Schumacher, whom Warhol portrayed in 1983.

The exhibition's organizers brought the work depicting Schumacher to Bonn — on loan from the soccer star's private collection. The former goalkeeper himself also paid a visit.

Schumacher recalls his meeting with the US artist: "Back then, in the mid-1980s, in all modesty, I was already a star, too. But then Andy Warhol came along. And when we were together in the same room, I felt like I was looking at a superstar, so I took a bit of a back seat."

Also on display for the first time at the Beethoven House is a unique collection of record covers designed by Warhol, assembled by Belgian collector and Warhol expert Guy Minnebach.

Over the course of three decades, Andy Warhol designed strikingly beautiful and individual covers for more than 50 records.

The range extends from a relatively classical, yet already whimsically playful, Mendelssohn record featuring Vladimir Horowitz, whom Warhol also knew personally, to the legendary jeans cover with a real zipper for The Rolling Stones' "Sticky Fingers" (1971) and the famous banana album cover for The Velvet Underground & Nico (1967).

According to eyewitness accounts, music played continuously at Warhol's studio. His record covers offer a glimpse into the artist's musical tastes. Beethoven, notably, is not among them.

The Warhol exhibition is the prelude to an important event at the Beethoven House. In March 2027, people around the world will commemorate the 200th anniversary of Beethoven's death with a special edition of the Beethoven Festival.

"This exhibition is a wonderful opening to the Beethoven commemorative year," Wolfram Weimer, Germany's state minister for culture and media, told DW. As patron of the exhibition at the Beethoven House, he traveled to Bonn to see it and speak at the opening ceremony. "Today, we need more Beethoven," he said during his speech.
"Beethoven was not only a musical genius who delighted the world with the greatest musical works of art imaginable," Weimer said. The composer also embodied the idea of freedom: "He was a rebel, he broke norms, and he questioned the power structures of his time. He was a messenger of freedom, and that is a good message for us today. We need more freedom today, too!"

## Germany: Is it possible to replace the chancellor?

https://p.dw.com/p/5Mn5zSixteen months into office, Friedrich Merz has record low approval ratings, triggering a debate abour his futureImage: Annegret Hilse/REUTERSAdvertisementIt is the hot topic this week in Berlin: Does German Chancellor Friedrich Merz still have the backing of his own party, the Christian Democrats (CDU), and the coalition partners,  Bavaria's Christian Social Union (CSU) and the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD)? Will he be ousted? How would that work? Can he just step down?

Support for the chancellor has reached a record low: In the latest Deutschlandtrend representative survey, conducted by pollsters Infratest-Dimap for German public broadcaster ARD in early September, only 13% of respondents said Merz was doing a good job.

In early September, Merz's CDU suffered a resounding loss in the state of Saxony-Anhalt's parliamentary election, seeing the party's result halved from the 2021 election.

The election result has reinforced criticism of Merz, even from within his own ranks, and his communication style, which frequently alienates his audience.

But Merz remains chancellor. What are the options to change that? Germany's constitution, the Basic Law, grants the position a strong standing. German chancellors are not so easy to unseat.

Merz himself can decide to resign for any reason. That would not necessarily mean the end of the coalition government he leads. An example: In May 1974, then Chancellor Willy Brandt of the SPD resigned after a spy from the communist East Germany (DDR) was discovered to have been working in his inner circle. The governing parties, the SPD and the business-oriented Free Democrats (FDP), quickly agreed to continue their alliance.

Only 10 days later, Finance Minister Helmut Schmidt, also of the SPD, was elected as the new chancellor. To do this, an absolute majority of all lawmakers in the Bundestag, the lower house of parliament, was needed (not only those present during the vote). This majority is called the "chancellor majority". Schmidt achieved this effortlessly, eventually got reelected and remained head of the German government for 8 years.

If a new majority were formed through alliances in the Bundestag or if a chancellor fell so far out of favor that their own supporters no longer believed in them, the instrument of a "constructive vote of no-confidence" could come into play. That means the majority of lawmakers vote in favor of a new candidate for chancellor.

An example: Helmut Schmidt of the SPD, lost the support of his junior coalition partner, the FDP, in 1982. The FDP wanted to push through changes to economic policy, which Schmidt rejected. They joined forces with the conservatives from the CDU and CSU, and jointly put CDU leader Helmut Kohl forward as a new chancellor. He won a majority in parliament, replaced Schmidt and moved into the chancellor's office where he remained for 16 years.

The probability of this happening in Merz's case is very remote. The conservatives and Social Democrats do not want to end their coalition. There is no clear rival candidate for Merz. North Rhine-Westphalia's State Premier Hendrik Wüst of the CDU is seen as a possible successor, but has not made a step forward. Besides: The CDU is usually very loyal toward its political leaders; ousting the chancellor via a vote of no confidence on the open stage of the Bundestag is simply unthinkable for most conservative lawmakers.

Germany's parliament cannot simply dissolve itself and call new elections. Lawmakers can, however, call a confidence vote on the chancellor and his government. Several chancellors have already used this mechanism, coupling it with a vote on a contentious issue.

As chancellor in November 2001, Gerhard Schröder (SPD) put forward a confidence vote in the Bundestag and linked it with the question of whether his government should deploy the German military to Afghanistan to support the NATO mission led by the United States following the September 11 attacks. Schröder won, remained in office and continued with his military policy.

Merz has already said he sees no point in calling for a confidence vote. There are also no signs of large breakaway movements from members of his coalition in Parliament.

If a chancellor loses a confidence vote, the Bundestag can be dissolved and fresh elections called. Following the defeat in the Bundestag, the chancellor can ask the German President to dissolve parliament and call voters to the polls. The president is not obliged to do so, but usually does.

It seems that Merz has ruled out this option. Neither the SPD nor the conservative Union (of CDU/CSU) are interested in calling new elections at a time when their approval ratings are low.

So will the government remain the same? Right now, it seems so. However, things have been so dynamic in recent days and weeks in Berlin political circles that nothing should be ruled out.

## Germany faces energy tax losses as EV sales rise

https://p.dw.com/p/5MoreGermany's shift to EVs is reducing emissions while eroding fuel tax revenues, raising questions about future transportation fundingImage: DWAdvertisement"People are moving away from gasoline and diesel," Jens Boysen-Hogrefe, a tax and transportation expert at the Kiel Institute for the World Economy (IfW), told DW.

"The boom in electric cars triggered by high fuel prices is accelerating the transition in drive technologies that was already planned anyway," Boysen-Hogrefe said.

According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), sales of electric vehicles in Europe rose by nearly 30% in the first quarter of 2025 compared with the same period last year.

Norway is leading the way, with electric cars accounting for as many as 95% of all newly registered passenger cars in the country.

The agency's "Global EV Outlook 2026" reveals that EV growth rates in Asia-Pacific countries, excluding China, were even more remarkable, with sales in some regions rising by up to 80%. In Latin America, that figure was about 75%.

In Berlin, the boom in electric mobility has not been met with universal enthusiasm. That's particularly the case at the Ministry of Finance, as charging an electric car does not incur energy taxes, but only minimal electricity taxes.

Currently, a liter of diesel in Germany is taxed at 47.04 cents  (54 US cents per gallon), and a liter of gasoline at 65.45 cents. On top of that, there is a carbon levy and a 19% value-added tax.

With gasoline priced at €2.10 per liter, taxes account for €1.14 of the total, meaning that more than half of the pump price goes to the government, according to the ADAC motoring club.

The current fossil-fuel-based system, with its energy taxes, truck tolls and CO2 tax, is very lucrative for the government, Boysen-Hogrefe said. "The finance minister doesn't benefit much when people drive electric cars," he noted. "Quite the opposite."

"If the shift to electric cars takes place and nothing changes in the tax system, the road system will become a money-losing venture for the federal government," Boysen-Hogrefe said, predicting billions of euros in losses.

According to the Federal Statistical Office, revenue from the energy tax on diesel and gasoline fell from €37 billion in 2016 to €33 billion last year.

In 2022, a report by the scientific advisory committee to the German Transport Ministry projected that revenue from the energy tax would drop to as low as €5 billion by 2050.

"The transition to electric vehicles has fiscal implications that have hardly been discussed so far," the report states. The expected tax shortfalls add to the costly subsidies for electric vehicles that are currently still necessary to drive the transition forward, it added.

In Germany, government incentives to purchase electric cars — amounting to several thousand euros per vehicle — were scrapped at the end of 2023.

Electric cars are still exempt from vehicle tax through 2035. And companies that invest in electric cars continue to enjoy tax benefits.

In their report, the transportation experts advised the government not to wait until the tax deficit becomes so large that it is forced to act.

That's because "the lead time for such measures spans several legislative terms," they argued.

Boysen-Hogrefe said fiscal pressure could now increase. If people continue to buy electric cars in droves, "the government can't just shrug it off," he said.

Countries such as the United Kingdom and Norway have already come up with measures to deal with the new situation.

In the United Kingdom, for instance, a levy on electric cars is set to take effect in April 2028. The so-called "Electric Vehicle Excise Duty" charges 3 pence (3.5 euro cents) per mile (1.6 kilometers) on fully electric cars and 1.5 pence on plug-in hybrids.

Since 2024, New Zealand and Iceland have required annual odometer checks for electric vehicles to calculate mileage-based fees.

And in Switzerland electric vehicle owners will be required to pay a road-use charge from 2030, either through a weight-based fee per kilometer or a tax on electricity purchased at charging stations.

Meanwhile, Norway, a trailblazer for electric vehicles, has restricted the VAT exemption for electric cars starting this year. It has also introduced a weight-based purchase or registration tax and tolls.

In Germany many experts are advocating for a passenger car toll to compensate for the shortfall in energy tax revenue while also taking road use more fully into account.

"The recommended policy option is a distance- and traffic-based toll," according to a recently published  study by the University of Münster.

"If we realistically assume that policymakers will shy away from this step, the vignette solution is recommended," according to the report.

"If this, too, encounters insurmountable political resistance, the only solution left is to raise the motor vehicle tax," they concluded.

## VAR&apos;s mission creep a problem for football

https://p.dw.com/p/5McR0How much technology is too much? The video assistant referee (VAR) is perhaps the most discussed technology in modern sport. "The idea behind VAR was that technology could reduce human error while leaving the referee in charge," Susmit Gulavani, an assistant professor in AI and machine learning in sport at the University of South Carolina, told DW.

"That is where the sense of "mission creep" comes from. VAR is now expected to resolve almost every contentious decision, even though many incidents, such as fouls or handball decisions, still require human interpretation."

"The appeal is understandable because AI can significantly improve efficiency and productivity. Tasks that once took hours can often be completed in minutes.
