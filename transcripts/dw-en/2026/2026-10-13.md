## Saudi Arabia&apos;s military struggle against Houthi attacks

https://p.dw.com/p/5OCpFThe Houthis held a recruitment drive in Sanaa, Yemen on October 7Image: Mohammed Huwais/AFPAdvertisementYemen's Iran-aligned Houthi militia continues to escalate its attacks on Saudi Arabia targeting energy facilities but, above all, airports.

On Saturday, a missile attack on Riyadh's international airport killed 13 people and injured more than 300. In a second attack on Sunday, the militia killed another civilian and injured 11 others.

According to their own statements, the Houthis also attacked Saudi Arabia's airport in Dammam and two oil facilities within the past 24 hours. Saudi Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman Al Saud warned of the consequences for energy security and announced a strong response.

According to a report by the US think tank American Enterprise Institute, Saudi Arabia is targeting Houthi missile launch pads, storage facilities and other military infrastructure in Yemen. The institute quotes Saudi military spokesman Turki al-Maliki in its October 11 report as saying that 136 military targets have already been destroyed.

However, years of Saudi-led military intervention between 2015 and 2022 in Yemen have not succeeded in defeating the Houthi militia. Moreover, the Saudi-led international coalition formed in 2015 has fallen apart and the United Arab Emirates has withdrawn its troops.

The Iran-aligned Houthis not only control large parts of northern Yemen but also increasingly the western coastline from where they disrupt international shipping through the Red Sea and the strategically important Bab el-Mandeb Strait. In early September, drones also severely damaged the Saudi East-West oil pipeline. The temporary shutdown disrupted oil exports and increased risks for the energy markets.

For weeks, the Saudi government has been seeking allies in the fight against the Houthi militia. In late September, the United Kingdom and France pledged military support to defend Saudi infrastructure. London offered aerial refueling for Saudi fighter jets, while Paris announced it would provide troops, radar systems and air defense technology to protect the Yanbu oil terminal.

However, while the US recently refused to fight alongside Saudi Arabia against the Houthis, there could now be a change of heart in Washington, writes political analyst Osamah Al-Rawhani in a study by the Yemeni think tank Sanaa Center for Strategic Studies.

He notes that it remains to be seen whether this could actually lead to a successful offensive, as holding on to captured territories and launching counterattacks require significant financial resources, political commitment and the necessary resolve.

In his view, it is questionable whether Saudi Arabia would be able to muster the necessary perseverance. The kingdom could, however, change its current strategy. "If the costs of the war continue to rise, Riyadh might look for a way to withdraw from the conflict," Al-Rawhani says.

Marcus Schneider, project manager at the Friedrich Ebert Foundation's Center of Expertise on Peace and Security in the Middle East and North Africa, shares a similar view.

"To eliminate the Houthis, ground troops would be needed but Riyadh has no experience with this type of warfare, especially in a foreign country," Schneider recently told DW. As such, he believes the Saudi leadership is less concerned with achieving a military victory than with reducing its vulnerability, securing the broadest possible coalition of partners and gaining political maneuvering room.

"The Houthis are well-entrenched in Yemen and can withstand considerable blows. Above all, Saudi Arabia needs stability for its Vision 2030 reform set and that is currently further away than ever," Schneider said.

He added that military asymmetry is also a key factor: Saudi Arabia possesses modern weapons and spends enormous sums on its armed forces. The Houthis, on the other hand, do not need to win a conventional war. It is enough for them to deploy missiles and drones, threaten infrastructure and disrupt shipping traffic.

Saudi Arabia may also be able to turn to another partner in the region: the United Arab Emirates (UAE). According to media reports, Anwar Gargash, a senior diplomatic advisor to the UAE president, spoke out in favor of closer security cooperation among the Gulf states. "We are all targets," he stated on X.

Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia have undermined the security of the entire region, he notes. "It is necessary to revitalize cooperation among the Gulf states, particularly in the areas of security and defense, and to strengthen the collective security system," Gargash added.

Pakistan, which signed the Mecca Joint Defense Agreement in early August along with Saudi Arabia and Turkey, also initially ruled out military intervention.

According to media reports, however, the country is now said to be carrying out airstrikes on Houthi missile and drone positions — a claim that Islamabad denies. Pakistan does not intend to deploy ground troops. Turkey, another member of the Mecca Pact, rules out an offensive military operation but is providing air defense equipment.

Saudi Arabia can thus build on additional military support, according to an analysis by the US think tank Soufan Center. Whether this will result in an effective strategy, however, depends on whether the partners go beyond limited airstrikes and political expressions of solidarity and on whether the fragmented Yemeni government forces can better coordinate their military operations.

Meanwhile, Iran has offered to act as a mediator between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis. However, given Iran's support for the Houthis and the recent attacks on Saudi Arabia, it is questionable whether Saudi Arabia will accept this offer.

Saudi Arabia and the US are allies, whereas Iran and the US have been at war since February. It is also unclear whether Tehran wields sufficient influence over the Yemeni militia to persuade it to halt the attacks.

Deters began doing media interviews with any outlet that would listen after the WSJ story broke, publishing embarrassing text chains in which Guilfoyle implored him to transfer $100,000 (€89, 400) to her American Express account, writing, "Honey please, I need you to get this done for me today."

Deters, who said he had already paid her $300,000 and gotten nothing in return, told numerous US media outlets the deal was "absolutely a quid pro quo" and that he had warned Guilfoyle that he would go public about their dealings if she could not rectify the situation.

## DR Congo: Kabila’s move towards M23 raises questions over peace

https://p.dw.com/p/5ODBuEyeing a political comeback? Former DRC President Joseph Kabila is a fierce critic of President Felix TshisekediImage: Jospin Mwisha/AFPAdvertisementJoseph Kabila's decision to lead a new political-military movement, the Front for the Refoundation of the Nation (FRN), puts the former president of the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) in more direct confrontation with President Felix Tshisekedi's government. Kabila announced the FRN on October 12 in Goma, eastern DRC, at his residence, where leaders of the AFC/M23 rebel movements joined him.

FRN coalition adviser, Claude Ibalanky, said the group would unite opposition forces, including figures associated with the AFC/M23 movement. The new structure succeeds the Congo River Alliance (AFC), which had been allied with the M23 rebellion. Its leadership includes former AFC leader Corneille Nangaa and M23 political leader Bertrand Bisimwa. Sultani Makenga, a senior M23 military commander, was also assigned a senior role in the new organization.

The announcement comes as diplomatic initiatives seek to end fighting between Congolese government forces and the rebellion.

Congolese authorities have accused Kabila of supporting the insurgency, allegations he has denied. On September 30, 2025, a military court sentenced him to death in absentia after convicting him of treason, war crimes and other offenses linked to the conflict in eastern DRC. Kabila has rejected the case as politically motivated.

Adolph Amisi Makutano, an official of the ruling Union for Democracy and Social Progress (UDPS), criticized the former president for taking a more public role.

"It was a lack of courage that made this gentleman drag his feet. They held several meetings and today he is being pushed to step out into the open. But it is a disgrace for the DRC," Makutano said.

Eastern DRC has experienced decades of conflict involving armed groups, competition over territory andmineral resources, and tensions with neighboring countries.

The aftermath of Rwanda's 1994 genocide against the Tutsicontributed to successive regional conflicts, as armed groups and refugees crossed borders and neighboring states became involved in Congo's civil wars.

M23 takes its name from a March 23, 2009, peace agreement. The movement says the Congolese government failed to honor commitments to integrate its fighters and protect minority Congolese Tutsis.

Kinshasa, the United Nations (UN) and Western governments haveaccused Rwanda of supporting M23 and exercising influence over its operations. Rwanda denies backing the rebellion and cites security threats posed by armed groups operating in Congo. In 2025, M23 captured Goma and Bukavu, two major cities in eastern Congo. Its territorial gains weakened government control across parts of the region, displaced civilians and intensified an already severe humanitarian crisis.

Kabila's leadership of the new front raises questions about whether he intends to consolidate opposition to Tshisekedi, strengthen the rebellion's political leadership, or pursue both.

Alex Vines, Africa program director at the European Council on Foreign Relations, sees the move as evidence that Kabila remains politically ambitious. "It shows Kabila's political ambition is alive and kicking and he is prepared to challenge Tshisekedi," Vines told DW.

Timo Roujean, head of the Konrad Adenauer Foundation's office in Kinshasa, cautioned against assessing Kabila's potential support based on ethnic identity alone. He told DW that economic performance and political dynamics in Kabila's home region of Katanga are also relevant.

The new front could give the movement more explicitly political leadership, but it remains unclear whether the reorganization will broaden its domestic support or alter its negotiating position. Conflict analyst Cliffton Chifuwe told DW the alliance does not rule out a negotiated settlement, but could make a durable agreement harder to achieve.

"The Kabila alliance with M23 does not make peace impossible. However, it raises the threshold for achieving that settlement and its ability to hold," Chifuwe told DW.

For Chifuwe, lasting peace requires more than a cessation of hostilities. It also depends on credible security arrangements, the restoration of state authority, humanitarian access, and conditions that allow displaced people to return safely. The new FRN coalition also raises questions for mediators about whether existing peace frameworks can accommodate the political realignment and whether the parties involved can ensure compliance with any agreement.

As this new development plays out, the central issue for civilians in eastern Congo remains whether the leadership change will open a path to settlement or make an already complex conflict harder to resolve.

## India: Can &apos;cockroaches&apos; threaten Modi&apos;s political grip?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OCX6Despite the momentum, the CJP has not been able to replicate its success from JulyImage: Adnan Abidi/REUTERSAdvertisementIndia's Cockroach Janata Party (CJP) may have anticipated pushback from the government after its first round of protests in July, but the scale of barricading and detentions that greeted its leaders in a fortress-like New Delhi on October 10 was unlike anything they had anticipated.

The CJP began as a satirical online movement after the chief justice of India, Surya Kant, referred to unemployed young people as "cockroaches" in an offhand remark. The name is an ironic riff on the name of Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi's ruling Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP).

In its latest round of agitation, they are demanding the resignation of the country's controversy-hit Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar, after more than 130 million names were reportedly removed from electoral rolls nationwide.

CJP founder Abhijeet Dipke, accompanied by co-conveners Ashutosh Ranka and Saurav Das, was detained from the plane in which he landed in the national capital on Saturday.

Meanwhile, police were stationed outside the homes of several other faces associated with the movement, including social media personality Dhruv Rathee, who has nearly 20 million followers on Instagram.

In central Delhi, buses were loaded with protesters, many of whom were taken to far-flung centers, where they were kept for hours. According to local media, at least 3,000 people were detained across the city.

The protests come as Modi marks 25 uninterrupted years leading elected governments, first in Gujarat state as chief minister and then as prime minister of India.

Ironically, "this celebration has happened at the time when he's politically at his weakest," Delhi-based writer Nilanjan Mukhopadhyay told DW. Mukhopadhyay is the author of Modi's 2013 biography "Narendra Modi: The Man, The Times."

He contrasted the government's current response with its approach in July, when thousands of young protesters took to the streets to demand the resignation of Modi's education minister over repeated exam paper leaks.

At the time, authorities had used batons, detentions and pellets to disperse the crowds gathered in central Delhi, which had been on the streets for days.

This time, authorities shifted to mass security deployments and preemptive action on a large scale. This included notices to those associated with earlier protests, transport cuts, the internet cutoff and airport arrests.

"The government has learned from its previous experience. They didn't allow people to sit at Jantar Mantar for any length of time, so it couldn't balloon," Pearl Pandya, senior analyst for Asia at global monitor ACLED, told DW.

"What's worrying is whether this becomes a playbook for the future — that you are not allowed to protest in the first place," she added, referring to the government's decision to remove the 18th-century observatory Jantar Mantar in central Delhi from the list of designated protest sites.

Authorities, however, have announced three new designated protest sites in the national capital.

Mukhopadhyay had a different reading of the situation. "The scale of this deployment is actually a defeat for Mr. Modi," he said.

Mukhopadhyay argued that it was already a setback for the government if it was forced to deploy security forces on such a scale in response to protests from a completely new socio-political movement.

Despite the momentum, the CJP has not been able to replicate its success from July, with the protests against Kumar being led primarily by opposition leaders and student organizations.

"Education was far more of a cross-cutting issue... the right to vote is still seen as an intrinsically political one, where it's the opposition versus the BJP," Pandya said.

The CJP, however, has long maintained that it is non-political. This is often cited as the reason for them amassing such a large following of young followers.

Mukhopadhyay criticized that stance, saying: "You cannot raise issues about electoral neutrality... without saying that these are political issues."

Despite the differences between the previous round of CJP protests in July and the current wave of demonstrations, sociologist Surinder Singh Jodhka emphasized continuity.

"I don't think they're qualitatively different. Once again, young people are the ones who are mobilized, and they are much more encouraged because the earlier round of protests was successful," he told DW. "And it's also asking similar questions about institutions, about democracy, about transparency and accountability."

"There is a new hope for those who had earlier given up on India, and felt like the right wing was in complete control," Jodhka added.

Dismissing comparisons with volatile protests in neighboring Nepal and Bangladesh, which brought down their governments, the analysts pointed to India's strong institutions as a safeguard against a dramatic change of government.

"India has had a very strong state system and state institutions," Jodhka explained, highlighting that even the opposition parties were asking for "the revival of institutions, rather than an overthrow of the state."

Pandya also stated that faith in the judiciary "would prevent these protests from tipping over into a popular revolution which unseats the government."

Nevertheless, she said, sustained discontent "might come to bite the government in 2029... or before that in the state elections."

It could also erode Modi's popularity, which has been a defining feature of Indian politics over the past decade, the expert indicated.

For Mukhopadhyay, the most troubling aspect of the current protests is the erosion of the electoral body's credibility.

Recalling the pride he felt when he registered to vote at 21, he drew comparisons with young Indian voters today. "The youngsters are now fighting for their vote."

## Nord Stream: Germany puts suspected Ukrainian pipeline saboteur on trial

https://p.dw.com/p/5OByzMassive quantities of methane gas bubbled on the surface of the Baltic Sea after the explosion of the Nord Stream pipelinesImage: Danish Defence Command/ABACAPRESS/IMAGOAdvertisementThe aerial photos made headlines worldwide: On September 26, 2022, massive quantities of methane gas bubbled on the surface of the Baltic Sea near the island of Bornholm. Unknown people had blown up three of the four sections of the Nord Stream 1 and 2 gas pipelines from Russia to Germany.

The attack, which occurred seven months after Moscow launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine, meant the end of a mega energy project between Germany and Russia in which both sides had invested billions, and which secured affordable Russian gas for Germany. However, the flow of gas via Nord Stream 1 had been restricted or stopped several times by Russia since the Ukraine war began; Nord Stream 2 was not yet operational.

From October 14, Ukrainian citizen Serhii K. is facing trial before a court in the northern German city of Hamburg. He was arrested in Italy in August 2025 on a European arrest warrant and later extradited to Germany. This week he must answer for the attacks before the Hanseatic Higher Regional Court. He is accused of being an accomplice to a war crime by attacking civil energy infrastructure, disruption of public services, causing an explosion and destroying structures.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has repeatedly said that Ukraine was not involved in the 2022 attack on the Nord Stream underwater gas pipelines.

"Ukraine has never officially taken part in any operation related to the blowing up of Nord Stream," Zelenskyy told reporters again in August in response to a question about the ongoing investigation.

The fact that the accused is Ukrainian is especially politically sensitive. Germany continues to support the country after it was attacked by Russia: militarily, financially, with humanitarian aid and by taking in about 1.3 million refugees.

Critics of Germany's support for Ukraine, especially the right-wing extremist Alternative for Germany (AfD) and the left-wing populist Alliance for Social Justice and Economic Reason (BSW) want to improve relations with Moscow, resume energy deliveries from Russia – including via a repaired Nord Stream pipeline — and stop supporting Ukraine. Some of them have practically delivered their verdict on Serhii K., although the trial has not yet begun, and assume that Ukraine's political or military leadership was behind the attack on the pipeline.

Sahra Wagenknecht, former leader of the BSW, told news agency Reuters in 2025 following Serhii K.'s arrest: "It is totally absurd that Germany spends many billions on aid for Ukraine but never demands an explanation from [President Volodymyr] Zelenskyy. Questions should also be raised about possible compensation."

Alice Weidel, co-leader of the AfD, has shared similar sentiments. In early 2026, she asserted that the German government must seek financial repayment from Ukraine. If the AfD came into power, she would demand Kyiv compensate Germany for blowing up the pipeline and pay for its repair. According to her, a country that does such things is "no friend of Germany." She has, however, failed to provide proof that Ukraine was behind the attack.

In an interview with public broadcaster Deutschlandfunk in 2023, German naval commander Göran Swistek pointed out that, given the technical, financial, and organizational effort involved, “there is likely a state-run structure behind it.” In his opinion, a group of amateurs could not have carried out the act.

However, he considered it unlikely that this state could be Ukraine, since Ukraine, after all, would not want to jeopardize German support. At the time, Swistek thought a false lead pointing to Russia was possible.

The German-Russian Nord Stream project had many opponents from the beginning, including Poland, the US, the Baltic countries and — especially since Russia's full-scale invasion — Ukraine. That is why investigators always ask: Who benefits from the explosion?

Henning Hoff from the German Council on Foreign Relations (DGAP) thinks it is important to shed light on the background context.

"By halting supplies or using the threat to do so as leverage, Russia exerted immense pressure on Germany at the time, and gas markets went haywire," he told DW. "The explosion thwarted Russian attempts to extort Germany, prices for natural gas sank. I think one could say: The explosion was in German interests."

## 125 years of Thomas Mann&apos;s &apos;Buddenbrooks&apos;

https://p.dw.com/p/2z4J2Thomas MannImage: picture-alliance/dpaAdvertisementWho would have imagined it in the beginning? A 26-year-old man without a high school diploma, but greatly ambitious, writes a two-pound book with a melancholy undertone. "I'm not exactly delighted," his publisher initially responded. But he ended up publishing the novel anyway, more out of goodwill than conviction. And the rest is history.

For many, Thomas Mann's Buddenbrooks is considered THE German social novel. It is indeed a classic, and one that garnered the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1929. Global fame, an international best-seller, and required reading for many German students. Anyone who mentions Thomas Mann thinks of Buddenbrooks in the same breath. Why is that?

Does the explanation lie in the autobiographical connections between the work and the author?

Thomas Mann was born in Lübeck to a merchant family, but his artistic aspirations did not fit in well in the middle-class mainstream. This struggle inspired Mann's first work. The inability of this sensitive bohemian to carry on the family's time-honored business after the death of his father was sniffed at and derided.

Reflecting the situation of his novel's character Hanno, Mann had a privileged life in a wealthy family, but one that was at risk of collapse. The aspiring novelist also had to withstand disappointing the expectations of others. He was forced to defend his dream of an artistic life. Yet as much as he was inspired by real life, he forcefully altered these biographical details to place his unique literary mark on his writing.

But does that really matter in light of people who choose not to make the distinction between an author's life and his work? Even though the name "Lübeck" does not appear in the novel, citizens there felt markedly insulted after the publication of Buddenbrooks in 1901.

That likely lies in the fact that the image the author portrayed was not one of an idyllic northern German city, but one of a place that had difficulty blending tradition with modernity.

This contradiction is also seen in each of the figures in the novel. Nothing is like it appears. Mann lays down this foundation of this skepticism from the very beginning, in a scene taking place on a very ordinary Thursday in October 1835. The Buddenbrook family comes together for their ordinary late lunch.

"There they all sat, on heavy, high-backed chairs, consuming good heavy food from good silver plates, drinking full-bodied wines and expressing their views freely on all subjects. When they began to talk shop, they slipped unconsciously more and more into dialect, and used the clumsy but comfortable idioms that seemed to embody to them the business efficiency and the easy well-being of their community. Sometimes they even used an over-drawn pronunciation by way of making fun of themselves and each other, and relished their clipped phrases and exaggerated vowels with the same heartiness they did their food."

There sits the leader of the family company, Johann Buddenbrook the Elder, and his wife, his son and counsel Jean, along with his family. The grandchildren are also there: Tony the dreamer, the quarrelsome rebel Christian, and Thomas, the eldest grandchild.

It is Thomas who is supposed to later take on the responsibility of continuing the family tradition, regardless of the cost. Mann weaves his narrative work starting with this main family, and then across several generations.

But appearances in the family are deceptive. The reader senses it's all headed toward a downfall. The new house is taken over from a family that was once as illustrious as the Buddenbrooks, but faced financial ruin and had to leave.

Johann and his family aim to make a better go of it. They make decisions on what is demanded economically, but fail — financially, interpersonally, and mentally.

Tony and Thomas end up falling in love with people not befitting their social standing, but do not have the courage to stand up to their parents. They enter marriages of convenience, yet it is these partnerships that paradoxically set disaster in motion.

From an early age, Thomas Mann was influenced by Schopenhauer's pessimistic notion of the will being a blind force at work in the world. Thus, there is likewise no escape for the Buddenbrooks. They speak of emotions and morality, yet it is money and economics that steer the ship. Failure, decline and even death seem to be the only viable outcome.

Mann also punctuates this hopelessness in a stylistic manner, by consistently repeating entire passages word for word, or evoking motifs such as the family registry or the estate in all their various forms.

The extent of this demise appears linguistically molded and exaggerated. The Buddenbrooks, this proud north German family of refinement, actually speaks the vernacular studded with empty French expressions. "Je, den Düwel ook, c'est la question, ma très chère demoiselle!"

The lowly servants, the courtesans, and the demi-monde: all of them speak the language of the street, raw and unpolished. In a virtuoso manner, Thomas Mann orchestrated this mass of voices into a panorama of society in the 19th century. The snobbishness on the part of the Buddenbrooks thus appears as a relic of a long lost time. It's a symbol of their inability to adapt to change and place their faith in progress.

Yet the fact that this extensive work spanning several generations does not get weighed down by aesthetic excess is purely due to Mann's brilliance as an author. He is able to bring every one of his numerous characters to life in words. Life that is full of complexity, contradictions and ambiguities. Some of these characters accompany the reader page for page; others appear only briefly.

And yet one begins to contemplate how someone like Grünlich could become a dodgy, unscrupulous husband and catches oneself wanting to chase the fearful Hanno out of the music conservatory and yell to him: "Go, throw yourself into life! You are not a failure." It is precisely this empathy that spurs one on to read through the night and prompts one to chuckle at all the intricate sentences and philosophical digressions.

Thomas Mann was born into a privileged Lübeck merchant family in 1875. He died in Zurich, Switzerland in 1955. "I am a townsman, a citizen, a child and a great-grandchild of German bourgeois society," the author once wrote about himself. Mann received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1929 for his debut novel, Buddenbrooks. He also garnered numerous other prizes and honors for his extensive work, including Death in Venice (1911), The Magic Mountain (1924), and the tetralogy Joseph and His Brothers (1933-1943). He emigrated to Switzerland in 1933, and to the US in 1938. From there, he made his famous radio commentaries in which he agitated against the Nazis. He returned to Switzerland in 1952.

## What is holding back Cyprus&apos;s Schengen ambitions?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OAO8There are nine designated crossing points between the internationally recognized Republic of Cyprus and the Turkish-controlled northern part of the island. Pictured here: the Leda Street crossing point in NicosiaImage: Mpalis/Depositphotos/IMAGOAdvertisementCyprus is closer than ever to joining the Schengen Area.

Together with Ireland, Cyprus is currently the only member of the European Union not to be part of Europe's border-free travel zone.

After years of preparations and the implementation of 120 technical recommendations, Nicosia received a positive assessment from the European Commission in July, confirming its technical readiness.

The recommendations, issued as part of EU evaluations dating back to 2021, covered areas including border controls, visa policy, access to European security databases and police cooperation. Their implementation required technological upgrades as well as legislative and administrative reforms.

On October 1, Cyprus's Deputy Minister for Migration and International Protection, Nicholas Ioannides, confirmed that "all recommendations made by the European Commission are now considered to have been implemented."

According to Ioannides, Cyprus is technically ready to join Schengen, with the next step being to secure the necessary political agreement.

However, a positive technical assessment alone is not enough for Cyprus to join the Schengen Area.

Accession requires a unanimous decision by the EU Council following consultation with the European Parliament.

According to information obtained by DW, Austria and the Netherlands have already informally expressed reservations over security concerns linked to the Green Line, which divides the island of Cyprus, and the special status of the British Sovereign Base Areas.

Although neither country has officially threatened to veto Cyprus's accession, their reservations carry particular weight.

Both Austria and the Netherlands have previously raised objections to the expansion of the Schengen Area to Bulgaria and Romania.

Perhaps the most significant obstacle is the Green Line, the approximately 180-kilometer-long (112 miles) ceasefire line that has divided Cyprus since Turkey's military intervention in 1974.

It separates the areas controlled by the internationally recognized Republic of Cyprus from the Turkish-controlled northern part of the island.

The buffer zone is monitored by the United Nations Peacekeeping Force in Cyprus (UNFICYP), while the Green Line itself is not an internationally recognized state border.

Crossings between the two sides take place at nine designated crossing points and are governed by a special EU regulation.

This unique arrangement poses a challenge to Schengen membership, as the Republic of Cyprus does not exercise effective control along the entire length of the line. At the same time, Nicosia rejects any arrangement that could turn the Green Line into an external border, either legally or even symbolically.

"The issue raised by some Schengen countries is that the Republic of Cyprus does not control the entire Green Line. It may control the crossing points, but there are also open stretches of land," Dr. Nasia Hadjigeorgiou, associate professor of Transitional Justice and Human Rights at the School of Law of UCLan Cyprus, told DW.

"For it to be considered to have full control, something would have to change. Either the Cyprus problem would have to be resolved, or a strictly controlled physical border would have to be established. There is no other way," said Hadjigeorgiou.

Another obstacle to Cyprus's Schengen ambitions is the presence of the British Sovereign Base Areas of Akrotiri and Dhekelia, which remained under UK sovereignty when Cyprus gained independence in 1960.

Together, the two areas cover 254 square kilometers (98 square miles), almost 3% of the island, and remain British sovereign territory.

The main complication concerns the Dhekelia base area, which borders both the territory controlled by the Republic of Cyprus and the self-declared Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus, which is not internationally recognized.

Two crossing points, at Pergamos and Strovilia, are located in this area, where checks are carried out by British authorities. The UK is no longer a member of the European Union.

Dr. Nasia Hadjigeorgiou explains the difficulties arising from this unusual arrangement: "This is problematic because the area in question is controlled on one side by the authorities in the occupied north and on the other by the British Sovereign Base Areas."

"As a result, the United Kingdom would be controlling who enters and leaves the Schengen Area. This is an unusual situation, since it is normally the Schengen member states themselves that decide who can enter and leave the area," she said.

According to information obtained by DW, Nicosia and the British Sovereign Base Areas have already begun discussions on upgrading border control procedures at Pergamos and Strovilia to bring them into line with EU requirements.

In the meantime, Nicosia has stepped up its diplomatic efforts to secure the necessary consensus.

Deputy Minister for Migration Nicholas Ioannides held bilateral meetings with his counterparts from Austria, the Netherlands and Finland on October 1 on the sidelines of the EU Justice and Home Affairs Council meeting in Luxembourg. Cyprus's accession to Schengen was among the issues discussed.

Cyprus also secured significant political backing on October 7 during the summit of the nine Mediterranean EU member states (MED9) in Croatia.

In their joint declaration, the leaders welcomed the European Commission's positive assessment and called for the timely completion of Cyprus's accession process.

Nevertheless, diplomatic sources speaking to DW on condition of anonymity cautioned that despite the positive signals, Cyprus's entry into the Schengen Area should by no means be taken for granted.
