## German Olympic Committee names Munich as bid city choice

https://p.dw.com/p/5NKJ2Munich previously hosted the 1972 Summer Olympics.Image: Sven Hoppe/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementThe German Olympic Sports Confederation (DOSB) on Saturday announced it chose Munich as Germany's entry to bid for at least the next three Olympic Summer Games.

Ahead of the vote, former Olympic champion and new Sports Minister Ronald Rauhe said: "We have an international task to fulfill and must enter the race with the city or region that gives Germany the greatest chances internationally. The decision on that is made by sport, and I assume that it will make it with a great deal of responsibility."

Germany's president Frank-Walter Steinmeier said the olympic bid represented a huge opportunity for all of Germany to "show that we are a land of opportunity."

According to the Munich bid, all competitions would take place in Bavaria, except for the football matches which would be played in Stuttgart and the sailing competitions in the nothern city of Kiel.

As many as 17.6 million tickets could be sold for the Games in Cologne-Rhine-Ruhr, more than ever before at a Games, the premier of North Rhine-Westphalia said recently.

Munich was campaigning after two failed bids for the 2018 and 2022 Winter Games and having previously hosted the 1972 Summer Olympics.

Up until Thursday, Berlin had been among the cities in contention, but the city's mayor announced that the city would withdraw from the race.

The withdrawal comes after the socialist Left Party's victory in the German capital's elections last weekend. The party's lead candidate and potential mayor, Elif Eralp, said she did not support Berlin's bid.

On Friday, Sports Minister Ronald Rauhe said he would welcome a German Olympic bid for the 2040 Games, with it coinciding with an important historical milestone.

"2040 will mark the 50th anniversary of reunification. Here we could send a great signal that also aligns with the values of the Olympic Games," Rauhe told German news agency dpa ahead of the deciding vote on Saturday.

"Based on my personal history, 2040 would be my favorite date. That has to do with the fact that I'm a Berlin child. As a schoolchild, I experienced what happens to people when a wall falls, when people grow together again, when two population groups that had been separated become a community again,” Rauhe said.

"Ultimately, though, one thing matters above all: bringing the Olympic and Paralympic Games back to our country. No matter when," Rauhe said.

## Propaganda in Russian schools prepares students to die

https://p.dw.com/p/5NE46Patriotism has become a core part of the national curriculum since 2022Image: Konstanin Kokoshkin/Pivcario/picture allianceAdvertisementEarlier this year, several schools in Novosibirsk, a city of 1.6 million in southern Siberia, received a textbook on patriotism from the authorities. According to students, with whom DW spoke to on condition of anonymity, in the book the authors urge children to prepare themselves to die for their country. Furthermore, they equate homosexuality with pedophilia and Disney cartoons with pornography, and give instructions on how women should use their "charms." Europeans are described as "thieves, murderers and liars."

The textbook, which provides "materials for spiritual, moral, and patriotic upbringing" is supposed to be used as part of "patriotism lessons" that have been on the curriculum since 2022. Though it is not included on the national list of core curriculum books, the content reflects the Kremlin's intentions to spread propaganda in educational institutions.

One of the main topics of the book is the war in Ukraine and the idea that dying for one's country is a blessing is repeated several times: "Love always involves sacrifice," writes one of the authors. "The sacrifice of a defender of the homeland is their life."

Elsewhere in the book, the authors make constant claims that Russia is better than Western countries. They also say that children are "mentally crippled" because of "computer games, Hollywood action films, pornography and Disney cartoons."

They argue that European nations that used to be colonial empires legitimized "murder, lies and plunder" through the conquest and subjugation of new territories. Russia, on the other hand, "never exploited its numerous peoples," they say, but merely improved their living conditions. The book makes no mention of the culture of Indigenous peoples in what is Russia today, or of any religions other than Christianity and "Slavic paganism."

For Ilya, a 16-year-old student in Novosibirsk who has read the book and whose name has been changed, the idea of dying for his country is anathema. "It's proof that the country wants more cannon fodder," he told DW, adding that "dying for the country" actually meant dying for "the president's interests and ambitions."

He said that a few years ago, he had started watching the videos of Alexei Navalny, the Kremlin critic and opposition leader who died in jail in 2024 "more for fun than for any other reason." He said it was Navalny's 2021 investigation into Russian President Vladimir Putin's Black Sea palace that had first drawn his attention.

But it was when the authorities decided to block access to the US platforms YouTube and Discord that he began to oppose the government. He set up a VPN out of "contrariness" he said, looking for information about other opposition figures and reading the independent media. As he found out more about the cruelty and senselessness of the war, he criticized the government more strongly.

He confirmed that propaganda had become an integral part of the curriculum in the past four years. On top of the "Conversations about Important Things" that take place every Monday morning, there are now also subjects such as "Fundamentals of Homeland Security and Defense." He said that people who have taken part in the war in Ukraine are invited to lessons focusing on students' future and decisions regarding what career path to take.

Even outside of the classroom, students are encouraged to be "patriotic." Ilya said that a student had been reprimanded by the principal for wearing a sweater with a slogan written in English on it. "Young man, do you realize that your sweater has words on it in the language of unfriendly countries?" she reportedly asked him. "A call to the Federal Security Service will take care of that!"

Ilya also recalled that a school assembly held to mark May 9, which commemorates the Soviet Union's victory over Nazi Germany in 1945, the principal and her deputy had compared what is known in Russia as the "Great Patriotic War" to the current war in Ukraine. They had said that Russia would once again be steadfast and emerge victorious.

Alexei (name changed), another 16-year-old student in Novosibirsk, told DW that many of his classmates avoided going to "patriotic events." He too said that the blocking of YouTube had made a difference to him. "I looked into why it was blocked," he said. "Because nothing happens by chance. And that's how my interest in politics began." That's when he realized that the authorities wanted to get rid of inconvenient citizens and suppress free speech. "I love freedom, so it was hard," he said.

Ilya thinks that most students don't take the propaganda seriously. "On the one hand, we laugh about it in class, at least my friends do," he said. "On the other, we don't care. Some simply don't listen. We're very good at being quiet and not talking much about it,"

Dima Zicer is an educator and the author of the Russian-language podcast "Love, Don't Discipline" who moved to Estonia after the full-scale invasion of Ukraine. He told DW that "many students are happy to be part of a pro-Putin majority that doesn't think about things, according to the principle: 'We'll crush them all; we're stronger than everyone else. Everyone will be scared of us.'"

He said that there was no statistical data to back his argument, but he said that he had drawn his conclusions from his extensive experience working with Russian children, as well as his communication with parents. He said that these often only became aware of how serious the situation was when their children started saying things such as "We'll crush them" or "Putin is great," as well as talking about supposed Nazis and fascists in Ukraine.

Alexei and Ilya, who think differently and want the war in Ukraine to end, are worried about the unstable situation in Russia. They want to leave the country after graduating.

## South Korea&apos;s gender pay gap grows as women get older

https://p.dw.com/p/5NHhlCaregiving responsibilities still fall on women, pushing them into lower-paid jobs when they try to return to work. Image: DWAdvertisementAt 27, a woman in South Korea earns 8% less than male peers. By her late 50s, she earns 50% less. This is according to government wage data published in a recent Human Rights Watch (HRW) report.

South Korea is a global technology and industrial powerhouse, but its gender pay gap has remained the highest in the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) since comparable records began in 1992.

"The gender division of labor inside the home leads directly to a gender division of labor in the labor market," Min Hee Kim, a Korean activist for women labor struggles, told DW.

The problem, she argues, is not simply that women are paid less than men. As women age, the kinds of work available to them narrows and the consequences of lower pay, career breaks and precarious jobs accumulate.

For the report, "Too Old and a Woman," HRW interviewed 41 South Korean women aged 28 to 87 about their working lives.

Discrimination has changed form rather than disappeared, Kim said. Until the 1980s, it was legal for employers to fire women simply for getting married, a practice known as "marriage-based dismissal," where employers assumed a married woman would soon leave to raise children anyway.

Today, "it is difficult to openly say, 'We won't hire you because you're a woman,'" Kim added. But caregiving responsibilities still fall on women, pushing them into lower-paid jobs when they try to return to work.

According to the HRW report, women's university attainment has passed men's since the 1980s. Higher education would suggest equal opportunity after graduation, but the job market says otherwise.

One year after graduating, women already earn 22% less than men, according to a 2024 study.

Many women told HRW that after leaving work for marriage or children, chances of returning to the workforce were limited to a handful of jobs such as call centers, cleaning or caregiving.

Women who opt to focus on their career told HRW they hit a glass ceiling at work. Women held 18% of manager jobs in 2024, and only five of 38 senior civil servants were women.

Women's wages peak in their late 30s and then steadily decline, according to Labor Ministry survey data cited by HRW.

South Korea's Gender Ministry told HRW that career interruptions, along with occupational segregation and discrimination within organizations, drive the gap.

J. Mi-sook, 53, told HRW that her three years of experience weren't recognized by her next employer, so she started at entry level on a lower salary.

The longer women spend outside the workforce, the less experience they accumulate on their resumes, which results in less pay and less work stability.

Kim pointed to structural discrimination, a concept from the report that HRW describes as discrimination so embedded in social practices and institutions that even the people harmed by it may not recognize it as a problem.

"When you are alone, it is easy to think, 'It's because I'm not capable enough,' or 'It's because I'm getting older,'" she said. It's only when women who've had similar experiences meet and talk, she added, that they realize, "this wasn't just my problem."

Park Jae-soon, 61, was forced to retire at 60 from a utility company and now works part-time as a care worker. Her annual income fell from 55 million won ($40,500/€35,600) to 15.6 million won, a 72% drop.

HRW argues the mandatory retirement age and the "peak wage" system, which lets employers cut older workers' pay in the years before retirement, should both be abolished.

According to data cited in the report, women received 53% of men's National Old Age Pension on average in October 2025, a 47% gap.

"I think what is important here is that this is not simply a problem of middle-aged women having difficulty finding jobs," Kim said.

"The work that women have traditionally done inside the home, like cleaning, cooking, caregiving and managing other people's emotions, is classified as 'work suitable for women' when it enters the labor market, and then low wages are assigned to it," she added.

HRW argues the disadvantage builds stage by stage, from a woman's first paycheck up until her pension.

"Career interruption among women in their 30s is already a pension issue," Kim said. "Part-time work among women in their 40s is a pension issue, and low wages among women in their 50s are already a pension issue," she added.

South Korea's government has said it will pursue a phased increase in the statutory retirement age from 60 to 65.

"This cannot be solved with just one policy. Equal pay for equal work needs to be actually implemented, wage structures need to be transparent, and care needs to be treated as a public responsibility rather than something that is pushed onto individual families and women," Kim said.

The rights activist said the report is an important starting point, but believes its picture is incomplete.

Most interviewees were recruited through civic organizations and largely in Seoul, she noted, while disabled women, migrant workers and others may experience a different combination of age and gender discrimination.

"The important question is not simply, 'How much discrimination do women face?'" she said. "It is which women, in what positions, become increasingly distanced from which resources and rights as they grow older."
