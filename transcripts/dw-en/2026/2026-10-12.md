## Hundreds of French supermarkets hit by a cyberattack that  defrosted refrigeration systems

https://p.dw.com/p/5OBaTHackers targeted refrigeration systems at hundreds of French supermarkets overnight, spoiling tons of foodImage: Fred Tanneau/AFPAdvertisementHundreds of French supermarkets were forced to throw away perishable goods on Monday after hackers commandeered refrigeration systems across the country, spoiling stock at major chains in a sophisticated overnight cyberattack.

Hackers destroyed the food by taking control of refrigeration systems at Cooperative U, Leclerc, Les Mousquetaires, and Intermarche stores across the country and then defrosting thousands of freezer and refrigeration units.

During the overnight attack, in some cases, fully-stocked fridges were heated up to 50 degrees Celsius (122 degrees Fahrenheit) for more than six hours.

Prosecutors in Paris confirmed the attacks and said fires had been reported at some locations.

Intermarche said impacted stores had no choice but to "throw away all fresh and frozen products."

Leclerc told French news agency AFP that "a cyberattack affected one of its refrigeration equipment suppliers," triggering "repercussions on the operation of refrigeration systems in several stores."

The refrigeration systems targeted were reportedly from Danish supplier Danfoss, something suggested by Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu's office, which reportedly identified "a vulnerability in software supplied by a Danish company and has contacted the vendor."

Danfoss said ​it was "aware of incidents in France affecting a limited number of refrigeration management systems," adding that the units in question "were running outdated firmware."

Danfoss said it had "long advised customers to keep supported systems updated with the latest firmware and to migrate legacy systems to current-generation solutions."

Prime Minister Lecornu's office has tasked the French cybersecurity agency ANSSI with investigating the attack and is "liaising with its European counterparts, as other countries may be affected."

"Security issues" were also reported in Belgium, Luxembourg and the Netherlands, with Dutch supermarket chain Ahold Delhaize saying it had taken refrigeration systems offline to protect them, though the company added that it saw no connection to the French attacks.

It is currently unclear who could have been responsible for the cyberattack, and no group or individual has yet to claim responsibility.

The incident comes amid growing concern over vulnerabilities related to industrial ransomware and cyberattacks by malign actors in general, as well as heightened sensitivity toward Russian hybrid attacks in the midst of rising tensions over the war in Ukraine.

Last month, French President Emmanuel Macron — a strong supporter of Kyiv's self-defense against Russia — ordered measures put in place to protect France's critical infrastructure from Kremlin "hybrid attacks."

Cooperative U CEO Dominique Schelcher, wrote on X: "Targeting equipment essential to the preservation of fresh products and to the food supply of the French people is extremely serious and is unprecedented. It is unacceptable."

"Food security requires more attention at all levels," wrote EU Food and Agriculture Commissioner Christophe Hansen on X, who condemned the attack, adding that the "exploitation of food" is now a "reality."

## Fact check: Viral videos falsely linked to Riyadh airport attacks

https://p.dw.com/p/5OAn7Riyadh's King Khalid International Aiport has been targeted by Yemen's Iran-backed HouthisImage: Social Media/REUTERSAdvertisementYemen's Houthi rebels have claimed responsibility for a series of attacks on Saudi Arabia's King Khalid International Airport in recent days. At least 16 people have been killed since last Thursday, according to Saudi authorities.

As news of the attacks spreads, social media users have been sharing videos that they claim show the strikes. While some authentic footage of the attacks and their aftermath has circulated online, DW Fact check found that several viral videos are not genuine and show signs of being AI-generated.

Claim: This post with 1.7 million views on X, claims it shows the moment of an attack on the airport.

The video has been online since March 2026 and could not show the most recent attacks on the airport.

Hectic camera movements and blurry footage help conceal glitches that often point to AI-generated content. However, our frame-by-frame analysis identified several inconsistencies. That suggests the video was AI-generated.

In the video, people run toward an exit as an explosion occurs outside. Despite the apparent danger, they continue moving toward the blast, seemingly through a glass door. This reaction is implausible.

Around 10 seconds into the video, an image of a hand holding a smartphone appears on the side of a man's head.

Meta's AI identification tool finds AI signatures in the video and concludes it was created using generative AI.

Many shared the video as authentic, including the Times of Israel– its post had more than 256,000 views at the time of writing.

The video has appeared in many languages, including Arabic, English, Russian, Spanish, Turkish, Nepali, and it has been shared on YouTube, Instagram, TikTok,  Facebookand other platforms.

Claim: In this post on X with 2.9 million views, a user claims it shows "new video footage of the moment King Khalid International Airport in the Saudi capital Riyadh was targeted."

The video is AI-generated and contains several inconsistencies when compared with the real airport. Using a reverse image search, we found the photograph used to generate the fake video.

The original image was uploaded to stock photo database Shutterstockin 2016. Although it was taken from a slightly different angle it shows many visual cues that overlap with the video, including the white triangular geometric patterns of the airport building roof, the dome to the left, the luggage transport vehicles and the open and closed doors of the plane.

Other elements are missing. In the video, the light pole to the left of the aircraft appears without any lamps attached. The video is grainy and contains some visual glitches where parts of the image suddenly become blurry.

When the supposed missile strikes the plane, a nearby vehicle significantly changes its shape as it is thrown into the air. Another inconsistency is that, despite the missile's massive explosion and a resulting fireball, nearby objects do not cast corresponding shadows.

Whereas the October 8 attack at King Khalid International Airport did reportedly badly damage a plane, this video does not show the moment of attack.

Claim: This TikTok postwith more than 301,000 views claims that the footage was recorded "just now from the heart of Riyadh Airport."

This video had already circulated online in March 2026, months before the attack. We found the video was shared in Greek, Albanian, Turkish and Russian.

The footage contains several visual anomalies. Parts of the image appear blurry, and some people seem to merge into one another. In addition, a row of red lights in the terminal switches on one second before the supposed explosion becomes visible through the window.

The clearest indication that the video is AI-generated is the way people move through the queue barriers. At the beginning of the video, several people appear to walk directly through them. After the supposed explosion, some individuals run straight through barriers that appear to end mid-air and are connected on only one side.

Claim: This TikTok post, which has garnered more than 500,000 views, reports on the attacks on the airports in Riyadh and Abha and includes a compilation of videos. We examined the first video.

Analysis using Google Gemini detected that the video was generated, at least in part, with Google AI tools. The clip contains a SynthID watermark in both the images and the audio.

The same video also circulated on Facebook, where it has received more than 157,000 views. On Facebook it has been posted by an account that only shares AI-generated videos, and it is clearly labeled as "AI-content."

All four videos examined by DW Fact check are very short. Two are between six and ten seconds long, while the other two last 13 and 15 seconds, respectively. Such brief videos are easier to create with AI and often contain very little visual information, making it difficult for viewers to verify their authenticity.

## Germany: Labor Minister&apos;s resignation puts a spotlight on cracks within the government

https://p.dw.com/p/5OBW3Bärbel Bas' resignation sent shockwaves through Germany's governmentImage: Christophe Gateau/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementThe week kicked off with a bang: On Monday morning, Bärbel Bas announced her resignation as Labor Minister and as Co-Chair of the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD). "Following the death of my stepdaughter, I realized that I need time to come to terms with my grief," Bärbel Bas wrote in a personal statement to party members.

However, she also cited "the public attacks against me — unfortunately, including some from within the ranks of our coalition partner" as reasons for her resignation. In fact, the pressure on Bas increased significantly in recent days. The centrist coalition government is urgently awaiting key legislative proposals from the Ministry of Labor.

Before the summer recess in July, a commission had already presented comprehensive proposals for reforming Germany's statutory pension system. The coalition partners of SPD and the center-right Christian Democratic Union and Christian Social Union (CDU/CSU) wanted these proposals to be quickly turned into legislation. To date, however, no finalized drafts have been presented.

"The minister is no longer tenable. For months, she has been blocking the reforms our country so urgently needs," railed Christian von Stetten, head of the CDU/CSU parliamentary group's caucus representing medium-sized enterprises, said in reference to Bas in September.

In early October, such criticism was echoed by CDU Economy Minister Katherina Reiche, who demanded that the pension reform be passed by the end of the year. Bas had to submit the bill "promptly," Reiche said, although it was known at the time what a difficult personal situation her cabinet colleague was in at the time.

SPD co-leader, Finance Minister and Vice-Chancellor Lars Klingbeil reacted sharply on Monday, saying the political attacks against Bas had been "disgraceful."  Following a meeting of the SPD leadership, he told journalists in Berlin: "This poisons the political discourse, and — in the interest of preserving our political culture — I hope that everyone will take a step back and reflect on what has actually happened over the past few weeks." He called on all centrist politicians to be careful "that we don't destroy the good people in politics by attacking one another."

Such accusations carry significant weight at a time when the coalition is already mired in a deep crisis.

Adding to this is the new vacancy at the top of the SPD. Without Bas, Klingbeil will lead the SPD alone. A party convention will be scheduled for December or January to elect new leadership for the Social Democrats. It is not certain whether Klingbeil will run again. He received a very poor result during his last reelection as party leader.

Alexander Schweitzer is to succeed Bärbel Bas as Federal Minister of Labor. The former state premier of the state of Rhineland-Palatinate had previously served as that state's Minister of Labor and Social Affairs. Chancellor Friedrich Merz expressed confidence that Schweitzer's experience would ensure " good cooperation within the federal government," adding that he hoped that the pending legislative proposals "will now be quickly approved by the cabinet and can be introduced into the parliamentary process."

The chancellor refuted the accusations leveled by Bas, saying that he had never discussed personal matters with her. Friedrich Merz said he had explicitly advised Bas to take the time she needed to grieve.

Mistrust between the coalition partners has increased significantly, with SPD lawmakers speaking of an increasingly toxic political climate.

For months, the CDU and SPD have been grappling with low poll numbers, disappointing election results, and disputes over key reform projects. Plans for changes to the electricity tax, the introduction of a sugar tax, the pension and the labor market reform — hardly any initiative has been able to proceed without public controversy.

Adding to this is the crisis within the chancellor's party, the CDU, which saw a historic election defeat in the eastern state of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania in September, when it even lost representation in a state parliament for the first time in history.

On Sunday and Monday, the party leadership met for a closed-door session to discuss the way forward.

They produced a four-page document which indicated a shift in priorities, putting more emphasis on social issues: affordable housing, lower electricity prices, better health care, and greater respect for citizens' lifetime achievements are mentioned in the paper. This is seen as a response to criticism that the CDU had been focusing too much on economic and regulatory issues.

The chancellor admitted that voters feel that politicians are not taking their hardships and fears of social decline seriously enough. "We have to work our way out of this slump — and get out of a crisis of confidence," Merz said. "You can't just reinvent a party overnight over a weekend, but we want to show that we're ready for change... and we have to be, given the election results."

The chancellor announced that he would lower the electricity tax for everyone — a promise he had made before the general election in 2025 but had not kept.

With the closed-door meeting paper, the CDU is also sending a signal to the SPD. Instead of a programmatic shift further to the right, it is emphasizing its traditional social values — a stance that resonates well within the Social Democrats.

The chancellor made an effort to emphasize his intention to continue with the current coalition government: "The personnel changes will not call into question the basis of the coalition agreement." He then repeated what he had recently said during a visit to Ukraine: "We need a stable federal government right now in order to remain capable of acting on the international stage."

Both CDU/CSU and SPD know that calling early elections could primarily benefit the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD), which is leading in the polls nationwide. So despite the deep rifts, Klingbeil and Merz currently see no alternative to their coalition.

## Pope lung nodule: Could it be cancer?

https://p.dw.com/p/5OAONPope Leo XIV, who turned 71 in September, had only recently returned from a trip to France when he announced doctors had found a nodule on his lungImage: Grzegorz Galazka/SIPA/picture allianceAdvertisementIt often takes a chest X-ray or CT scan to spot small lung (or pulmonary) nodules, especially when a patient is asymptomatic.

At less than 3 centimeters (1.1 inches), lung nodules are less likely to press against your airway and cause any chest pain or shortness of breath or wheezing.

In the case of Pope Leo XIV, who told a gathering at the Vatican on October 11, 2026, doctors had found a nodule on his lung, it is unclear exactly how large that nodule is.

All we know (at time of writing on October 12, 2026) is that it was "detected at a very early stage," and that it was "well localized."

That suggests it has not spread or metastasized, although its full status is not publicly known. Doctors advised the pontiff to have his lung nodule removed.

Whether his was a chance finding — or whether the pope had presented with symptoms — we do not know.

But "incidental findings of lung nodule(s) in asymptomatic individuals" is considered an "increasingly common clinical dilemma," according to a 2019 paper published in the Annals of Thoracic Medicine.

This "dilemma" rests in part on the fact that biopsies — removing a small sample of tissue from the spot or nodule to determine whether it's malignant (cancerous) — are difficult to perform on small nodules.

The risks involved in a surgical diagnosis are considered "excessive compared to the relatively low prevalence of malignancy in the small nodules."

That's according to a 2017 paper published in the European Respiratory Review. And that may also be the dilemma that Pope Leo's doctors have faced.

Fortunately many — if not most — early-stage lung nodules are benign. It is also possible to have more than one nodule at a time, and they can all be benign.

It's said the pope will need a week to recover after his operation to remove the lung nodule. He is due to travel to Uruguay, Argentina and Peru between November 6-17.

Lung nodules are generally defined as a white spot or lesion on the lung. They have a rounded or irregular shape.

Radiologists refer to their "opacity" because, compared to the surrounding air-filled lung tissue, lung nodules appear as white or gray spots on a CT scan — that is, they have a certain density.

Lung nodules can be caused by old lung infections, foreign particles that are inhaled and get lodged in the lung, inflammation, scar tissue and indeed the onset of cancer.

At more than 3 centimeters, the nodule is defined as a mass. The bigger it gets, the greater the chance that it will obstruct your airway and the lung's ability to function.

Symptoms may start with chest pain, shortness of breath and wheezing. They may then progress to a chronic cough or coughing up of blood, fatigue, hoarseness, loss of appetite, unexplained weight loss and recurring respiratory infections like bronchitis or pneumonia.

It is hard to determine when lung nodules become life-threatening cancer. But one indicator of a lung nodule's malignancy is its size.

At 2 milimeters or smaller, the risk of cancer is very small and remains so up to a nodule size of about 6-7 milimeters.

The risk of cancer becomes 9%-10% at about 8 to 10 milimeters. And at larger than 1 centimeter, the risk of cancer is about 15%.

There are two main categories of lung nodule density: solid and subsolid. Subsolid nodules are further divided into pure ground-glass (nonsolid) and part-solid nodules.

According to a 2026 review article published in the New England Journal of Medicine, subsolid nodules are less prevalent than solid nodules, but they have "a higher likelihood of being malignant."

It is said that radiologists see lung nodules in about 30% of chest CT scans. But that number may be rising.

A Dutch study published in 2023 cited an increase in lung nodule detections from 38% of scans in 2008 to 50% in 2019. The same study said "the proportion of patients in whom significant new nodules (≥ 5 mm) were reported increased from 9% in 2010 to 17% in 2017."

However, this may also be a result of the development of better scanning technology in recent years and the use of artificial intelligence to spot tumors.

Bearing in mind that most lung nodules are benign, lung cancer does tend to start with a lesion or nodule. And lung cancer remains the most common type of cancer and the most common cause of cancer death in the world.

The World Health Organization's International Agency for Research on Cancer says that in 2024 almost 2.65 million people were diagnosed with lung cancer and more than 1.8 million people died from the disease.

## Cyprus closer than ever to joining Schengen but political hurdles relating to the Green Line and British Sovereign Base Areas remain

https://p.dw.com/p/5OAO8There are nine designated crossing points between the internationally recognized Republic of Cyprus and the Turkish-controlled northern part of the island. Pictured here: the Leda Street crossing point in NicosiaImage: Mpalis/Depositphotos/IMAGOAdvertisementCyprus is closer than ever to joining the Schengen Area.

Together with Ireland, Cyprus is currently the only member of the European Union not to be part of Europe's border-free travel zone.

After years of preparations and the implementation of 120 technical recommendations, Nicosia received a positive assessment from the European Commission in July, confirming its technical readiness.

The recommendations, issued as part of EU evaluations dating back to 2021, covered areas including border controls, visa policy, access to European security databases and police cooperation. Their implementation required technological upgrades as well as legislative and administrative reforms.

On October 1, Cyprus's Deputy Minister for Migration and International Protection, Nicholas Ioannides, confirmed that "all recommendations made by the European Commission are now considered to have been implemented."

According to Ioannides, Cyprus is technically ready to join Schengen, with the next step being to secure the necessary political agreement.

However, a positive technical assessment alone is not enough for Cyprus to join the Schengen Area.

Accession requires a unanimous decision by the EU Council following consultation with the European Parliament.

According to information obtained by DW, Austria and the Netherlands have already informally expressed reservations over security concerns linked to the Green Line, which divides the island of Cyprus, and the special status of the British Sovereign Base Areas.

Although neither country has officially threatened to veto Cyprus's accession, their reservations carry particular weight.

Both Austria and the Netherlands have previously raised objections to the expansion of the Schengen Area to Bulgaria and Romania.

Perhaps the most significant obstacle is the Green Line, the approximately 180-kilometer-long (112 miles) ceasefire line that has divided Cyprus since Turkey's military intervention in 1974.

It separates the areas controlled by the internationally recognized Republic of Cyprus from the Turkish-controlled northern part of the island.

The buffer zone is monitored by the United Nations Peacekeeping Force in Cyprus (UNFICYP), while the Green Line itself is not an internationally recognized state border.

Crossings between the two sides take place at nine designated crossing points and are governed by a special EU regulation.

This unique arrangement poses a challenge to Schengen membership, as the Republic of Cyprus does not exercise effective control along the entire length of the line. At the same time, Nicosia rejects any arrangement that could turn the Green Line into an external border, either legally or even symbolically.

"The issue raised by some Schengen countries is that the Republic of Cyprus does not control the entire Green Line. It may control the crossing points, but there are also open stretches of land," Dr. Nasia Hadjigeorgiou, associate professor of Transitional Justice and Human Rights at the School of Law of UCLan Cyprus, told DW.

"For it to be considered to have full control, something would have to change. Either the Cyprus problem would have to be resolved, or a strictly controlled physical border would have to be established. There is no other way," said Hadjigeorgiou.

Another obstacle to Cyprus's Schengen ambitions is the presence of the British Sovereign Base Areas of Akrotiri and Dhekelia, which remained under UK sovereignty when Cyprus gained independence in 1960.

Together, the two areas cover 254 square kilometers (98 square miles), almost 3% of the island, and remain British sovereign territory.

The main complication concerns the Dhekelia base area, which borders both the territory controlled by the Republic of Cyprus and the self-declared Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus, which is not internationally recognized.

Two crossing points, at Pergamos and Strovilia, are located in this area, where checks are carried out by British authorities. The UK is no longer a member of the European Union.

Dr. Nasia Hadjigeorgiou explains the difficulties arising from this unusual arrangement: "This is problematic because the area in question is controlled on one side by the authorities in the occupied north and on the other by the British Sovereign Base Areas."

"As a result, the United Kingdom would be controlling who enters and leaves the Schengen Area. This is an unusual situation, since it is normally the Schengen member states themselves that decide who can enter and leave the area," she said.

According to information obtained by DW, Nicosia and the British Sovereign Base Areas have already begun discussions on upgrading border control procedures at Pergamos and Strovilia to bring them into line with EU requirements.

In the meantime, Nicosia has stepped up its diplomatic efforts to secure the necessary consensus.

Deputy Minister for Migration Nicholas Ioannides held bilateral meetings with his counterparts from Austria, the Netherlands and Finland on October 1 on the sidelines of the EU Justice and Home Affairs Council meeting in Luxembourg. Cyprus's accession to Schengen was among the issues discussed.

Cyprus also secured significant political backing on October 7 during the summit of the nine Mediterranean EU member states (MED9) in Croatia.

In their joint declaration, the leaders welcomed the European Commission's positive assessment and called for the timely completion of Cyprus's accession process.

Nevertheless, diplomatic sources speaking to DW on condition of anonymity cautioned that despite the positive signals, Cyprus's entry into the Schengen Area should by no means be taken for granted.

## How green is Europe&apos;s urban public transport? A data-driven reality check

https://p.dw.com/p/5NBJZElectric buses, like this one in the Belgian capital of Brussels, are becoming increasingly common in European public transport systemsImage: Dursun Aydemir/Anadolu Agency/IMAGOAdvertisementAn electric plug icon on their windows makes them easy to spot in cities like Brussels and Madrid. Quietly navigating the streets, electric buses have become an increasingly common sight on Europe's streets in recent years.

Michela Le Pira, assistant professor of transportation at Italy's University of Catania, says removing tailpipe emissions — the gases and particles generated when a combustion vehicle is running — might be "the easiest strategy to improve public transport."

Most Europeans live in urban settings, and road transport harms air quality. In 2023, almost 300,000 deaths across the EU were caused by air pollution levels exceeding the World Health Organization's (WHO) recommended thresholds.

Of all fine particulate matter (PM2.5) concentrations in European urban areas, about 14% come from road transport, including public, private and freight transport. Additionally, towns and cities account for nearly a quarter of the European Union's transport greenhouse gas emissions (GHG).

DW and the European Data Journalism Network evaluated more than a decade's worth of data from more than 100 reports and sources on public transit and the emissions it causes, and identified 148 sustainability commitments by local authorities and transport operators in 33 cities across Europe. The analysis focuses on motorized mass transit, leaving out bikes and pedestrians as well as taxis, shared vehicles and e-scooters.

The EU wants to become the first climate-neutral continent by 2050. A key part of that plan is cutting transport-related GHG emissions by 90% from 1990 levels.

As DW's analysis shows, Copenhagen is the only EU capital that has almost electrified its entire bus fleet — its vehicles do not have combustion engines and therefore do not produce tailpipe emissions. In cities like Rome, Lisbon and Nicosia, more than 90% of buses still run on diesel, and metros and trams account for less than 10% of their public transport fleet.

Although cars still dominate European streets, "electric buses are one way to reduce local air pollution," said Martin Baierl, data analyst with Transport & Environment, an advocacy non-profit for clean transport.

EU funds enabled many cities to increase their electric and alternative fuel bus fleets from 2021 on.

Electric buses comprise 20% of the total fleet in EU capitals and selected cities. Their number increased by 55% between 2021 and 2025 — equivalent to 1,234 more such vehicles on the road. The number of diesel-only buses has been declining since then, although they still outnumber those running on alternative fuel.

In addition to decarbonizing the bus fleet and helping to reduce air pollution, embracing electric models can help to lower noise and public transport operating costs. All the more so if the city produces its own electricity, explains Baierl. However, the outlay for e-buses is higher than for diesel models. In 20 out of the 31 cities with data to analyze, over 50% of the bus fleet is made up of traditional vehicles.

Brussels, for example, was an early adopter of moving away from traditional diesel-powered buses, switching to hybrids instead. Now the Belgian capital is in the process of electrifying its fleet, but at a slower pace. "It's not easy to know what the right technology will be in the end," Baierl said.

Another example is hydrogen-powered buses, which also produce zero emissions but are more costly to operate than their electric counterparts.

Transportation is the only major sector where greenhouse gas emissions remain above 1990 levels, despite the EU's ambition of a 90% reduction by 2050.

On average, public transport — including buses, trams and metros — is used for more than a quarter of trips in the 10 largest EU capitals, according to recent data from the non-profit group CDP (formerly the Carbon Disclosure Project). In Stockholm, the share is 49%, while in the smaller Slovenian capital of Ljubljana, it's 11%. DW analysis of transport sustainability pledges reveals that only four cities have explicitly committed to achieving zero emissions in buses or all public transport.

Yannis Papadimitriou lives 85 kilometers (about 53 miles) away from Athens, the EU capital with the highest air pollution mortality risk. He travels to work by commuter rail, a means of transport he'd like to see promoted more.

"For me it is a very conscious choice to use electric public transport, especially trains, which are the safest, most environmentally friendly and quickest way of transport."

Though trains, trams and metros run on electricity, there are more buses within city boundaries, and in many urban areas the majority still run on diesel.

In Northern Europe, 15 kilometers away from Copenhagen, Poul Kattler also used to commute to work. He is now retired and takes the bus more frequently with his grandchildren. Despite Copenhagen having an almost completely fossil-fuel-free public transport system (only one bus runs on biomethane), the metro expansion, which is responding to the city's growth, comes with its own set of concerns.

"The whole construction phase [of the new metro line] is extremely environmentally demanding," Kattler said. If the construction emissions are considered, "the CO2 balance would be very poor for the metro now."

But in the long run, the benefits of a new metro line in reducing CO2 emissions outweigh its construction-related emissions, Le Pira said.

Combustion or exhaust emissions are not the only way transport pollutes the air and the environment. Indirect emissions, like those coming from electricity generation or fuel production, add to transport's CO2 footprint. Brakes and wheels release non-exhaust particle matter when wagons or vehicles slow down.

Since 1992, buses and any vehicle running on fossil fuels in the EU have been subject to stricter emissions standards. Fine particle emissions from buses and heavy-duty vehicles had dropped by 92% in 2022 according to the European Environment Agency. Yet overall road transport non-exhaust emissions from tire wear have increased 58% in two decades, as cars have become heavier.

With a few exceptions, EU member states are not close to cutting greenhouse gas transport emissions by 90%, as envisioned by the EU's 2050 target.

Zero-emission public transport is one step toward decarbonizing transport in cities and meeting GHG reduction goals. The next challenge is to get people out of their cars.

"If we talk about sustainable mobility in general and not only clean public transport, the main problem is the model shift from private cars to public transport," Le Pira said.

On average, private automobiles account for more than a third of transport in the largest EU capitals, according to CDP data.

Cheaper ticket prices for groups, like students, and disincentives for car use, such as low-emission zones, are two complementary ways of attracting more passengers while reducing private transport.

In her research work, Le Pira investigates how public transport can be more efficient and appealing for city dwellers, for example, by combining transport modes where people step off their bikes and onto a train. This kind of solution could be particularly relevant for small cities "where cars are still the main transport mode."

According to Baierl, places with good public transport draw people in. "Cities where not everybody has to use a car are just more livable," he said.
