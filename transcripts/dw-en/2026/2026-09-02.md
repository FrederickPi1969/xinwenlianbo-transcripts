## Germany: How to woo voters with an immigrant background

https://p.dw.com/p/5Kb4xDo centrist parties in Germany not have the right messages for immigrant voters?Image: Emmanuele Contini/IMAGOAdvertisementGerman voters of immigrant background are distancing themselves from the established parties even more than others, according to a study by the Berlin-based German Center for Integration and Migration Research (DeZIM).

In 2023, 12% of eligible voters in Germany were of immigration background — meaning they or at least one of their parents were born without German citizenship. That amounts to more than seven million people in a population of close to 84 million. But they are drifting away from the traditional centrist parties.

Nevertheless, according to the study, the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) continues to have the greatest voter potential among immigrants and their descendants: Two out of three voters with an immigrant background said they considered the SPD a viable option.

The center-right bloc of Christian Democratic Union and Christian Social Union (CDU/CSU) is close behind in second place. Only one in five respondents categorically rejected these parties.

"Older respondents with an immigrant background lean toward the SPD," said Friederike Römer, co-author of the study, at an online press conference this week. "This bond has been relatively strong in the past, but that seems to be breaking down now among younger generations."

Perhaps unsurprisingly, the anti-immigration Alternative for Germany (AfD) has the least potential voters of immigrant background. Nearly two-thirds of those surveyed said voting for the far-right was out of the question. People with roots in Turkey, the Middle East and North Africa, in particular, reject the party, though the AfD does enjoy significant support among Germany's Russian community.

"For a long time, it was the first party to communicate in immigrants' native languages using images that actually worked," said Martin Gerlach, federal executive director of the Turkish Community in Germany (TGD), recalling the AfD campaign in Berlin during the 2021 federal election.

"It read: 'Atatürk would vote for the AfD.' Other parties simply don't bother to think about these target groups. That's crazy, because if you win over big segments of voters with a migration background, you win elections."

The TGD was active in 60 German cities ahead of the last federal election in February 2025, encouraging voters of Turkish background to cast their ballots. But Gerlach has noticed that immigrants are not inherently pro-immigration; on the contrary, there is widespread skepticism toward new immigrants or refugees, an issue that the AfD has seized on. This, he argues, is just one of many reasons why other parties are losing support among those voters.

"The political parties don't address safety at all, and that's a huge issue," he said. "Yet people in our community feel extremely threatened in many parts of Germany — they don't even dare go to the Baltic Sea coast," he added, referring to a series of xenophobic attacks in that region.

Gerlach believes that Islamophobia has a significant effect on voters from the Turkish community, but the centrist parties don't pay enough attention to it.

"Anti-discrimination policy is seen in Germany as something progressive and left-wing, yet for the Turkish-origin community, it is about being protected by the constitution in the truest sense of the word," he said. "I believe this is a narrative that could win over a great many people."

Political scientist Özgür Özvatan pointed out that by 2030, more than half of Germany's population will have an immigrant background. He has also been critical of the established parties, most recently in his 2025 book "Every Vote Counts — Underestimated by Democrats, Courted by Populists: Migrant Germans as a Political Force."

"They are not reaching out to voters with an immigrant background the way they should — neither to recruit them for their own party, nor as volunteers, nor simply as voters," he said. "Sadly, we're missing the boat with this social change."

Özvatan argues that there is enormous potential to mobilize voters who have moved to Germany from authoritarian countries — people, for example, who have direct experience of trying to defend democracy.

"We can tap into that if we enable them to obtain citizenship with voting rights as quickly as possible," he said.

The AfD, meanwhile, relies on a strategy of addressing the Turkish diaspora while establishing connections to the right-wing authoritarian President Recep Tayyip Erdogan. Furthermore, he said, the AfD has become very skilled at pitting people with immigrant backgrounds against one another.

"A great deal of effort is being put into telling older immigrants: 'You are different from the newcomers, this whole debate about migration and deportation won't affect you.' And this tactic works well with those who do not reject the AfD," said Özvatan.

## Macron visits Bayeux Tapestry with King Charles, PM Andy Burnham

https://p.dw.com/p/5KWHFTickets to the exhibition are sold out through the end of 2026, with tickets for next year available in OctoberImage: Adrian Dennis/AFPAdvertisementFrench President Emmanuel Macron began a two-day trip to the UK by visiting another famous new arrival from France, the Bayeux Tapestry, with King Charles III and Prime Minister Andy Burnham.

Macron and his wife Brigitte, and Burnham and his wife Marie-France van Heel joined King Charles and Queen Camilla to walk the length of the 70-meter tapestry in the British Museum.

Charles hailed the "unprecedented loan" as a "precious symbol of trust" between the UK and France, while Macron said the two countries were "as close as the threads" in the artwork.

The tapestry, which depicts the momentous events of 1066 culminating in William the Conqueror of Normandy claiming the English throne, is on temporary loan from its usual home in France. It's the first time it's touched British soil since its creation in the 11th century.

The embroidery is on loan for a 10-month exhibiton opening to the public next week, on September 10. The exchange was first announced during Macron's state visit last year.

Though it's thought to have been made in the British Isles, it had been on French soil ever since the 11th century. It's usually on show in Bayeux in Normandy in northern France, hence the name. The effectively priceless artefact was transited across the Channel in secret in July.

Prime Minister Burnham, who took office six weeks ago, called the tapestry's return "a landmark moment" in the history of the two countries.

He also hailed ties with Macron's French government more generally in the House of Commons on Wednesday, alluding to reduced illegal migration crossings of the English Channel — a bid to counter one of Reform UK's favorite summer talking points.

"The loaning of the tapestry is a great example of French-British cooperation — the same cooperation that has helped us reduce summer small boat crossings to a seven-year low," Burnham said.

It's Macron's first UK visit since Burnham took office and their first in-person meeting as leaders.

The Bayeux Tapestry is a 70-meter long artwork, which is thought to be incomplete, depicting the Norman conquest of England in 1066.

Norman forces led by William the Conqueror were able to defeat and dethrone King Harold Godwinson in a campaign culminating in victory at the Battle of Hastings. The tapestry famously depicts Harold being shot in the eye with an arrow, as he is said to have died — admittedly in what modern historians believe is likely a myth or artistic exaggeration.

This conquest was a crucial contributing moment in the establishment of what would become England and later the British Empire and the UK. It was the last of several successful foreign invasions of England in a millenium, and turned out to be the last one for a millenium.

The conquest also added the final core ingredient, French, to the linguistic melting pot that produced the modern English language.

The embroidered cloth, which has brief Latin titles captioning its 58 scenes, is widely accepted to have been made in England as a tribute to William after his victory. It was then transported to his family home in Normandy.

## Russia&apos;s hybrid attacks on Germany: When is it &apos;war?&apos;

https://p.dw.com/p/5KZNuUkrainian Antonov cargo planes like this one take off from Leipzig, carrying military supplies for UkraineImage: Hendrik Schmidt/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementFortunately, nothing bad happened this time around. Had the attack succeeded, however, who knows what the consequences might have been: On the evening of August 4, a drone outfitted with explosives and a detonation device was discovered near Ukrainian cargo planes in the security area of Leipzig/Halle Airport. The airport serves as a hub for military aid to Ukraine.

The German government hesitated for a long time before publicly holding Russia responsible. But now it has announced its findings.

On Tuesday, Federal Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt told reporters that the methods used in the attempted attack were consistent with those seen in other Russian hybrid operations, citing the drone's construction, various components, explosives and detonation technology.

He noted that the attack had been planned with a "high degree of technical expertise and considerable organizational effort."

In late August, Chancellor Friedrich Merz said of the incident: "The federal government will make the perpetrators of hybrid attacks pay a price. They will pay for this."

At the time, he did not mention Russia by name. And even now, Merz himself has not commented on the matter.

However, the price Russia will have to pay is relatively small. The Russian House cultural institute in Berlin is being shut down amid suspicions that it is serving as a cover for espionage. The Russian Consulate General in Bonn is also being closed. What's more, entry requirements for Russian citizens are set to be tightened, and Germany also plans to take tougher action against the Russian "shadow fleet" — the network of ships ​accused of circumventing fuel sanctions. Exactly how and when remains unclear, however.

For the environmentalist Green Party in the Bundestag, however, the government's response was lacking.

"The federal government's analysis came a bit late, and the steps it's taking aren't enough for me," said Sara Nanni, the Greens' security policy spokesperson, in a DW interview. "I don't think anyone in Moscow is afraid or giving it a second thought right now."

As an example, she said Germany should be taking tougher action against the Russian shadow fleet. This would be necessary to "stop the flow of money to the Russian war machine," she said.

Roderich Kiesewetter, security expert with Chancellor Merz' center-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU), is also dissatisfied. In the Handelsblatt newspaper, he called for the delivery of Taurus cruise missiles to Ukraine — and for the activation of Article 4 of the NATO Treaty, which requires allies to engage into consultations in the event of an external threat.

He urged the German government to abandon "the politically convenient path of appeasement."

However, wouldn't responding more assertively, as Kiesewetter has demanded, risk provoking a military confrontation with Russia, especially given that Russia has flatly denied responsibility for the Leipzig incident?

On Tuesday, Russian President Vladimir Putin called the German sanctions against his country a "serious mistake" and accused Germany of fabricating evidence. The Foreign Ministry in Moscow announced that it would take countermeasures.

Once again, the question of whether Germany is already at war with Russia is being raised — and this time with even greater urgency. But the federal government has unequivocally denied it.

"We are not at war, but we are the daily target of hybrid warfare," said Interior Minister Dobrindt on Tuesday, after he blamed Russia for the failed attack.

Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul told German public broadcaster ARD that Germany is not at war but is a Russian "war target."

A year ago, in September 2025, Chancellor Merz said — though without naming Russia: "We are not at war, but we are no longer at peace either."

This came against the backdrop of increasing attacks on data networks, critical infrastructure and hybrid threats.

Security expert Sönke Marahrens from the University of Kiel offered a more nuanced answer: "No, Germany is not at war at the moment. We are facing hybrid activities by other countries, but we are not at war."

In Western countries, he noted, declarations of war are regulated by law. And in Germany, according to the constitution, the Bundestag must declare that the country is at war, and that applies only after a physical attack.

"We are not yet at war with Russia, but Russia appears to be preparing for a war not only against Ukraine but against all of Europe," she said, calling the incident in Leipzig an "attempted terrorist act."

From a legal standpoint, too, Germany is not at war with Russia. No declaration of war has been made, no recognized armed conflict exists, and no collective defense obligation has been invoked under NATO's Article 5. Furthermore, consultations among NATO members, which must take place before a collective defense obligation can be invoked, have not been scheduled under Article 4.

But things look different when it comes to hybrid warfare, which is not as precisely defined. Numerous security experts have argued that Russia is already waging just such a hybrid war against Western nations. This refers to attacks on infrastructure, cyberattacks, disinformation and traditional espionage.

However, the line between hybrid war and an open act of war are blurry. Marahrens believes Russia is using hybrid actions — such as the one in Leipzig — to advance its strategic interests in Germany, the second-most important supporter of Ukraine after the US. Had the attack at the airport been successful, it would have relieved pressure on the Russian front, Marahrens said.

He went on to say that "if Russia succeeds in convincing the German public not to support these political decisions [to support Ukraine], that would also directly reduce the pressure on the front lines."

The state election on Sunday in Saxony-Anhalt is one current example of Russian strategic interests. Many observers believe that this election is the most important in Germany in decades.

The key question is whether the Alternative for Germany (AfD) — which is classified as far right by the Office for the Protection of the Constitution, Germany's domestic intelligence agency — will secure an absolute majority and, for the first time, govern a German state.

The AfD opposes further military support for Ukraine and would like to normalize relations with Russia. This resonates with many in eastern Germany, where the war in Ukraine is seen by many as none of Germany's business, and where there is a feeling that too much money has already been sent to Ukraine.

## Austria women ice hockey team fight for future generations

https://p.dw.com/p/5KW5KAustria won an IIHF Division 1 tournament in China last year to qualify for this year's world championship in DenmarkImage: Peng Zhigang/Xinhua/IMAGOAdvertisementWhen the Austrian women's national ice hockey team took to the ice in the Women's European Cup of Nations in Slovakia in August, they did so without 11 of their top players.

Captain Anna Meixner and the rest of her team were frustrated that the Austrian Ice Hockey Association (ÖEHV) still hadn't responded to a list of grievances outlined in a letter to the organization last March. Meixner stressed that the players only went public with their complaints as something of a last resort.

Among the nine complaints on their list of demands were the standard of accommodation, having to change in unheated dressing rooms in the winter and not being paid per diems during training camps and international tournaments. They also said they had to make do with hand-me-down equipment from the men's team.

"We really tried to respect the federation [ÖEHV] and we really tried to solve it from within, without outside noise," Meixner told DW in a Zoom interview on Wednesday.

"So we had been fighting for four or five months without going out to the newspapers. But when we realized that nothing had changed, we had to take the next step."

"The final straw, if you want to call it that, was a camp last February. It was the no-heater-in-the-locker-room situation," said the 32-year-old forward, who, after two seasons in North America's PWHL (Professional Women's Hockey League) with the Ottawa Charge and Vancouver Goldeneyes is returning to her previous team, Swedish outfit Brynas, for the 2026-27 season.

"Here were people that had been sitting on a train for 14 hours, who didn't get food when they arrived. We had to go on the ice straight away. So, I think that was when it really became clear to us that enough is enough."

Meixner also stressed that their aim was primarily to improve things for the women's national team in the longer term.

"We have this older generation right now and we're not going to play for the national team forever. We really felt it's time to give the next generation everything that we didn't have over the last 10 to 15 years. We brought it up with the team and everybody was on board right away," she said.

To ratchet up the pressure on the ÖEHV, the players also threatened to boycott the Women's Ice Hockey World Championship in Denmark in November ― a significant moment, as this is the first time Austria have qualified for the top IIHF (International Ice Hockey Federation) tournament.

In a press release following last month's tournament in Slovakia, in which Austria came second out of the four teams, the ÖEHV said it had taken the opportunity to hold "constructive conversations" with the players.

"We have gotten some answers from the federation and we are just in the process of going over everything. But now, we're happy about the direction things seem to be moving in," said Meixner, who added that the players and federation were finally speaking on an equal footing.

"It feels like they [the ÖEHV] actually want to change something. If it stays that way, I'm sure we can figure something out and make it work. Because obviously the last thing we want to do is to miss the world championship."

Indeed, in a letter to the team sent on August 31, the ÖEHV responded to each of their nine demands outlining specific steps that had either already been implemented or were in the works.

These include a pledge to implement "clear regulations regarding per diems/remuneration and the timely payment of bonuses and travel expenses," to provide "adequate accommodation and reliable, needs-based catering" for training camps, "adequate cabin and gym facilities" as well as "sufficient time for travel and recovery."

The ÖEHV also noted that it would seek a "teamwear solution" as part of a new contract with the association's equipment provider.

While this all seems promising, Meixner still finds it frustrating that in 2026, she and her teammates have had to battle so hard for what she termed "bare minimums."

At the same time though, she said that if the players and the ÖEHV do actually manage to put aside their dispute, it will be bigger than everything she has achieved on the ice ― including winning the tournament in China last year that put Austria through to a first Women's Ice Hockey Championship.

"I'm unbelievably proud of us for staying hard at it for many months. There are so many girls from other nations reaching out saying, 'You know what? It's great that you're doing that and we should do the same,'" she said.

"We've heard the men's team stepping up, cheering for us. I think this is bigger than hockey. Hopefully this will be a big step for Austrian women's sports and Austrian women in general."

Unless an unforeseen hitch arises over the next few weeks, Austria will open their first-ever World Championship campaign against Czechia in Esbjerg, Denmark on November 6.

## Women&apos;s football: Anyomi, London City and the German exodus

https://p.dw.com/p/5J9QBNicole Anyomi has moved clubs and league with the World Cup on the horizonImage: Branislav Racko/IMAGOAdvertisementThe London City Lionesses have existed for less than a decade, have played in England's top flight for just one season and can't play in the Champions League. Yet, having broken the women's transfer record last year, the Lionesses have signed some of the game's biggest stars for the coming season as their ambition becomes ever more apparent.

As well as two-time Ballon d'Or winner Alexia Putellas and her Spanish teammate Mapi Leon, England's Euro 2022 winning goalkeeper Mary Earps and French star Kadidiatou Diani, Germany winger Nicole Anyomi is among the new arrivals.

Anyomi left Eintracht Frankfurt, where she'd scored 50 goals in 101 Bundesliga games over five years, for what she considers a greater challenge in England.

"The next step was to go to the league to compete every single weekend and this is what I see here in this league," she told DW.

"I have some friends who already played in the WSL  (Women's Super League) and they told me about the league so I think this will be the perfect step for me."

Anyomi is one of several players to leave Germany's Women's Bundesliga for England this summer. Among them are Anyomi's international teammates Vivien Endemann (Wolfsburg to Liverpool), Selina Cerci (Hoffenheim to Arsenal), Carlotta Wamser (Bayer Leverkusen to Manchester City) and England's Georgia Stanway (Bayern Munich to Arsenal).

Anyomi pointed to the increasing gap in infrastructure, finances and coaching as the primary reasons for the exodus.

"In Germany you have two, three teams who are competing at the top but here in England there are five, six, seven, eight teams.

"Also, the facilities and the staff members. We have 30-plus staff members here whereas in Frankfurt we had like seven or eight. You can learn from each person and there is something new to learn every day. I think that's how you develop as a player faster."

With England's national team having won the last two Euros, a competition once dominated by Germany, and no German side having won the Champions League since 2015, there is a concern that Germany rested on its laurels for too long.

In an effort to address this issue, last December, the Frauen-Bundesliga FBL e.V. (Women's Bundesliga Association) was formed. Similar to the DFL, which operates the men's Bundesliga, the FBL is designed to improve the league's marketing and increase revenue. It is to take over operation of the league next July 1.

"They have to bring something, not new, but something different into the league so maybe the players would stay," Anyomi said.

"I think it's an investment thing, but I think now they are building something new, to do different things.  I'm going to see this season of course and watch the Bundesliga. I hope they are going to invest more, do more for the league so players also from abroad can come to Germany."

Her new club are the foremost example of the changing face of women's football. London City did not exist until 2019 when, effectively, they took over the license of another team, Milwall. Their progress has recently been accelerated by Michelle Kang.

The Korean-American businesswoman is a serial investor in women's football and added London City to an ownership portfolio that includes serial Champions League winners OL Lyonnes and US side Washington Spirit in December 2023. Kang's deep pockets and ambition mean London City are on track to compete at the top end of the WSL within the next few years.

But her ownership of OL Lyonnes prohibits London City from playing in the Champions League. UEFA, who organize the tournament, have enshrined laws that mean clubs that are in multi-ownership structures cannot both be in the same competition in the same season. Last season, in the men's game, this led to English side Crystal Palace being demoted from the Europa League to the Europa Conference league.

"Why would we want to preserve the sporting integrity of men's football, but not of women's football? It's out of the question," said UEFA's head of women's football and former Germany international, Nadine Kessler earlier this year.

Anyomi, who must recover from a hamstring injury before she can make her debut, said she trusts the club, who have so far "done everything they promised" to find a solution.

But the lack of continental football means fewer chances to make her mark alongside the other new arrivals. Given that the World Cup in Brazil is at the end of the coming season, the move could be seen by some as a risk.

"I did think about that because I know I have to play at the club level, otherwise I won't be selected," she said. "I wanted to go abroad two years ago and I didn't in the end, so I decided to go this summer. I had the feeling this is the right step."

Whether it's to London City, or to the WSL more broadly, she's not the only Bundesliga player to feel that way.

## Germany says Russia behind attempted drone attack at Leipzig airport

https://p.dw.com/p/5JjRNForeign Minister Wadephul (left) and Interior Minister Dobrindt (right) have officially blamed Russia for an attempted drone attackImage: Kay Nietfeld/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementThe German government said on Tuesday that it held Russia "responsible" for an attempted drone attack at Leipzig/Halle Airport on August 4.

"The federal government concludes that Russia is responsible for the hybrid attack in Leipzig," said Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt at a press conference in Berlin .

"The means used — such as the drone configuration, components, explosives and detonation systems — are known to us from other hybrid operations by Russia and its war against Ukraine," added Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul.

He said the attack fit the pattern of a "long series of destabilization operations" against Europe.

Authorities discovered a drone fitted with explosives and a detonation mechanism on the evening of August 4 in the security area of Leipzig/Halle Airport between Ukrainian cargo aircraft and believe a second drone may have collided with a DHL aircraft nearby.

Last week, German media reported a third drone, also loaded with explosives, had also been discovered at the airport.

German federal prosecutors described the incident as a "serious attack on Germany's transport and logistics infrastructure."

"Taken together, police investigations, patterns of the crime and intelligence findings substantiate Russian responsibility for the attempted attack at Leipzig Airport," said Interior Minister Dobrindt on Tuesday.

"We do not view Leipzig as an isolated incident, but rather see it in the context of other hybrid actions," he continued. "We assume that Germany will be the target of further hybrid attacks by Russia."

Dobrindt insisted that Germany is "not at war" but that it is "a daily target of hybrid warfare."

Therefore, he said, Germany would respond to the hybrid attack in Leipzig in a "proportionate and measured, yet resolute manner."

Foreign Minister Wadephul said Germany's response would be coordinated closely with European Union and NATO partners.

"We are tightening entry controls for Russian nationals" and Berlin would propose the listing of more Russian individuals for European Union sanctions, Wadephul said, as the Russian ambassador to Berlin was summoned.

In addition to a strengthening of internal security, he also announced additional measures including the closure of the Russian consulate in the western city of Bonn from September 18 and the rescinding of a contract enabling the operation of the "Russian House," a Russian cultural center in Berlin.

"Our message to the Russian leadership is clear," Germany's top diplomat said. "We are sticking to our security course. The attack on Leipzig shows that this is necessary. We will not allow our social cohesion to be divided by Russian attacks. We remain ready to react along with our partners when the protection of our population demands it."

Finally, he said that Germany would continue to support Ukraine in its "defensive battle against Russian aggression."

"Russia's attempts to intimidate us, to undermine our unity and our willingness to support Ukraine are futile and will fail," he added.

"The German government has taken strong measures and the message is clear: hybrid attacks will not go without a clear response," said European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen.

"We are united in our resolve to stand against Russian aggression," she continued. "We will deter anyone who seeks to undermine our freedom and security. Russia is seeking to sow distrust and fear among our societies. But it will fail. And we will stand by Ukraine for as long as it takes."

The European Union's foreign affairs chief Kaja Kallas said earlier on Tuesday that it was "clear" that Russia was involved in the drone incident, but declined to comment further until Germany had identified those it believes were responsible,

Russia's President Vladimir Putin rejected Berlin’s allegations, claiming Germany's leadership was attempting use the incident to deflect from their own failings and dissatisfaction among voters with "the need to counter an aggressive Russia."
