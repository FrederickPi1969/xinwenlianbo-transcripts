## Why Ukraine may be involved in Mali&apos;s conflict

https://p.dw.com/p/5OauFThere are growing indications that Ukraine is providing military support to Tuareg rebels and jihadists in MaliImage: Souleymane Ag Anara/AFPAdvertisementUkraine is fighting Russia — and the conflict may extend into Africa's Sahel region.

According to an investigation published by US media outlet CNN in September, around 15 Ukrainian drone specialists are reportedly supporting Tuareg rebels in northern Mali. Their common enemy: the military government in Bamako and its Russian allies.

But how reliable is the information? And what price could Kyiv pay for engaging in Africa?

Marta Oliynyk-D'omochko, coordinator of the African Studies Program at the National University of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy, sees a strategic motive. "Ukraine's main interest is to weaken Russia's influence around the world," she told DW. If Russia's military influence were rolled back, she added, the economic opportunities arising from it would also diminish.

Russian historian Irina Filatova, professor emeritus at the University of KwaZulu-Natal in South Africa, also considers Ukrainian support for the Tuareg movement plausible. However, she said she has no independent evidence to confirm such involvement.

Kyiv's alleged partners are controversial. The Tuareg separatist Azawad Liberation Front (FLA) cooperates with the Jama'at Nusrat al-Islam wal Muslimeen (JNIM), a jihadist group affiliated with al-Qaeda.

In July 2024, DW reported on possible involvement by the Ukrainian military intelligence service (HUR) in an ambush on Russian Wagner fighters near Tinzaouaten in northern Mali.

At the time, HUR spokesperson Andriy Yusov said the Tuareg rebels had received information they needed to carry out the attack. Ukraine denied direct involvement.

Paramilitaries from Russia's Wagner Group and Malian troops suffered heavy losses in the July 2024 attack.

CNN's investigation has reignited the question of whether the Russia-Ukraine war is also being fought on African soil.

The suspicions were renewed by a post on X by Kyrylo Budanov. The former head of Ukraine's military intelligence, now chief of staff to President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, wrote on September 7 that Ukrainian intelligence protects the country's interests worldwide — "from Russia to Africa … in the burning desert sands and in the snows of icy Antarctica...".

Budanov did not explicitly mention Mali and his post does not confirm any specific operation. CNN reported that the Ukrainians provided drone training, operational planning and remote support for drone missions. DW has not independently verified those claims.

DW reached out to the HUR for comment. Among other things, DW wanted to know whether personnel had been deployed to Mali, whether the political leadership had approved such a mission, and what measures were in place to ensure that the JNIM — which cooperates with the FLA — does not indirectly benefit from Ukrainian support.

DW did not receive a specific response. An HUR spokesperson said the agency might provide information at a later date. The extent of any potential Ukrainian involvement therefore remains unclear.

Following military coups in 2020 and 2021, Mali increasingly turned away from Western partners and toward Russia. The Wagner Group was subsequently replaced by the Africa Corps in 2025 as Moscow reorganized its military presence.

Russia has sought to consolidate its influence in the Sahel region and present itself as a reliable security partner.

Ulf Laessing, former head of the Sahel program of the Konrad Adenauer Foundation in Mali and now head of its Syria and Iraq program, said Russia is continuing to expand its activities in Africa.

"I have received information that the Africa Corps still uses Syria and Libya as transit points for flights from Russia, including for refueling and loading, to support its operations on the continent," Laessing told DW.

From Ukraine's perspective, it could be attractive to exert pressure on Russian forces in Africa.

Filatova sees a strategic logic to this: "The Ukrainians regard Russian troops anywhere in the world as legitimate targets." Setbacks in Mali could tie up Russian resources and undermine Moscow's reputation as a security partner, she argued.

However, Oliynyk-D'omochko stressed that the relationship with the Tuareg is not a comprehensive alliance. The FLA is not seeking to replace the government in Bamako. Instead, it is focused on northern Mali and is pursuing self-determination or extensive autonomy.

Oliynyk-D'omochko describes the relationship as a tactical arrangement: "It is more of a situational alliance against a common enemy than a comprehensive partnership."

The FLA is fighting for an independent state in the north of Mali, a region predominantly inhabited by Tuaregs. JNIM, by contrast, aims to impose strict Islamic rule. Both groups oppose the military government and its Russian allies, but their objectives differ.

Any potential Ukrainian support for the FLA would not, in itself, prove that Kyiv is directly supporting JNIM. However, jihadists could benefit indirectly if they conduct joint operations with FLA or gain access to knowledge and equipment.

After the ousting and killing of Libya's longtime dictator Muammar al-Gaddafi during the Arab Spring in October 2011, heavily armed Tuareg fighters returned to Mali and took up the struggle for an independent state.

In the process, separatist ambitions became intertwined with Islamist movements. Iyad Ag Ghaly, the current leader of JNIM, was himself involved in the Tuareg rebellion before turning to jihadism.

According to Oliynyk-D'omochko, the cooperation is primarily tactical in nature. The two sides have collaborated during past uprisings but later fell out. It remains unclear how long the current alliance will last.

For Kyiv, this also poses a political risk. In many African countries, the Tuaregs are viewed as separatists. Cooperation with them could therefore create the impression that Ukraine is interfering in another country's internal affairs.

Mali, Niger and Burkina Faso accuse Ukraine of supporting armed groups and threatening their territorial integrity. The three Sahel nations, all ruled by military governments, have severed diplomatic relations with Kyiv and deepened their cooperation with Russia.

## Macron vows &apos;strong measures&apos; to combat protests that rocked France

https://p.dw.com/p/5OgJkPresident Macron visited Marseille city hall which was vandalized during recent protestsImage: Aurelien Morissard/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementThe French government is set to announce a series of "strong measures" to assuage protests which have rocked France in recent weeks, President Emmanuel Macron said on Monday.

Speaking in the southern city of Marseille, Macron warned that fresh demonstrations planned for this coming Saturday were "risky" and said that protest routes would remain outside city centers in order to protect public order.

French Interior Minister Laurent Nunez told broadcaster BFM that he was expecting further disturbances on October 31.

"Halloween evening is traditionally always a bit lively," he said. "We expect it to be complicated again."

Prior to Macron's arrival in Marseille, the Elysee Palace said the president intended to "pay tribute to and thank the internal security forces that helped restore republican order" in France's second-largest city where protesters briefly stormed the city hall on October 17.

He acknowledged that French authorities had "not anticipated at all" the scale of demonstrations which descended into widespread violence in looting in several major cities, leading to suggestions the state had lost control of the streets.

The French president also highlighted the role of social media in the protests, saying the online networks were "damaging our democracies" by "bringing young and not-so-young people to places where criminal acts, attacks and looting were taking place."

While Macron admitted that French security forces underestimated the extent to which "extreme left forces" would meet up "so early in the day" on October 17, Interior Minister Nunez highlighted the phenomenon of organized far-right mobs confronting left-wing activists in several cities.

"We ​knew there had been calls from numerous identity-based and nationalist movements to come out to defend businesses," he told BFM.

"In a number of cities, such as Nice and Montpellier, these groups arrived in organized formations, mainly to target the ultra-left movement."

A presidential aide said Macron wanted to demonstrate that "the state is present, standing firm and fulfilling its mission to protect."

The president said that curfews enabled relative order to be maintained during last Saturday's protests (October 24), when authorities also deployed 70,000 police officers across the country, making 1,435 arrests.

"The state prevailed this weekend. The state was strong," government spokeswoman Maud Bregeon told RTL radio.

Nevertheless, President Macron also said he had instructed his government, led by Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu, to "respond to the legitimate demands that lie at the root of this social movement."

He said the government will soon announce several "concrete and huge" measures aimed at addressing student concerns over education and university admissions, following a nationwide consultation with high school and university student representatives.

"There are demands that must be heard and answered," confirmed government spokeswoman Bregeon.

"But to answer them, ​there is a prerequisite: a return to calm. We cannot have a functioning social dialogue in a climate of violence such as the one we experienced a week ago."

## Germany&apos;s Merz heads to Asia as security issues loom large

https://p.dw.com/p/5OgHBMerz's visit to Asia will be his longest foreign trip since he took office in May 2025Image: Kay Nietfeld/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementGerman Chancellor Friedrich Merz kicked off a five-day tour of Asia on Monday, traveling to Singapore before his scheduled visits to Japan and South Korea. Merz is accompanied by an entourage of 30 senior businesspeople, including representatives of Germany's industry, tech and defense companies, and energy suppliers.

While the trips are not officially related, they both seem aimed at boosting Berlin's economic and security relationships at a time of geopolitical instability, which threatens global supply chains.

In Singapore, Merz is set to deliver a major foreign policy speech and meet Prime Minister Lawrence Wong. He will then travel to Japan on Wednesday to meet with Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi and take part in the German-Japanese Business Summit.

Merz's visit "will be very much welcomed in Tokyo," said Ben Ascione, an assistant professor of politics and international relations at Tokyo's Waseda University.

"For more than a decade, Japan has been looking to generate new security allies and closer trade partnerships," he told DW.

"This greater outreach was started under Prime Minister [Shinzo] Abe, but Takaichi is very much following that blueprint because of the growing tensions in the Indo-Pacific region and the greater uncertainty around the second administration of [US President] Donald Trump," he added.

The challenges posed by an increasingly expansionist China in the region are of deep concern to Tokyo, particularly as Beijing is forging closer ties with both Russia and the unpredictable regime in North Korea. China and Russia have conducted joint military exercises off Japan, and Beijing is resolute in its insistence that Taiwan be brought under the control of the mainland.

Ascione points out that any conflict over Taiwan, the disputed islands of the South China Sea or territorial claims elsewhere in the region would have a major impact on global trade. Such disruptions would also impact Germany.

"Japan and Germany have different primary security threats at the moment, with Germany focused on the Russian invasion of Ukraine and Japan keeping a close watch on China, North Korea and Russia, but I do believe that Germany has come more into line with Takaichi's perception of the security threat that is posed by China," Ascione said.

"Takaichi is going to underline the importance of a rules-based order and the policy of a free and open Indo-Pacific at a time of such geopolitical uncertainty," he added. "Japan has in recent years moved towards greater resilience building, supply chain resilience and more and deeper security partnerships."

According to Ascione, Japan would also "welcome" Germany deploying its military assets to the region, following its Bayern frigate docking in Tokyo in 2021 and its fighter jets conducting air drills with Japanese fighters in 2024.

The frigate Bayern became the first German Navy warship to dock in Tokyo in 20 years in May 2021 and went on to conduct air and maritime exercises with Japanese units. In July 2024, German Eurofighter Typhoon aircraft similarly carried out air drills with Japanese fighters over northern Japan.

This would include ensuring supply chain resilience, protecting strategically important industries and ensuring access to technologies and components, such as the rare earths that are critical to a range of advanced technologies but remain largely controlled by China.

Merz is then scheduled to visit South Korea, where he will meet with President Lee Jae-myung and open the 19th Asia-Pacific Conference of German Business in Seoul. He is also due to visit the Demilitarized Zone that marks the border between North and South Korea.

Security issues will again dominate discussions in Seoul, believes Saeme Kim, a research fellow at The Asan Institute for Policy Studies.

However, the German leader could voice concerns about South Korea's perceived "outreach" efforts towards North Korea and, by extension, Russia.

"There has been discussion recently about exports of petroleum to Russia, although Seoul says it has not breached sanctions," she said.

South Korea is trying to set up a way to communicate with Pyongyang, according to the expert, despite the regime of Kim Jong Un sending troops to the conflict in Ukraine.

"It's not just Germany, I think a lot of European countries will be irritated by the Lee administration trying to improve relations with North Korea and Russia," Kim said.

The South also continues to provide humanitarian assistance to North Korea, even as the regime forges ever closer economic ties with China and Russia.

"The government needs to keep in mind that some of these policies are going to be damaging to South Korea's own interests over the long-run," she said.

At the same time, Merz could also offer insight into two Koreas trying to work with one another, drawing on the German experience of reuniting in 1990.

"There are lessons that South Korea can learn, of course, but there are a lot of differences," Kim said. "The two Koreas are still technically at war and Seoul has to deal with an aspiring nuclear power on its border."

Merz will have the opportunity to see the North at a distance when he visits the DMZ. His visit to the border, however, could also be canceled due to lingering tensions over the North Korean mine injuring three South Korean soldiers last month. Pyongyang has accused the South of staging the incident.

## Steinmeier in China: A major state visit in uncertain times

https://p.dw.com/p/5OgJRSteinmeier begins a five-day trip to China on MondayImage: Hendrik Schmidt/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementEight years have passed since Frank-Walter Steinmeier's last trip to China. Since then, the relationship between China and Europe has changed significantly. The German president plans to spend several days touring China before concluding his visit in Beijing, where he will meet with China's leaders.

His visit comes just as the Chinese Communist Party's Central Committee is convening for its plenary session, a major annual event that foreign dignitaries usually avoid when timing their visits.

The focus of the president's five-day trip will be "Germany's diverse and complex relations with China, as well as the challenges involved," his office said in a statement. In addition to sites of cultural exchange, the itinerary will also include a visit to a Volkswagen plant in Hefei.

The automotive industry, in particular, illustrates the dilemma facing Germany's China policy. China is one of the most important markets for German automotive and machinery manufacturers, as well as for the chemical industry. At the same time, state-supported overcapacity in China is putting pressure on European manufacturers.

Not only that. Germany and Europe are dependent on China for raw materials and technologies, which means they are increasingly seeking out alternative partnerships.

Steinmeier was once an advocate of the policy of "change through trade" — the idea that other countries could be persuaded to become more democratic with the leverage of global trade — which has few adherents left in Germany today.

Now he must now pursue a tougher European line in Beijing. Caught between partnership and competition, Europe leaders recently managed to reach an agreement: Imports of plug-in hybrid vehicles from China are to be scaled back.

"That was a concrete step, but it can only be the beginning," said Michael Laha, an expert on China at the German Council on Foreign Relations (DGAP) in Berlin.

Coinciding with Steinmeier's state visit to China, Chancellor Friedrich Merz is traveling to the Indo-Pacific with business representatives. His visit to Japan, Singapore and South Korea will focus on competitiveness, technological upheavals, defense cooperation and critical raw materials. Merz is visiting companies and attending important regional economic conferences in Tokyo and Seoul.

The dual trips are Berlin's attempt to send a twofold message: While the chancellor is "deliberately strengthening Germany's partnerships in the Indo-Pacific at a time of global upheaval," according to Laha, the president is seeking dialogue with Beijing at a time when Germany and Europe are recalibrating their relationship with China.

"This joint trip sends a very well-coordinated message to the Chinese that we remain committed to the partnership but at the same time are taking steps to reduce risk," said Laha, before adding that this is a difficult balancing act that also allows China to save face.

While Merz will be accompanied by a large economic delegation, Steinmeier will be traveling to China with representatives from German culture, politics, and academia. Mikko Huotari, director of the China Institute MERICS, is also expected to be part of the delegation — even though Beijing has imposed sanctions on the institute. "The fact that he is coming along sends a clear message to the Chinese side that we do not approve of the sanctions," said Laha.

In 2021, the EU imposed sanctions against several Chinese party and regional officials, as well as an organization accused of serious human rights violations and oppressing the Uyghur minority. It was the first time such sanctions had been imposed on China since the Tiananmen Square massacre in 1989, and Beijing did not take kindly to them:

. In response, the Chinese government imposed entry bans and other sanctions against European politicians, scholars, and institutions, including MERICS.

Laha did not expect human rights to play a major role in the German president's trip. Steinmeier is considered a pragmatist when it comes to China policy. As foreign minister, he distanced himself from his predecessor Joschka Fischer, a critic of China, and even criticized Chancellor Angela Merkel when she hosted the Dalai Lama.

On the subject of Ukraine, Steinmeier is likely to be more direct and raise the issue of China's role in the conflict. "Beijing is clearly on Moscow's side," said Laha. In Berlin, however, officials are convinced that China can do more to persuade Russia to engage in serious negotiations and find a solution to the conflict.

Steinmeier may make this clear in his speech at Peking University on Thursday. Germany has "long viewed China's emergence with respect and goodwill," read a statement from the president's office. Today, however, the focus is less on China's continued rise and more on how it uses its growing power.

"If the president points out that Russia used its power to attack a sovereign state, he could be alluding to another regional connection: Taiwan," said Laha. That could be seen as an appeal to Beijing to assume more responsibility for the regional and international order.

To conclude his visit, Steinmeier will meet with China's leadership in Beijing. Talks are planned with President Xi Jinping, whom he has known for nearly two decades, as well as with Premier Li Qiang and the chairman of the National People's Congress, Zhao Leji.

The trip marks the culmination of Steinmeier's focus on Asia during his second term in office. Since then, he has deliberately sought to forge close ties with partners in the region and has advocated for greater diversification. Now China – Germany's most important and most challenging partner – will come to its own conclusion.

## Jordan: 3 arrested over deadly church attack

https://p.dw.com/p/5OfKjJordanian security forces arrested three suspects after a dawn shootoutImage: Alaa Al-Sukhni/REUTERSAdvertisementState security forces in Jordan arrested three men on Monday morning in connection with a deadly attack on a funeral at a Greek Orthodox church on Friday, in which nine people were killed and around 40 more injured.

Officials said the arrests took place following a dawn shootout with the armed men who were discovered hiding in an abandoned house in the Karak governorate, located some 120 kilometers (75 miles) southwest of the capital, Amman.

All three suspects were wounded in the operation, but  Jordanian security forces reported no casualties as they recovered firearms and ammunition.

"The security services have fulfilled their duties, resulting in the capture of those who carried out this terrorist act," said Jordanian Interior Minister Mazen Al-Faraya in a phone interview with state broadcaster Al-Mamlaka on Monday.

"[The suspects] put up fierce resistance but were ultimately subdued and taken into custody, having sustained severe injuries."

He added that there "three individuals involved" who were "identified early on in the wake of the terrorist incident" and that the security services had "employed all the means at their disposal to apprehend them."

King Abdullah II of Jordan had ordered authorities to respond with an "iron fist" against anyone attempting to undermine the unity of the Jordanian people.

"We vowed the terrorists would not escape punishment, and no proponent of dark ideologies that divide Jordanians will be tolerated," he added in a statement on Monday, following the arrests, which relatives of the victims had said brought some relief.

"Despite the profound and immense tragedy, what happened today has eased our grief — and the grief of the Jordanian people — not just for us, the families of the martyrs," one relative, himself a survivor of the attack, told Al-Mamlaka.

There has been no claim of responsibility for Friday's deadly attack and Jordan's Public Security Directorate has not named the suspects or publicly ​identified possible affiliations with any Islamist militant groups.

Attacks on Christian minorities are very rare in Jordan, a relatively stable middle-eastern state which is a close ally of the United States and which maintains diplomatic and military relations with neighboring Israel.

## From rotten food to political opponents: Researchers explore how disgust relates to our beliefs, and why the connection is complicated

https://p.dw.com/p/5OYctFrom rotten food and sexual behavior to political beliefs, researchers want to know how different forms of disgust relate to our attitudesImage: picture allianceAdvertisementSome studies have linked disgust sensitivity to socially conservative attitudes. But ahead of the US midterm elections, does disgust shape our politics, or do our beliefs shape what we find disgusting?

The smell of rotten food can make us recoil. Certain sexual practices can provoke a similar reaction. And in today's polarized political climate, even political opponents can become objects of disgust.

Psychologists have long investigated whether sensitivity to disgust helps explain political beliefs. A review of 24 studies found that people who reported being more easily disgusted tended to hold more socially conservative views.

"We think that it evolved in part to reduce exposure to things that could make you sick," psychologist Yoel Inbar, of the University of British Columbia in Canada, told DW.

But disgust is not a single emotion. The Three-Domain Disgust Scale, developed in 2009, distinguishes three types:

One theory is that people who are especially wary of germs and disease may also prefer traditional social rules.

Inbar explains that avoiding potential sources of infection can also mean missing out on new experiences.

"Some people are more on the cautious conservative side, and some people are more on the exploring risk-taking side when it comes to pathogen exposure," he said.

Indeed, the language of contamination also appears in political rhetoric. During his 2024 presidential campaign, Donald Trump repeatedly described immigrants as "poisoning the blood of our country." Trump has also described himself as a "germaphobe."

Inbar acknowledges that the evidence linking sensitivity to disgust with conservative political beliefs remains uncertain.

"We have hypotheses. I don't know that we have as strong of evidence as I would like," Inbar said.

Disgust is not exclusively conservative. And what people find revolting can depend on their values.

A 2020 study titled "Is Disgust a "Conservative" Emotion?" found that liberals and conservatives were disgusted by different things.

Conservatives, generally aligned with the Republicans in US politics, reported greater disgust at examples involving sexual behaviors and drug use, while liberals, generally aligned with the Democrats, reported greater disgust at animal abuse and environmental pollution.

However, when asked how easily disgusted they were in general, there was no clear difference across the political spectrum.

Some early studies suggested conservatives showed stronger physical reactions to frightening or disgusting images, measured partly through changes in sweating.

But subsequent research, including a 2020 study by political psychologist Bert Bakker at the University of Amsterdam, struggled to reproduce those findings.

Studies based on questionnaires, however, have generally found that people with more conservative attitudes report being more easily disgusted.

"If bodily reactions don't differ across political groups, why do feelings?" Manos Tsakiris, of Royal Holloway and the Centre for the Politics of Feelings at the University of London, told DW.

Tsakiris and colleague Mariana von Mohr wondered whether the answer might lie in how people perceive and interpret signals from their bodies.

Scientists call the perception of internal sensations, such as hunger, breathing or a racing heart, "interoception." It's sometimes called our eighth sense, alongside the familiar five, plus our senses of balance and body position.

"A bodily signal doesn't arrive with a label attached," Tsakiris told DW. "We have to notice it and make sense of it."

The researchers surveyed 632 US adults, asking how much they noticed and trusted signals from their bodies — a measure known as "interoceptive sensibility."

They found that people who were more easily disgusted by sexual behavior tended to be more conservative, regardless of how aware they felt of their bodies.

But for moral disgust, the link to conservatism was found only among people who reported lower bodily awareness.

What particularly surprised Tsakiris was that perceived bodily awareness alone had no link to political views.

"People who are highly attuned to their bodies were neither more liberal nor more conservative," he said.

Inbar questions whether all forms of disgust are equally useful for studying political attitudes.

He and Joshua Tybur, one of the creators of the Three-Domain Disgust Scale, generally prefer measuring pathogen disgust — reactions to potential sources of disease — rather than sexual disgust.

Inbar wants the findings on bodily awareness and disgust tested again, with predictions set out in advance. For now, he considers them "suggestive at best."
