## Germany’s Gen Z protest riots spark debate on integration and bias

https://p.dw.com/p/5P7RZPrefer DW on GoogleA weapon store was one target of looters and plunderers in the Frankfurt riotsImage: Kai Pfaffenbach/REUTERSAdvertisementResponses to the violence scenes that followed recent Gen Z demonstrations in Germany have revealed differences in opinion within the federal government over who or what is responsible.

While there is general agreement that the perpetrators responsible must be punished, some conservative lawmakers have largely blamed migrant groups — raising questions about whether they are identifying problems or reinforcing prejudice.

Leading Social Democrats have warned against generalizations and one CDU minister would prefer to examine the causes of the violence. Meanwhile, Gen Z's fears for the future are at threat of being overshadowed.

In Frankfurt, more than 100 police officers were injured, one severely, in the street battles that erupted after the protest on Saturday (October 31). Shops were damaged and looted, too. The situation in Cologne, Hamburg and Berlin also escalated.

Earlier, tens of thousands of young people had taken to the streets across Germany in predominantly peaceful rallies. The initiative Gen Z regelt — which roughly means Gen Z takes charge — had urged young people to highlight their fears for the future and to call for better educational opportunities, affordable housing and more climate action.

German Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt and Roman Poseck, Interior Minister of Hesse, the state in which Frankfurt is located, quickly set the tone of the response. Most of those arrested were German citizens, said Poseck (CDU), but added: "This group of offenders was predominantly from a migrant background."

Dobrindt, who is from Bavaria's CSU, the sister party to Friedrich Merz's CDU, said: "There were peaceful demonstrations, which into the evening were hijacked by violent left-wing groups and migrant hooligans."

Chancellor Friedrich Merz (CDU) backed the statements made by his Interior Minister. His assessment of the background of the offenders was based on "the observations of the investigating authorities and the emergency personnel on the ground," his spokesperson Stefan Kornelius said. It also matched "the images that many have seen."

Merz announced a tough approach toward the rioters. "Violence does not belong on our streets, and my government will show no tolerance here," Merz said, according to the script for a speech he was due to give at a closed-door CDU party event in Berlin on Monday (November 2). "Whoever attacks police and emergency services officers and loots shops attacks our freedom, ridicules society and the legitimate interests of young people."

The center-left Social Democrats (SPD), who are in a coalition government with the conservative CDU and CSU bloc, are in agreement on the point that criminals were involved. "It is criminals who attack and injure police officers and commit serious property damage," Defense Minister Boris Pistorius (SPD) told German public broadcaster ZDF on Sunday (November 1).

This is, however, where the similarities end. "What then are ‘migrant groups'?" said Pistorius, challenging the Interior Minister's choice of words. "Does that include people in the third generation living in Germany and who are German citizens? When do we stop drawing this distinction?" he countered. "I think that leads in the wrong direction."

The German federal government's integration commissioner, SPD politician Natalie Pawlik, followed up on Monday (November 2). "Always attributing such violent riots to migrant milieus is too simplistic," Pawlik told the German newspaper, the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung. "Because blanket generalizations about groups also affect those with migrant backgrounds who demonstrated peacefully or who were themselves on duty with the police or emergency services."

She said nothing justified the outbreak of violence and that such crimes had to be punished. However, Pawlik added that if investigations showed that certain groups, in particular, were involved, then the root causes also had to be named. "We cannot ignore difficult questions about the lack of prospects, experiences of marginalization or possible integration problems," the integration commissioner said.

German Family and Education Minister Karin Prien (CDU) also wants to examine the causes of the violent behavior. She told German public radio broadcaster Deutschlandfunk that stirring resentment "would also be politically wrong." It is much more about reflecting upon reality. "If the (perpetrators) come from migrant backgrounds then that should cause us concern," Prien said.

According to Germany's Federal Statistical Office (Destatis), some 31% of the German population have a migration background. The proportion is higher in many big cities. The figure is over 50% in Frankfurt. Prien says it is necessary to examine why integration has been less successful in some groups of immigrants than others. The real challenge lies in facilitating societal participation and preventing parallel societies.

The political discussion was intensified by the Hesse interior minister suggesting a ban on further Gen Z protests. In respect of demonstrations planned for November 20, Poseck said that the right of assembly was very valuable, but social peace just as crucial. He said there had also been "massive attacks" against the police even during the demonstrations. "The organizers do not have these gatherings under control," he added.

The organizers of the demonstrations reject those accusations. They immediately distanced themselves from the violence. Germany's Family Affairs Ministry has announced that Karin Prien is due to meet with representatives from the Federal School Students Council (BSK) as well as the German Education Union (GEW) this week.

Prien herself called for younger people's interests to be taken into account more. "These are exactly the debates we need to be having, which young people are also very rightfully demanding," she told German public regional broadcaster RBB.

## Paraguay&apos;s President Santiago Pena resigns amid a bank crisis that froze millions of customers&apos; accounts and sparked protests

https://p.dw.com/p/5P7RMPrefer DW on GooglePena resigned amid a banking crisis that froze millions of customers' accounts and sparked protests (FILE: 03/03/2025)Image: Bernd von Jutrczenka/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementParaguayan President Santiago Pena resigned Thursday under mounting pressure over the collapse of digital lender Ueno, as lawmakers debated his removal from office.

The bank's accounts, also used to process pension payments, are frozen, leaving millions of customers unable to access their money. Ueno also held hundreds of millions of dollars in public funds.

Pena's resignation followed the withdrawal of support by Congress President Basilio Nunez and ruling conservative Colorado Party leader Horacio Cartes.

Thirty-five opposition lawmakers and ruling-party dissidents had submitted impeachment articles in the 80-seat Chamber of Deputies, accusing Pena of conflicts of interest, misuse of pension funds and institutional cover-ups linked to Ueno.

Pena sold his shares in Ueno Holding, whose businesses include the bank, in April 2025. Lawmakers also cited two loans he received from Ueno as part of allegations of improper financial gain.

They alleged improper presidential interference in the Social Security Institute, known as IPS, and preferential treatment for Ueno. IPS manages pension and health care funds for most Paraguayan workers and held $355 million at the bank — about 9% of its investments, according to financial manager Gladys Vera.

The motion also questioned Pena's 2023 appointment of central bank chief Carlos Carvallo, who previously chaired Financiera Ueno. According to the document, the central bank approved the company's conversion into a bank just 83 days after his appointment.

As of last month, the bank said it held roughly $800 million in public-sector deposits — about a quarter of its total deposits — including money from the operators of Paraguay's giant hydroelectric dams.

Paraguay's central bank ordered a resolution or closure process Tuesday after finding that Ueno's solvency ratio had fallen below half the required minimum.

Ueno's app, card payments and transfers stopped working, while branches closed, leaving customers queuing outside unable to withdraw their savings.

Hundreds of employees of Ueno and other companies in its parent conglomerate protested outside the Labor Ministry Thursday, demanding unpaid wages and severance.

Retirees, depositors, students and workers had marched through Asuncion a day earlier, demanding Pena's impeachment and holding signs saying "Give us back our money."

Authorities detained eight senior executives, including founder Miguel Vazquez and bank president Juan Manuel Gustale. Prosecutors also charged 27 bank officials and business associates with offenses including breach of trust and attempted fraud.

Lawyers for several detainees said they needed time to review the allegations before commenting.

Pena had sought to reassure customers in a video message Wednesday, pledging legislation "that will return to every saver what they are entitled to as quickly as possible."

The crisis also forced him to cancel his attendance at the Ibero-American summit in Madrid.

Khamenei also warned that Tehran would respond more forcefully to any future US military action.

The elections took place after months of military confrontation between Tehran and Washington, which disrupted global energy markets and raised fears of a broader regional conflict.

Mohammad Sadegh Javadi Hesar, Iran-based journalist and member of the reformist party Etemad-e Melli, said the election outcome was unlikely to affect Iran's calculations.

"Iran will not change its position simply because Republicans have lost their majority," he told DW.

"The Americans said no to war," Ghalibaf said on X, while acknowledging that rising cost of living also contributed to Donald Trump's party losing the election.

Veteran Iranian diplomat Hossein Alizadeh says the actual midterm fallout is difficult to predict, but notes US politics sends ripples out across the entire world.

"The USA is a superpower and a hegemonic power. Consequently, developments in that country are of significance to the entire world — from Mexico and Canada, through the European Union, to China, and, of course, the Middle East," he told DW.

## EU court says CBD foods need approval for sale before they can be certified organic

https://p.dw.com/p/5P6jdPrefer DW on GoogleThe EU's top court ruled that CBD foods need market authorization before they can be certified organic (FILE: June 2024)Image: Monika Skolimowska/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementThe European Union's top court ruled Thursday that foods containing cannabidiol, or CBD, cannot receive organic certification without first being authorized for sale in the bloc.

The court said an organic label could mislead shoppers into thinking CBD foods had been approved for sale.

CBD is a compound found in cannabis, commonly extracted from hemp — cannabis varieties with low levels of THC. Unlike THC, CBD does not produce a high.

The compound is widely marketed in oils, capsules and gummies as a wellness ingredient, often with claims about sleep, stress or pain relief.

The European Commission classifies CBD as a "novel food" — a food not consumed to a significant degree in the EU before May 15, 1997. Such foods require a safety assessment and authorization before they can legally be marketed.

The court noted that CBD has not yet received that authorization, although it did not say that it was unsafe.

CBD is also found in medical marijuana, but the ruling concerns its use in food rather than medicines.

The court said meeting organic production standards did not remove the need for market authorization.

"Such certification contributes to the promotion of those products and conveys, in the eyes of consumers, a positive image of quality and approval," the court said.

The ruling concerns CBD-based or CBD-enriched foods that lack EU market authorization, rather than all cannabis-derived foods.

Some cannabis-derived foods, such as hemp seeds and ordinary hemp seed oil, can legally be sold and certified organic if they meet the relevant standards.

France's organic farming regulator, INAO, told certification bodies that CBD-based products and CBD-enriched foods could not be certified organic. It also barred CBD as an ingredient in certified organic foods, while exempting hemp oil not enriched with CBD.

A hemp industry association challenged those restrictions before France's Council of State, which referred the case to the EU court.

## Who is drug testing Russia&apos;s athletes, now that they have returned to international competition?

https://p.dw.com/p/5P5sJPrefer DW on GoogleRussian athletes are competing under their national flag and colors at the World Weightlifting Championships in Ningbo, ChinaImage: Giorgio Scala/DBM Deepbluemedia/Insidefoto/IMAGOAdvertisementFresh questions have emerged over Russia's return to world sport after figures reviewed by DW showed that only four of the 16 Russian weightlifters competing at this year's world championships underwent out-of-competition testing by the International Testing Agency (ITA).

Two-time European champion Anastasiia Romanova, who previously served a two-year doping ban, was among those not tested by the ITA during the first nine months of 2026, according to its quarterly anti-doping reports.

By comparison, all 13 members of the North Korean team underwent at least one ITA test during the same period. North Korea, like Russia, has a history of doping violations in weightlifting.

The ITA, which runs weightlifting's anti-doping program, told DW that it tested all 16 Russian weightlifters "ahead of their participation in the event." DW understands those tests were conducted in Ningbo, China, where the championships are being held.

In a power sport like weightlifting, performance-enhancing drugs are most beneficial during training, when athletes build strength and muscle mass in the months before competition. Some drugs, such as anabolic steroids, may be harder to detect by the time the competition comes around.

Rob Koehler, head of the athlete-led movement Global Athlete, called the lack of out-of-competition testing "absurd."

"Out-of-competition testing is the backbone of anti-doping," Koehler told DW. "Everybody knows there's going to be testing at the event. But not everybody knows when they're going to be tested 365 days a year outside the events. It's concerning that the ITA doesn't see this as a red flag."

Marta Nawrocka, the ITA's spokesperson, said that Russian athletes were among the ten most-tested nationalities in weightlifting this year. She also defended the fact that only two Russian weightlifters feature in the sport's highest-priority testing pool, saying inclusion is based on performance and risk profiles.

"Russia's history of doping in weightlifting remains an important factor in the ITA's risk assessment and testing priorities," Nawrocka said, adding that "Russian weightlifters remain subject to substantial testing activity."

"You simply can't treat Russia like any other country," he said. "It has to have more scrutiny. It has to have more testing. If you don't do that, you're undermining athlete confidence in the system."

Weightlifting was one of the sports most implicated in Russia's state-sponsored doping scandal, which came to light after the 2014 Sochi Winter Olympics. It ranked second only to athletics for the number of positive cases identified by Canadian lawyer Richard McLaren in his 2016 report commissioned by WADA, the global anti-doping watchdog.

The scandal led to dozens of Russian athletes being banned across numerous sports. RUSADA has remained suspended for most of the past decade.

As a result, its Moscow laboratory cannot analyze urine and blood samples, meaning samples collected by the agency must be sent abroad for analysis under what WADA described as a "strict chain of custody."

WADA said it continued to monitor RUSADA but had been unable to gain access to the country to review the agency's suspension because of the "ongoing geopolitical situation."

It added that RUSADA had "collected thousands of samples … including hundreds from weightlifters" so far this year.

"Given the history, WADA remains vigilant when it comes to Russia," James Fitzgerald, WADA's spokesperson, told DW. "We must ensure that no stone is left unturned when it comes to verifying that all the proper testing and other anti-doping activities are taking place."

Despite being suspended, RUSADA still has the power to investigate and prosecute anti-doping cases involving Russian athletes, which critics find difficult to reconcile.

"It's a compounding problem that WADA is still giving them a free pass when it can't even get into Russia to see what they're doing," said Koehler, WADA's former deputy director general.

"There's simply no oversight and no accountability. And quite frankly, I don't think there ever really has been."

Critics also argue that ongoing questions over RUSADA's leadership and independence suggest that little has been fixed in the decade since the McLaren report.

Even the International Olympic Committee (IOC), whose president, Kirsty Coventry, supports the full return of Russian athletes, has acknowledged a "lack of confidence" over RUSADA's governance.

Nawrocka said the IOC had "recently entrusted" the ITA with an expanded role within Russia in the run-up to the Los Angeles Olympics in 2028, but did not explain what the role would involve or when it might begin.

In April, investigative outlet The Insider reported that Veronika Loginova, the head of the agency, was in a long-term relationship with an alleged FSB officer. The FSB, Russia's domestic security service, played a central role in covering up positive drug tests at the Sochi Games.

Loginova did not respond to questions for this article but dismissed the allegations at the time as "all this nonsense," writing in an email that she believed "sport exists separate from politics."

WADA would only confirm to DW that its investigations unit was examining the allegations against Loginova and that the inquiry was ongoing.

## What does Trump&apos;s midterm elections loss mean for strained US-India ties?

https://p.dw.com/p/5P6jhPrefer DW on GoogleWhile the US and India remain engaged in dialogue, the relationship between them has grown tenseImage: Nathan Howard/REUTERSAdvertisementWith the US Democrats scoring a resounding midterm victory and taking control of both chambers of Congress, the Republican administration led by Donald Trump is set for a new level of scrutiny until the end of Trump's term.

Trump is likely to face pushback on his controversial foreign policy, which saw the US alienate most of its allies and get entangled in yet another unpopular war in the Middle East. At the same time, the Trump administration has also taken a slew of less conspicuous but still highly controversial moves on the global stage, including a series of confrontations with India.

The ties between New Delhi and Washington are still strained by a stalled trade agreement, restrictions on immigration, and disagreement over India buying Russian oil and weapons, despite the US granting an oil ban waiver in March 2026 and Trump waving some of the Russia sanctions in October.

And while the new US Congress can choose to pressure Trump over issues such as tariffs and immigration, the president still wields significant authority on foreign policy.

The immediate test for Trump's India policy is trade. India and the US have yet to formalize the interim framework discussed by Trump and Prime Minister Narendra Modi in February, with the two nations still at odds over market access and other trade commitments.

Shanthie Mariet D'Souza, founder of the Mantraya Institute of Strategic Studies, expects the new Congress to apply "increased scrutiny of protectionist measures" pushed by the Trump administration.

The US lawmakers could push for "stronger trade oversight and clearer standards for environmental and labor issues," she told DW.

She also emphasized that "the fundamental pillars of the India-US strategic partnership are expected to remain strong" and "the long-term goal of deepening economic ties is likely to continue."

## What the Democrats&apos; midterms win means for the US economy and Trump

https://p.dw.com/p/5P3bAPrefer DW on GoogleSoaring gas prices played a key role in the outcome of the midtermsImage: Patrick T. Fallon/AFPAdvertisementFor the millions who cast their ballots in the US midterm elections, one issue ranked above all others: the economy.

So what does the Democrats' stunning capture of both the US Senate and House of Representatives mean for the economy and economic policy, given that President Donald Trump still has two years of his term to go?

Markets have rebounded after initially trending negatively. Global stocks rallied as investors appeared to take heart from the Democrats' victory.

With Democrats now holding comfortable majorities in both houses of Congress and Trump still in the White House, it could lead to gridlock in Washington. Trump has already said he would become "Mr Veto," meaning he would block any legislation the Democrats advance.

"The magnitude of the gains for the Democrats in the midterms does not change too much for the near-term policy outlook, given the party still lacks a filibuster-proof majority in the Senate and President Trump has veto power to block legislation," Stephen Brown, chief North America economist with Capital Economics, said in a note.

"I think we are looking at two years of complete 100% gridlock," Jacob Funk Kirkegaard, nonresident senior fellow with the Peterson Institute for International Economics, told DW after the results came in. "Nothing will get done of any kind."

Some investors appear worried that the Democrats could force through AI regulation, especially if they also win the White House in 2028. Rising opposition among large majorities of the US population to data centers — the key infrastructure behind AI — helped secure victories for many Democratic candidates.

Kirkegaard, who is also a senior fellow at Bruegel, a European economics think tank, said the vote showed the extent of that opposition.  Even if the Democrats can't force regulation, he thinks local opposition will make it harder for data centers to be built, which could hit the valuation of big tech firms that have benefitted from the AI boom.

"This election shows that the political underpinning of the rapid, aggressive AI growth story is shaky," he said. "It doesn't mean it's going to stop or be made illegal, but the dynamic of the growth story has shifted."

If the Democrats were to win the presidency in 2028, Brown said, they would be perfectly placed to make major policy changes. "That includes the potential for tax hikes on corporations and high-income individuals as well as regulation that could delay the AI rollout," he said.

According to polling conducted in early October by the Pew Research Center, 56% of Americans say Trump's tariff policies will have mostly negative impacts on the country, with only 20% saying mostly positive.

That sentiment clearly drove many Democrats to victory in the midterms — particularly in states where tariffs have had a particularly negative impact. That includes those with sectors reliant on exports, such as Texas, Michigan, Iowa and Illinois, where counter-tariffs have hit and those that have high trade volumes with Canada.

Yet, barring an unprecedented climbdown from Trump — who has been ideologically wedded to tariffs since he first became a public figure almost 50 years ago — it's not clear how the Democrats could force substantial change to the president's signature economic policy.

"Trump will not be moderated," Deborah Elms, head of trade policy at the Hinrich Foundation in Singapore, told DW earlier this year. "Expect him to continue his trade disruption and tariff policies for as long as he remains in the Oval Office."

Though the US Constitution gives Congress the power to collect and organize tariffs, various laws passed over the years have given presidents power to implement tariffs when they cite certain conditions, such as national security or economic emergencies.

Trump has already used such avenues for his tariffs and can continue to do so, despite some legal rebukes. However, while Congress can't necessarily force an end to Trump's existing tariffs, it can now take further legal action against them to prevent greater escalation.

Though Trump and the Democrats are clearly not going to make common cause, Kirkegaard said, the scale of the Republicans' defeat might force Trump to be less erratic in his policymaking, from escalating his trade war with Canada to his Iran policy.

Trump said in the aftermath of Tuesday's defeats that he would "work with" the Democrats and told the Financial Times in the run-up to the vote that he was unlikely to escalate the war in Iran.

Were a deal to be struck with Iran, soaring energy prices — a pivotal election issue — could finally fall, helping voters with their cost-of-living concerns.

"You've got to get a deal with Iran, and you've got to bring down gas prices," Kirkegaard said. "From a political point of view, that's the most important thing now."

George Saravelos, an analyst with Deutsche Bank FX Research, thinks the markets are beginning to become more optimistic in that area. "With oil flows through the Middle East having materially recovered, the market is pricing a large imminent risk premium of re-escalation and energy supply disruption," he said in a note.

Despite the scale of the Democrats' victory, the fact that Trump remains in the Oval Office will complicate all efforts to force a change to signature economic policies and issues.

Yet Congress can now exert considerable pressure. Another issue vital to voters was corruption and the belief that the Trump administration has engaged in corruption and personal enrichment while in office.

That raises the prospect of the new Democratic-controlled Congress launching legal investigations and potentially pursuing another impeachment of Trump, which could force the president to negotiate on some economic issues to secure his political survival.

"Can you find 10 or 12 Republican senators who, in the face of overwhelming corruption, would be willing to vote for articles of impeachment? I wouldn't rule it out," Kirkegaard said. "And that's a scenario in which Trump might suddenly be much more accommodating to, for instance, abandoning most of his tariffs."

Despite voters' clear rejection of Trump's economic policies, the Democratic Party's newly minted senators, congressmen and congresswomen face an uphill battle in trying to reverse them.
