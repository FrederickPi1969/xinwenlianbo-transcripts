## The &apos;end of an era&apos; as Goethe-Institut in Russia closes

https://p.dw.com/p/5MPrdThe Goethe-Institut will close its doors in Russia on September 13Image: Igor Ivanko/AFPAdvertisementIn recent years, Moscow has declared numerous foreign foundations and organizations "undesirable" and banned them from operating in the country. However, despite the significantly deteriorated relations between Russia and the West as a result of Russia's war of aggression against Ukraine, the Goethe-Institut, which opened in Russia in 1992, had been able to continue operating.

But that's no longer the case. Employees of the Goethe-Institut must stop working on September 13 — and staff members sent from Germany must leave the country by that date.

According to Russia's Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov, the closure of the Goethe-Institut's three locations in Moscow, St. Petersburg and Novosibirsk comes as a response to the announced closure of the Russian House in Berlin, the minister said. Germany intends to terminate its contract with the cultural and events venue as a punitive measure. The German government holds Russia responsible for the attempted drone attack at Leipzig/Halle Airport.

"The announcement by the Russian foreign minister marks the end of an era in German-Russian cultural relations," wrote Gesche Joost, president of the Goethe-Institut, in a press release. She said it was bad news for people in Russia and that the Goethe-Institut had connected the two countries through culture and language.

As early as 2023, the Russian Foreign Ministry had demanded the Goethe-Institut's staff be significantly cut. Cultural exchanges with German artists were limited extent and kept to digital formats. Nevertheless, interest in German-language courses remained high, with around 10,000 students taking part. "Our work in Russia was and remains important," Joost said, referring to learning the German language, the many encounters and mutual understanding between people.

For the past 75 years, the Goethe-Institut has represented Germany abroad, promoting the German language and sharing German culture with people around the world.

It was founded in 1951, at a time when Germany was seeking to rebuild international trust in the aftermath of World War II and the crimes of the Nazi regime. Originally established as the "Goethe Association for the Further Training of Foreign Teachers of German," the organization initially focused on inviting foreign teachers to Germany for language training.

The first Goethe-Institut opened its doors in Athens in 1952, and just a decade later, the organization had already established 54 branches around the world.

Today, the Goethe-Institut operates a global network of 154 institutions in 100 countries, employing nearly 4,500 people.

Around one million people take an official German exam at a Goethe-Institut or one of its partner organizations each year.

In a speech honoring the institute's 75th anniversary, German President Frank-Walter Steinmeier emphasized the significance of promoting German culture abroad, stating, "For countries like ours, which are not great powers, our foreign policy will continue to have to rest on four pillars: yes, military strength, so that we are taken seriously, but also smart diplomacy to devise solutions and forge alliances; wherever possible, an international legal order to provide a framework and direction in addition; and, fourthly, culture and science as soft power, as something we offer to the wider world."

One element that proved crucial to the institute's success from its founding onward is its credibility as an independent association. Although two-thirds of its funding stems from the German Foreign Office, the organization itself determines its programs — and over the decades, these programs have regularly been adapted to be in step with the prevailing political and social climate.

The institute promptly made its ambitions clear to expand its scope beyond mere language instruction by striving to provide a more comprehensive picture of Germany through a range of cultural and informational programs. "And it was also very clear that this was not about exporting so-called German culture to the world," explained Gesche Joost, president of the Goethe-Institut since 2024. "On the contrary — the aim was to reflect on how we could have a fresh start and how we want to shape society and the future together."

In its early years, the institute's cultural programs deliberately drew on Germany's rich heritage in the areas of classical music and literature. Figures such as Schiller, Bach and Beethoven had lost none of their international appeal despite the Nazi era.

Then, in the 1960s, German jazz was a hit among the institute's cultural exports — with musician and composer Klaus Doldinger and his band touring the globe.

Over the decades, Nobel Prize-winning authors Günter Grass, Heinrich Böll and Herta Müller also traveled internationally on behalf of the Goethe-Institut.

With the spirit of change ushered in by the generation of 1968, with flower power and student protests, the institute's profile changed once again, and it increasingly incorporated critical viewpoints on social issues into its programs, including efforts to critically examine Germany's Nazi past.

In this period, West Germany was redefining itself under Social Democratic Chancellor Willy Brandt. In 1970, sociologist Ralf Dahrendorf, then parliamentary state secretary at the foreign office, announced a new set of guiding principles. Cultural policy was to become the "third pillar of foreign policy."

But Dahrendorf didn't simply wish to give culture a higher profile — he advocated for a new way of thinking. "What we give is worth only as much as our willingness to receive," he said. "Openness toward others is thus a principle of our foreign cultural policy." As a result, the Goethe-Institut moved away from the idea of simply exporting culture and toward dialogue, cooperation and reciprocal learning. That approach continues to guide the organization's work today.

While Germany was divided, East and West also competed to forge political alliances abroad through their foreign cultural policies, especially during the Cold War. In the 1970s, the East German press even spread rumors that Goethe-Instituts were being used to conduct espionage.

After the Iron Curtain fell in 1989, the Goethe-Institut opened numerous new locations in Eastern Europe and the former Eastern Bloc, including in Moscow in 1992. Despite turbulent times, its doors had remained open there until now.

For its part, the Kyiv branch, in Ukraine, has also remained open throughout the war. The institute offers a flexible hybrid scheme for its German courses there: When an air raid alert sounds, in-person classes are moved to a shelter. A range of digital and hybrid options are also available if necessary.

"I have the impression that working under crisis conditions has become the norm in many regions," Gesche Joost explained. "Either because democracy is being pushed back, because colleagues are working under censorship or because they are being threatened by armed conflicts." In many Goethe-Instituts, she said, the much-discussed Zeitenwende  — or "turning point" — has long been reality.

This term entered public discourse after a famous speech by former German Chancellor Olaf Scholz after the war in Ukraine broke out in 2022 and refers to the dawn of an era marked by geopolitical turmoil in Europe.

"We've recognized that we're important partners for civil society in crisis regions. At the same time, we have to make sure that we act sensitively and diplomatically so that we don't get expelled," added Joost.

In times where freedom of cultural expression, freedom of speech as well as science and education are increasingly coming under pressure, the Goethe-Institut plans to continue providing spaces for dialogue where open debate, critical reflection and cultural exchange remain possible.

Meanwhile, Germany's position on the Middle East conflict is currently making the organization's work more challenging in many countries. Some partners in the Arab world have suspended their cooperation as a result, while Goethe-Institut events have seen boycotts elsewhere.

"Our task then is to explain the German position and the history behind it, but also to show the debates taking place on the issue within Germany," Joost said. "At the same time, we also convey the criticism coming from many countries back to Germany."

The Goethe-Institut's work has also been made more difficult in recent years due to deep cuts in federal funding, forcing it to restructure and even shut down some locations.

At the same time, integrating Germany's growing number of foreign skilled workers presents new challenges; Joost feels her institute plays a crucial role here. "We see that many foreign skilled workers see a future for themselves in Germany. But settling into life in Germany is not always easy," she said. "To support them, we have our welcome coaches who are available to answer questions about living and working in Germany and provide guidance, especially in the early stages. That is an important contribution the Goethe-Institut can make in Germany."

Judges cited the group's call to "end one-party dictatorship," saying it challenged the legitimacy of Communist Party rule in the Chinese special administrative region.

Lee and Chow denied the subversion charges under Hong Kong's controversial national security law. The defendants also insisted the alliance had not asked people to do anything illegal.

Chow, a barrister who represented herself, raised both arms and waved at supporters as she was escorted out.

## Magyar vs. Moscow: Hungary&apos;s new tough stance toward Russia

https://p.dw.com/p/5MPdAThe close and friendly ties between Hungary and Russia ended when Peter Magyar became prime minister earlier this yearImage: Attila Kisbenedek/AFPAdvertisementWhile Viktor Orban was in power, no other EU member state was as pro-Russian as Hungary. Indeed, the former prime minister's apparent eagerness to be of service to the Russian president earned him the nickname "Putin's pinscher" or "Kremlin agent."

For years, Hungary was seen as a security risk both in the EU and NATO. Russian spies were able to operate in the country largely unhindered for a long time.

In February, it came to light that former Foreign Minister Peter Szijjarto had passed on confidential information from EU meetings to his Russian counterpart, Sergey Lavrov.

All this came to an end in May, when the new government headed by Peter Magyar took office.

In a clear departure from the government line under Orban, Hungary's new prime minister has already heavily criticized Russia's war of aggression against Ukraine several times.

This week, it took its biggest step yet against Russia, expelling 10 Russian diplomats for engaging in activities "that are unacceptable for diplomats under the Vienna Convention," as announced by Hungarian Foreign Minister Anita Orban (no relation to the former prime minister).

Although Orban did not provide any more details, the phrase is generally understood to mean that the diplomats in question were spies.

The minister went on to say that the decision "protects Hungary's security and sovereignty," adding, "It does not imply breaking off diplomatic relations with Russia. Hungary intends to continue the necessary dialogue, based on mutual respect and adherence to the rules."

The minister's brief announcement immediately generated a huge number of headlines and comments — hardly surprising given that this was not only evidence of the government's 180° turn on the country's policy toward Russia but also one of the toughest diplomatic sanctions targeting Moscow since the collapse of communism in 1989/1990.

Speaking to Hungarian news website Index, secret service expert Peter Tarjani said that this was "not a diplomatic conflict, but a serious secret service matter" and that with this decision, Hungary was simultaneously returning "to the mainstream of its allies' security policy."

Political scientist and leading Hungarian Russia expert Andras Racz told the Hungarian national news agency MTI that Hungary was the only EU and NATO member not to have made such an expulsion in recent years.

Racz said that such a step "should really have been taken four-and-a-half years ago," adding that "Hungary is now settling its old debt with its allies."

Under Viktor Orban, Hungary was in fact the only EU country never to unreservedly condemn Russia's all-out war against Ukraine.

Indeed, during the last two years of his rule, Orban even blamed Ukraine and the West for Russia's aggression.

But long before that — even after he came to power back in 2010 — Viktor Orban took a markedly pro-Russian line, which he justified, among other things, with the claim that Hungary was more dependent on Russian energy supplies than almost any other country in the region.

His government's repeated assertion that Russian gas was cheaper has long been disproved. What's more, the expansion of the Paks nuclear power plant with Russian support will cost Hungary dearly for decades to come.

For Hungary, this closeness to Russia often led to absurd situations in which the country's national security was put at risk. At the same time, Orban repeatedly told his critics that it was they who were a threat to Hungary's sovereignty.

For example, Russian secret service agents for years took part in military-style training exercises in the far-right scene right up until 2016.

Starting in 2012, Russian secret service operatives for years stole huge amounts of data from the Hungarian Foreign Ministry and other Hungarian authorities without the government ever publicly condemning it.

In 2019, Russia got permission to move the headquarters of the International Investment Bank (IIB) to Budapest. The IIB was known as the "spy bank" because its leadership was made up of individuals linked to or associated with Russia's FSB intelligence service. It was only after the US imposed sanctions on the bank in 2023 that it left Hungary again.

Investigative journalist Szabolcs Panyi has warned multiple times in recent years that Hungary had become a "logistical center and deployment zone for Russian secret services," thereby putting the whole region at risk.

The decidedly pro-European and pro-Western orientation of Peter Magyar and his Tisza party had a considerable role to play in their election victory earlier this year. The EU flag was often seen and spontaneous cries of "Russians, go home" often heard at election rallies last spring.

Unlike Viktor Orban, Magyar and leading politicians in his Tisza party are convinced that Hungary's place is exclusively in Europe and that the country should only maintain pragmatic, low-level relations with Russia.

Nevertheless, many observers and experts are surprised at how radically the Magyar government has turned away from Russia.

Andras Telkes, the former deputy head of Hungary's foreign intelligence service (IH), wrote in the newspaper Nepszava that the expulsion of diplomats shows that the presence of Russian secret services in Hungary obviously runs "very deep" and that the country has a serious problem.

Telkes says that the expulsion of 10 diplomats will help rebuild trust in Hungary in the eyes of the secret services of its NATO allies.

At the same time, he emphasizes the scale of the move: "Expelling two or three people would have been a sign. Expelling 10 people already means war."

The spokeswoman for the Russian Foreign Ministry condemned the expulsion of Russian diplomats in unusually strong terms: She spoke of a "Russophobe lobby in Budapest" and announced a "hard and painful" response.

Russian lawmaker Andrey Kolesnik, a member of the State Duma's Defense Committee, said that halting gas supplies to Hungary would have to be considered.

Hungary's Foreign Minister Anita Orban knows more about such Russian threats than anyone else in the new Hungarian government: She wrote her doctoral thesis in 2007 on Russia's energy policy in Central Europe. A year later, she published a book entitled "Power, Energy, and the New Russian Imperialism."

## Philippines ferry fire: Search continues for dozens missing

https://p.dw.com/p/5MPdBThe fire pm the MV June Aster continued to smolder Friday morning Image: Philippine Coast Guard/REUTERSAdvertisementPhilippine firefighters on Friday managed to enter a smoldering ferry in a search for dozens of people still unaccounted for after the vessel caught fire on Wednesday evening, with rescue work complicated by heavy smoke and extreme heat.

Firefighters have reported that five people have died, 43 people have been rescued, and more than 80 are missing.

The ferry "MV June Aster" had originated in Manila, and was nearing its destination, the tourist hotspot of Coron in the western Philippine province of Palawan, when it caught fire just off the coast.

The Philippine Coast Guard updated the number of missing people from 86 to 84 after confirming that two people originally listed on the manifest had not been on board.

Philippine Coast Guards spokeswoman Noemie Cayabyab told a radio interview Friday that the fire broke out in "seconds."

"From the time that they heard the loud explosion, the appearance of smoke, and then fire. It spread very quickly," she said, citing survivor accounts.

Cayabyab said some of those who made it off the ship did not have time to put on life jackets.

The Coast Guard has deployed several vessels and divers to the incident, while army rescue boats, fishermen and private resort
owners have also provided assistance in the search.

The fire began in the cargo hold, and may have moved even more rapidly due to strong winds, Cayabyab added later.

Although the exact cause of the accident has yet to be officially determined, survivors reported hearing explosions inside the ship.

Some passengers jumped into the sea, where they remained afloat until they were rescued.  The vessel caught fire some 7 kilometers (4.5 miles) off the village of Turda.

The ferry was carrying electric cars, motorcycles, cargo, and heavy-duty equipment, as well as provisions for the crew and passengers.

Ferries are one of the most widely used forms of transport in the Philippines, an archipelago made up of 7,600 islands.

The country has a history of maritime accidents, some of which are caused by overcrowding, adverse sailing conditions, and lax safety standards.

## Germany&apos;s connection to the 9/11 attacks

https://p.dw.com/p/5MKIQThe so-called Hamburg cell played a key role in the Sept. 11, 2001, attacks in the US: Zakariya Essabar, Abdullah Binalshibh, Said Bahaji, Mounir El Motassadeq, Mohammed Atta and Abdelghani Mzoudi (from top left to bottom right)Image: dpa/picture allianceAdvertisement"This day has changed the world," German President Johannes Rau said. He had watched in shock as the burning twin towers of the World Trade Center collapsed in New York.

Nearly 3,000 people were killed on September 11, 2001, when Islamist terrorists hijacked four passenger planes and turned them into deadly weapons.

Germany’s head of state struggled to find the right words: "We do not know what political or social consequences what happened today will have — what we have watched unfold on our television screens in shock, barely able to comprehend or even reflect on it."

The following day, a rapidly spreading rumor was confirmed: Three of the four suspected hijackers had recently lived in Hamburg. The Islamist terrorists were thought to have been led by Egyptian-born Mohammed Atta, who had come to the northern German city in the early 1990s to study.

At some point, the young man became radicalized, particularly at Hamburg's Al-Quds Mosque, which was later closed by German security authorities. He spent the final three years of his life in an apartment shared with two other suspected attackers in the Harburg district.

The so-called Hamburg terrorist cell was believed to have included six men, among them Moroccan national Mounir al-Motassadeq. He was charged as an accomplice in the world's first trial related to the September 11, 2001, attacks. In 2003, the Hanseatic Higher Regional Court sentenced him to the maximum 15-year prison term for aiding and abetting thousands of murders and for membership in a terrorist organization.

After serving his sentence, al-Motassadeq was deported to his native Morocco. Another suspected attacker from the circle around pilot Mohammed Atta was Ramzi Binalshibh, who was born in Yemen and had been held without trial at the US detention facility at Guantanamo Bay since 2006.

The United States detained hundreds of suspects at the prison, which human rights groups and international bodies have described as contrary to international law. Among them was Murat Kurnaz, who was born in Bremen. He was released in August 2006 after nearly five years without evidence establishing his involvement in the terrorist attacks.

Meanwhile, investigative committees in the German Bundestag sought to determine why Kurnaz and other suspects had wrongly come under the scrutiny of US and German intelligence agencies. Among those called to testify was then-Foreign Minister and current German PresidentFrank-Walter Steinmeier.

At the center of the inquiry was the question of why German security authorities rejected the possible return in 2002 of the German-born detainee, who held a Turkish passport.

"No one doubts, and neither do I, that Mr. Kurnaz suffered terribly in Guantanamo," Steinmeier said. "That is why we also sought to ensure that he was treated in accordance with the rule of law. But at the same time, please understand that we were also obliged to ensure security in our own country."

The Kurnaz case illustrated the pressure on German policymakers in the aftermath of the terrorist attacks in the US. Chancellor Gerhard Schröder addressed parliament the following day.

"Yesterday, September 11, 2001, will go down in history as a dark day for all of us," the Social Democrat said.

He said he had expressed to the American president the deeply felt condolences of the entire German people.

"I also assured him of Germany's unconditional — I emphasize: unconditional — solidarity," Schröder said.

Germany, he added, would offer the US any assistance they requested, "of course, also by identifying and pursuing those responsible for and behind these despicable attacks."

Schröder's remarks set the tone and opened the door for a series of tougher laws. Just weeks after the attacks, the governing coalition of Social Democrats and Greens unveiled its first so-called security package, involving more funding, more personnel as well as greater powers for police and intelligence agencies.

Religious organizations that had previously been granted special protection could now be banned like other associations if their activities were directed against the Basic Law, Germany's constitutional order based on freedom and democracy, or if they pursued other criminal aims. Less than four months after the terrorist attacks in the US, Germany's Anti-Terrorism Act took effect.

Intelligence agencies were given access to personal data held by banks, airlines and telecommunications companies. Immigration laws were tightened.

Through data-mining searches, in which large amounts of data were cross-checked without any specific suspicion, authorities sought to identify so-called sleeper agents — people like Mohammed Atta from the Hamburg terrorist cell, who had not been identified by security agencies in time.

The Federal Constitutional Court brought an end to the legally questionable practice. Such searches were permissible only when there was a specific threat, the court said in a 2006 ruling. Seven years later, Germany's highest court also ruled that parts of the country's anti-terrorism database were unconstitutional.

The Islamist terrorist attacks of Sept. 11, 2001, also had far-reaching and lasting military consequences. The US launched attacks on the Taliban in Afghanistan as early as October 2001. The Taliban had refused to hand over Osama bin Laden, the head of the al-Qaeda terrorist network and suspected mastermind of the attacks.

Germany joined the US-led war in December 2001. The conflict lasted 20 years and ended in August 2021 with the Taliban's return to power. Around 3,600 soldiers from the international forces were killed, including 59 members of Germany's Bundeswehr. Three German police officers also died.

The words spoken by German President Johannes Rau on Sept. 11, 2001, in the aftermath of the Islamist terrorist attacks in the US, still resonate today: "This day has changed the world."

## Why are European banks moving their gold out of the US?

https://p.dw.com/p/5MMvFIn an age of geopolitical unrest, more and more countries want to have easy access to their gold reservesImage: CFOTO/picture allianceAdvertisementFort Knox, the fortified complex in the state of Kentucky where around half of the US government's gold reserves are held, is a byword for security.

To be "like Fort Knox" means to be extremely secure and well-guarded. Yet the perception of the US as a whole as a secure location to store gold is clearly on the wane.

The decision by the Dutch central bank recently to move some 86 tonnes (95 US tons) of gold from New York to London highlighted growing concerns among some governments over storing strategic assets in the US and whether they could be readily accessed in a crisis.

The De Nederlandsche Bank (DNB) cited "geopolitical unrest" and said moving its gold across different jurisdictions would improve its "crisis preparedness".

France also removed all its remaining gold exposure from the New York Federal Reserve between July 2025 and January 2026, although the Bank of France governor François Villeroy de Galhau said at the time it was not politically motivated.

Several politicians in Germany and Italy — which hold the world's second- and third-largest gold reserves respectively — have called for their countries' gold to be also taken out of the US amid concerns over the Trump administration's unpredictable policymaking and its growing antipathy towards the EU, including threats to annex part of its territory in Greenland.

Sebastien Tillett, an analyst with Oxford Economics, says that for European central banks, outright seizure of their assets is an "extremely remote risk". However, he says there are growing concerns over ease of access in an age of geopolitical uncertainty.

"The more relevant concern is that assets held in another jurisdiction could become temporarily inaccessible in an extreme sanctions, legal or geopolitical scenario," he told DW.

Krishnan Gopaul from the World Gold Council says that in general, central banks around the world have been buying more gold since the 2008 global financial crisis and are increasingly focused on where and how they store it. As a result, gold prices have soared to record highs.

A recent survey by the World Gold Council found that central banks have accumulated an average of 1,000 tonnes of gold over the past four years, up from the 500-ton average over the preceding decade.

Gopaul says that because the 2008 global financial crisis was followed by the euro sovereign debt crisis and then a variety of political crises, a pandemic, and sustained geopolitical upheaval and conflict, gold has come into sharper focus as a safe haven asset.

"As we've gone through the last two decades, there have been questions and concerns around the global financial system and the state of geopolitics in one form or another," he says.

The decisions by the Dutch and French to move their gold out of the US and back to Europe highlight the new focus on where exactly the gold is held, so that it can be easily and quickly leveraged in the event of a new crisis.

Central banks, governments and sovereign wealth funds hold physical gold in different locations. Moving it can be extremely complex, risky and costly, while the ease of selling it or trading it for currencies or other assets depends on the location.

"Central banks are paying greater attention to where reserves are held as well as what assets they hold, mainly to maximise resilience and flexibility of reserve holdings," says Tillett.

Gopaul says reserve management has become "incredibly important" and that while it might seem obvious to focus on the possible negative risks associated with a certain jurisdiction such as the US, part of the rationale behind such decisions is making sure the gold is as close to home as possible and in a trading hub which moves fast.

He highlights the fact that the Dutch central bank moved its gold to London rather than to the Netherlands because the UK is "one of the most liquid trading hubs in the gold market."

"The idea is that in the event of a crisis, in stress periods, that gold can be potentially mobilized," he says. "London is pretty much the heart of the gold market, and there is so much activity there, and it's such a liquid market, that in a time of crisis that is where you want your gold."

The Bank of England is a major global gold holder and custodian. There are estimated to be around 400,000 gold bars in its possession, worth around $270 billion (€232 billion).

While moving gold to a location such as London has an attraction of its own for European entities, it is clear that doubts over US reliability in the Trump era and over the US financial system as a whole is driving decision-making.

Last week, the manager of Norway's massive $2.3 trillion sovereign wealth fund said the fund needs to significantly cut its exposure to US treasuries.

That decision seems to be driven by concern over US government bond markets, with soaring inflation and government debt driving up borrowing costs and spooking investors.

Tillett says gold remains sensitive to decisions by the US federal reserve and that general concern over US financial markets means gold holders are looking to move their assets.

As well as those specific financial market concerns, Trump's rhetoric around Greenland and continued veiled threats towards the EU has added to the reasons for European institutions to want to move their assets around.

Yet the US, and the New York Federal Reserve in Manhattan in particular, remains a key custodian of much European gold, not least from Germany. Although the Bundesbank moved around 300 tonnes of gold back to Germany from the US between 2013 and 2017, it still holds well over 1,000 tonnes in the US.

Last year in a statement it said: "We have no doubt that the New York Fed is a trustworthy and reliable partner for the safekeeping of our gold reserves."
