## Mercedes-Benz halts all further investment at German facilities

https://p.dw.com/p/5POcHPrefer DW on GoogleMercedes-Benz has long been a symbol for the German auto industryImage: Bernd Weißbrod/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementMercedes-Benz announced on Thursday that it was halting all further investment at its German sites for the time being.

"The executive board has jointly decided to halt all further investments in German sites," Mercedes board member Michael Schiebe said in a memo on the company's intranet that was seen by the AFP news agency.

The announcement comes as the luxury German automaker faces increasing pressure from the works council and the IG Metall trade union.

Mercedes-Benz is "forced to examine, in concrete and detailed terms, which sites in Germany we can still afford to maintain," board of management member Britta Seeger said in an interview published on the company's intranet.

"We all want to avoid plant closures at all costs," she added. "Unfortunately, however, we must now pursue this option in more concrete terms."

Shiebe, the company's chief production officer, said the current cost structures meant it is not possible to invest in Germany.

German labor costs are sometimes five times higher than at plants in Eastern Europe, where administrative costs are also similarly much cheaper.

Schiebe had recently announced that two plants in Germany would have to close unless costs were reduced.

The carmaker, one of Germany's biggest and best-known brands, has been struggling with declining sales and profits in recent years.

Demand from China, the company's largest single market, which has historically accounted for around a third of Mercedes' passenger-car sales, has weakened considerably, particularly for luxury foreign brands. Mercedes' Chinese sales fell by around 19% in 2025.

The German carmaker has invested heavily in electric vehicles, but demand for EVs in Europe and in North America has been slower than many manufacturers anticipated. At the same time, Mercedes has also struggled with rising competition from cheaper EVs imported from China.

## What have the talks with Russia and the US in Abu Dhabi achieved for Ukraine?

https://p.dw.com/p/5PNbRPrefer DW on GoogleDelegations from Russia and Ukraine held US-mediated talks in Abu DhabiImage: Altaf Qadri/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementThe two-day talks between delegations from Ukraine, the US and Russia in Abu Dhabi on November 10 and 11 ended without a ceasefire agreement.

"We have agreed on the largest humanitarian release of prisoners, and tomorrow we will finalize the numbers," Jared Kushner announced after the talks. US envoys Kushner and Steve Witkoff arrived in Moscow on Thursday, where they are due to meet President Vladimir Putin.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy had earlier suggested an exchange of "1,000 for 1,000" prisoners, or "1,500 for 1,500." Russia said a large-scale prisoner exchange with Ukraine could take place within the next two to three weeks.

Following the meetings, Zelenskyy wrote on Telegram that the parties had discussed security guarantees and that "the first step should be ending this war and ensuring Ukraine's security." He also stressed that Europe should be involved "at every stage of genuine diplomacy."

"The agreement on a prisoner exchange is already a major step forward," says Artjom Bronzhukov of the Ukrainian think tank Polityka.

Pavlo Lakiychuk of the Ukrainian Center for Global Studies Strategy XXI also viewed it as a "very tangible result." He told DW that "getting our people back is already a major success. That is why it is worth continuing."

A real breakthrough, he added, would be the release of civilian detainees from territories occupied since 2014, particularly people from Crimea, who have been held in Russian prisons for years.

Davyd Arakhamia, head of the ruling Servant of the People parliamentary faction and Ukraine's representative at the talks in Abu Dhabi, gave a positive assessment of the trilateral talks with the US and Russia, expressing hope that an agreement could be reached by the end of the year.

"I think the Russians came with extremely strict instructions, and it was difficult to ease the tone of the discussion. But the way the US representatives structured the talks with a view to the postwar situation in both countries opened up a dialogue, in my opinion, and allowed us to work together more productively," Arakhamia said at a joint press conference with US and Ukrainian representatives following the talks.

Iuliia Osmolovska of the Kyiv office of the international think tank GLOBSEC cautions against reading too much into the positive assessments of the talks from Washington and Kyiv.

"Diplomats taking part in negotiations will never say that everything went badly and that they have no expectations. Even when no results are achieved, they will still emphasize that the talks were constructive," she said. "For anyone familiar with diplomatic language, these are simply standard phrases."

Osmolovska points to a joint assessment by 71 Ukrainian security and defense experts published by GLOBSEC in the summer. According to the assessment, the chances of a peace agreement or at least a ceasefire being reached this year or next stand at just 19%.

She blames this on the fact that neither side is "ready" for genuine negotiations. Russia, in particular, has yet to accept that its objectives cannot be achieved by military means. "What we are seeing now is more of a farce, an attempt by all sides to put a brave face on things and create the impression that something is moving forward," Osmolovska said.

"The visits by the Russian delegation, particularly under the leadership of Vladimir Medinsky, are nothing more than an attempt to stall and sell empty promises to the US administration in the form of grand agreements, in order to continue receiving so-called gestures of goodwill from Washington, such as the lifting of sanctions on Russian diesel," the political scientist says.

Ahead of the talks, several Western media outlets reported on new US proposals concerning disputed territories. The Economist, for example, reported on a proposal to redraw the administrative boundaries of the Donetsk and Luhansk regions. However, a member of the Ukrainian delegation stressed that there had "never been any discussion of concessions on territories occupied by Russia."

Kyrylo Budanov, a member of the negotiating delegation and head of the Ukrainian president's office, declined to say whether Russia had used the talks to demand that Ukrainian troops withdraw from the Donbas. Asked by journalists whether Moscow was still insisting that Ukraine pull out of the region, Budanov said the content of the negotiations would remain confidential until an agreement was reached.

"The issues discussed in the talks are matters for negotiation. The only thing that matters is the outcome. Once we achieve it, it will be made public," Budanov said.

The head of the Russian delegation, Vladimir Medinsky, took a different tone, declaring that "every inch of our land will be returned to Russia one way or another." He was responding to reports in European media that Ukraine was rejecting territorial concessions.

In addition to the Crimean Peninsula, annexed in 2014, Russian President Vladimir Putin claims the four Ukrainian regions of Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia and Kherson in their entirety as Russian territory, even though Russia does not have full military control over them.

"Putin is not fighting over villages and ruined cowsheds in the Donbas. He is fighting for geopolitical influence and trying to exploit the collapse of the old world order," says Artjom Bronzhukov.

The expert from the Polityka think tank recalls that Ukraine was already asked in early 2026 to withdraw its troops from the part of the Donbas it controls and turn the area into a free economic zone. Such a zone "would very quickly become a Russian-controlled area," he warns.

"Why would Putin stop there?" asks the expert from the Strategy XXI center. In his view, US negotiators are seeking to extract unilateral concessions from Ukraine "step by step" without demanding anything in return from Russia.

Iuliia Osmolovska describes the proposal as "very original," but says it would pose risks for Ukraine's leadership. Even if the Ukrainian delegation agreed to it, which Osmolovska doubts, the decision would have to be explained to the public, which, she says, is not ready to accept it.

The GLOBSEC expert points out that, following the last harsh winter, marked by shortages of electricity and heating, only 5% of Ukrainians were willing to make concessions to Russia in exchange for an end to attacks on the energy sector.

Following the meeting between Jared Kushner, US special envoy Steve Witkoff and Vladimir Putin in Moscow, Volodymyr Zelenskyy said he wants to "coordinate positions with the Americans once again." No further trilateral meetings have been announced so far.

Iuliia Osmolovska believes the parties will sooner or later have to admit that the negotiations have reached a dead end. She expects each side to return to military action.

Pavlo Lakiychuk, by contrast, believes the talks should continue, if only to secure the return of prisoners. He is also convinced that Europeans "will always be there," because the issue is not just the Russian-Ukrainian war but the security of the entire continent.

## Hungary eases abortion restrictions in women&apos;s rights overhaul

https://p.dw.com/p/5PNraPrefer DW on GoogleWomen will no longer be forced to listen to a so-called 'fetal heartbeat' before an abortionImage: Fotolia/Sven BährenAdvertisementHungary on Thursday said it would ease abortion rules and ratify an international treaty on violence against women.

Under the planned changes to the so-called "heartbeat regulation," women will no longer be forced to listen to the vital signs of the fetus before an abortion.

The changes proposed by the new government under Prime Minister Peter Magyar are a significant break with the conservative policies of his predecessor Viktor Orban.

The country also plans to ratify the Istanbul Convention, a treaty aimed at preventing violence against women and domestic violence.

Hungarian law allows for most abortions to be performed up to 12 weeks, and up to 24 weeks in cases of severe developmental abnormalities. Until now, women were required to have a medical provider show them evidence of a definitive sign of fetal life, commonly understood as the so-called "fetal heartbeat," before deciding whether to have an abortion.

The controversial 2022 policy had received widespread criticism from doctors and women’s rights groups as being cruel and humiliating. It will now be scrapped.

The policy had not led to a decline in abortions and according to Magyar, had raised "serious bioethical questions."

The second major change is that emergency contraception will be available without prescription.

Further measures include changing the criminal code aimed at better protecting women, including making the misuse of intimate images a criminal offense and strengthening the treatment of victims of domestic violence during criminal proceedings.

Hungary also plans to ratify an international treaty on the protection of women which most other EU members have long ratified. The Istanbul Convention came into force in 2014 and, among other things, requires rape to be criminalized based on the absence of freely given consent.

"We are making it clear that sexual acts can only ⁠take ​place with the voluntary consent of the other party," ​Magyar said.

The government of his predecessor Orban had opposed the treaty, arguing that it promoted what it described as "gender ideology."

"We are making a clear and accountable commitment to putting an end to the era of inaction," Magyar said in a Facebook video.

Among the EU’s other members, only Bulgaria, the Czech Republic, Lithuania and Slovakia have not yet ratified the convention.

The Istanbul Convention is a treaty by the Council of Europe, an international organization that promotes human rights and the rule of law across Europe and includes more countries than the member of the EU.

## Sri Lanka: Rights groups sound alarm over proposed anti-terror law

https://p.dw.com/p/5PO1TPrefer DW on GoogleThe government argues a robust anti-terrorism law is needed to address the increasingly complex activities of extremist groupsImage: Eranga Jayawardane/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementFor 46-year-old Ganesan (name changed) from Batticaloa in eastern Sri Lanka, any discussion of terrorism brings back memories of his two-year-old daughter, who was killed in the 2019 Easter Sunday attacks. She was one of 270 people who lost their lives in a series of coordinated bombings targeting three churches and three luxury hotels across the island nation.

The attacks were the deadliest in Sri Lanka since the end of its civil war in 2009. Over 500 people were injured.

But Ganesan remains skeptical when asked about the new counterterrorism bill pushed by the government in Sri Lanka.

"Laws against terrorism are necessary to prevent terrorist attacks, but they should not target the innocent or suppress human rights," he told DW.

has proposed the Protection of the State from Terrorism Act (PSTA) as a replacement for the existing Prevention of Terrorism Act (PTA). The PTA has many critics who claim that the contentious law has enabled arbitrary detention and the torture of detainees for decades.

The government argues that a robust anti-terrorism law is needed to address the increasingly complex local and international activities of extremist groups.

But rights groups are now also sharply criticizing the new bill. Activist argue that the changes will not bring about any improvement, warning that the draft features an overly broad definition of terrorism, provides inadequate judicial oversight, and grants extensive powers to law enforcement authorities. Activists also warn that the proposed law has only limited safeguards against official abuse.

The bill, for instance, allows security forces to stop, search and question individuals on the basis of "reasonable suspicion," enter and search premises, and seize items on suspicion of them being linked to an offense under the terrorism act.

"The definition of the term 'terrorism' in the bill as presently drafted is highly problematic. The term is unclear, and its meaning has not been clearly defined," Visakesa Chandrasekaram, a film director and human rights lawyer, told DW.

"Such lack of clarity in the law may result in violations of the fundamental rights of the people," he added.

Chandrasekaram also pointed out that the bill would confer police powers on the military, which is neither trained to investigate criminal offenses nor equipped to protect the rights of people targeted in such investigations.

Dhanushka Silva, a lawyer, said the proposed law is not much different from the existing one.

"The present government pledged to repeal the PTA in their 2024 election manifesto. But now the government seems to replace it with a new act that is a replica of the PTA," he told DW.

Silva believes the new bill, PSTA, could be misused to suppress democratic freedoms and muzzle dissent and free speech.

Under Section 9 of the PSTA, even posts or statements made on social media could be subject to scrutiny due to the broad definition of terrorism-related offenses in the bill, he explained.

The section further suggests that individuals who share content deemed to be a terrorism offense could face up to 15 years of rigorous imprisonment, according to the the lawyer.

The Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR) has also called on the government to ensure that the bill is consistent with international human rights law.

It noted that the PSTA, despite containing some additional safeguards and improvements on previous drafts, reproduces a number of shortcomings in the PTA and other, older counter-terrorism bills.

The legislation "should include robust safeguards against torture, ill-treatment, enforced disappearance and coercive interrogation," it said in a statement.

Government spokesperson Nalinda Jayatissa, however, rejected the allegations by rights activists and insisted that the new law would not compromise civil liberties and press freedom.

Passing the new counterterrorism legislation could also have economic and foreign policy ramifications for Sri Lanka.

The United Nations, which has repeatedly urged Colombo to repeal the PTA, has called for further revisions to the proposed PSTA.

The European Union has likewise urged Sri Lanka to uphold its international human rights obligations if it wishes to continue benefiting from the bloc's Generalized Scheme of Preferences Plus (GSP+), which grants preferential access to the EU market to eligible developing countries.

Failure to meet these requirements could result in the suspension of GSP+ preferences, likely to trigger significant economic penalties for the country.

During a parliamentary debate on November 12, Speaker Jagath Wickramaratne said that some provisions of the proposed law were inconsistent with the constitution.

The legislation was also challenged before the Supreme Court, which outlined several amendments and said Parliament could pass the measure once the changes were made.

## UK court convicts 2 Iranian men of spying on journalists

https://p.dw.com/p/5PNr5Prefer DW on GoogleThe trial opened at Woolwich Crown Court in London on October 5 this yearImage: Kin Cheung/AP Photo/picture allianceAdvertisementTwo Iranian men were found guilty of spying on dissident journalists in Britain on behalf of Iran's Revolutionary Guard on Thursday.

British counterterrorism police said the case reflected intensifying hostile Iranian activity in the country, an issue that recently made headlines amid the security scare at the RAF Fairford airbase that's a major operational site in Europe for the US Air Force.

The men, both in their 40s, were arrested last year, with Iran repeatedly rejecting the allegations against them.

The Woolwich Crown Court in London convicted one of the Iranian citizens not just of spying on journalists working for the Persian-language dissident TV channel Iran International, but of plotting a violent attack against one or more of them.

The second individual had also faced charges of plotting an attack but was only convicted of spying on the reporters.

One of the convicted defendants said he was acting under duress after his family was threatened, and the other said he did not know that his actions were assisting Iranian intelligence.

The men were accused of engaging in conduct likely to assist the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), Iran's multi-discipline military force that's seen as closer to the country's religious leadership and more influential than its regular armed forces.

The UK formally designated the IRGC as a threat to national security in July, meaning that supporting or assisting the organization can carry penalties of up to 14 years in prison as part of new rules on national security and state threats.

"This was a calculated operation to spy on those critical of the Iranian regime," said Frank Ferguson, chief prosecutor for the Crown Prosecution Service's special crime and counter-terrorism division.

"Thankfully, the defendants were stopped before any attack could take place," prosecutor Ferguson said.

Senior ‌National Coordinator for Counter Terrorism Policing Vicki Evans said that the IRGC or "middlemen" for Iranian intelligence were directing the plot.

Iran's government had repeatedly denied the allegations, with Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi saying he was "disturbed" by the trio's arrests last May.

There have been four cases targeting the Persian language television channel Iran International since late 2023.

A spokesman for the broadcaster's parent company, Volant Media, said in a statement that Thursday's verdicts "don't make the lives of our journalists any safer so long as the Islamic Republic of Iran continues its campaign of death threats and violence against them."

British law enforcement has been warning of an uptick of hostile Iranian activity in recent years, particularly since the US military operations against Tehran began earlier this year.

Most notably, the UK has accused Iran of being behind a suspected plot targeting the RAF Fairford base in September, after which US President Donald Trump withdrew some long-range bombers from the site.

Three men were indicted in the US in relation to that plot this week, while two others currently face charges in the UK.

But beyond this, the head of Britain's MI5 domestic intelligence agency Ken MacCullum said in October that Iran-linked operations had led to more than 50 arrests in the last year. He also said known cases of so-called state threats had more than quadrupled in a decade.

France this week also accused Iran of being behind a cyberattack targeting refrigeration units at hundreds of supermarkets in October, although Iran denied any involvement.

European governments are on heightened alert for so-called hybrid warfare tactics, from Russia as well as Iran, amid the geopolitical tensions of recent years. New technologies like low-cost drones and AI are also threatening to make such activities cheaper, harder to trace, and potentially more effective.

## Impeachment: How it works and why Trump should be worried

https://p.dw.com/p/5PNiKPrefer DW on GoogleDonald Trump and Pete Hegseth have both been impeached but neither was forced outImage: Evelyn Hockstein/REUTERSAdvertisementPete Hegseth, US President Donald Trump's secretary of war, described the failed efforts to impeach him this week as "farce" and "sabotage".

The case, built on an accusation that Hegseth waged war on Iran without the proper congressional approval, always looked a long shot. But many observers believe it could be the first in a wave of impeachment attempts, possibly headlined by a third attempt to impeach Trump.

While there are global variations in how impeachment works, in the US it is a process that allows the legislature (usually Congress, state or tribal legislature) to bring charges against an elected figure — a judge or a president, for example. These are not criminal charges but can result in the removal of the accused party from their post.

For federal impeachment, once an impeachment case is raised in the House of Representatives, it must be heard in a trial in the Senate, with both sides able to cross-examine relevant figures. However, for a conviction to be obtained, a two-thirds majority must be reached, a factor which often limits the chances of success given the partisan nature of US politics.

It is a simple process to introduce and, once started, the case is heard within 48 hours, as was the case with Hegseth.

Given only eight people, all judges, have been impeached since the first case concluded in 1799, the odds of impeaching a high-level politician are long. The two-thirds majority rule has meant that impeachment is instead often used to draw attention to perceived misdemeanors and potentially force a resignation.

"Using impeachment threats as a signaling device is a fairly old tactic, but it has certainly become more common in the Trump-[Joe] Biden era," Frank O Bowman III, a law professor at the University of Missouri and author of a forthcoming book on impeachment and Donald Trump, told DW.

That, added Keith Whittingtona Yale Law School professor and impeachment expert, was not what impeachment was intended to do.

"Congress has tended to take an extremely partisan approach to making use of the power. That has doomed any possibility of conviction and removal," he told DW.

"The possibility of partisan use of the impeachment power became evident very quickly in American history, but the founders certainly would not have intended or hoped that the impeachment power would be used as a partisan weapon or a means of partisan messaging."

The recent midterm results  will see the Democrats control both houses of Congress from next year, meaning there is a greater chance that the US President could be impeached for an unprecedented third time. He was acquitted on "charges of abuse of power and obstruction of Congress" in 2020 and then on the charge of "incitement of insurrection" in 2021.

Bowman consulted in both cases. "In my view, they should have succeeded," he said. "But they didn't and, from the perspective of the average member of the public, it's just fuss and bother, but nothing happened. So why are we even bothering? I do think that in the public mind, impeachment has been significantly devalued."

The law expert said there are multiple ways Congress could try again, including "conduct that rises to the level of treason, bribery, other high crimes or misuse," but political considerations are critical.

"Democrats should not start out their control of the House by rushing off to impeach Trump because … it would detract from what I think they need to be doing. That is demonstrating to the public that, having regained control of the legislature, they're at least going to try to do things which are of practical importance to the average person."

However, given the midterm results, Bowman thinks there is a chance that if enough Republicans — and public sentiment — turn further against Trump, the numbers could work. Should that time come, added Whittington, there is one particular avenue open to the Democrats.

"The best strategy would be close negotiations with Republican senators to discover if there are any grounds that might peel off the votes that they would need from across the aisle," Whittington said. "That might not be very satisfactory for the Democratic base, but that would put the president at greatest risk of conviction and removal."

The Hegseth case, effectively killed after the House of Representatives voted to refer impeachment articles to a committee, shows an appetite for impeachment against members of Trump's cabinet.

Indeed, impeachment resolutions have already been filed against Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and Education Secretary Linda McMahon, and Attorney General Todd Blanche could also be in the sights of Democrats in the upcoming Congress.

"The Democrats will face a tremendous amount of pressure from their own base to consider impeachments, and the fact that so many Democrats in the House supported the Hegseth impeachment in this procedural vote suggests that there will be an appetite to pursue that possibility in the next Congress," said Whittington.

With getting the votes to impeach Trump perhaps tougher due to his "cult of personality” within the Republican party, picking off his closest allies may be in play, said Bowman.

"You could eliminate cabinet officers, as a means of making a point to the administration, that: 'OK, we're not going to come after the big man himself, but if the rest of you minions insist on doing bad stuff, we will take you out.'"

## What are fossil fuels?

https://p.dw.com/p/4bCF2Prefer DW on GoogleFossil fuels such as coal can be found hundreds of meters below groundImage: Omer Urer/AA/picture alliance AdvertisementWhether in the form of crumbly rock, odorless gas or slick black liquid, ancient organic compounds have powered the flourishing of modern life. But now they threaten to make the planet unlivable for many plants, animals and humans.

The collective term refers to coal, oil and natural gas — a group of energy sources that contain carbon and hydrogen.

They started life hundreds of millions of years ago as living plants and animals. When they died, they sank to the bottom of oceans and swamps where they decomposed under layers of sediment.

Over time, this matter was buried deeper and deeper into the earth, subjecting it to increasing pressure and heat which changed its chemical composition. Plants and trees transformed into coal, whereas microscopic plankton and algae became oil and — if the heat and pressure increased further — gas.

Today fossil fuels can be found in the earth's crust all around the world although some countries, such as the US, Saudi Arabia and Russia, have bigger reserves than others.

Coal, found in underground coal beds, is extracted by mining, whereas oil and gas, found in reservoirs beneath the land and ocean, are accessed by drilling.

Since the early 19th Century, when the discovery and exploitation of fossil fuel reserves powered the Industrial Revolution, our use of them has skyrocketed, increasing eight-fold since 1950.

Today they provide 80% of our energy needs through heating, electricity generation and transport.

We transform them into energy either by burning them directly in furnaces that warm up our homes and businesses, or in power plants to heat water into steam which then generates electricity. When it comes to transport, crude oil is refined into a range of products such as diesel, gasoline and kerosene that are burned in the engines of automobiles, airplanes and ships. Natural gas is also sometimes compressed for use in cars and buses.

But fossil fuels are also essential to a huge range of products including steel, formed using a coal-derived material called coke, and plastic, which is produced by refining and processing crude oil and gas.

Fossil fuels are by far the biggest contributors to climate change. When burned to power our modern lives, they emit carbon dioxide (CO2) into Earth's atmosphere, trapping heat and causing temperatures to rise.

With 2025 officially one of the warmest years on record, the impact of these emissions is being felt around the world. Hotter temperatures are causing polar ice caps to melt and creating the conditions for extreme weather events — such as floods, drought, wildfires and storms — to become more intense and frequent.

Scientists stress the need to limit global warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 degrees Fahrenheit) to avoid unleashing the most catastrophic impacts of climate change. That was laid out in the 2015 Paris Agreement. Currently, there are 194 parties to the agreement, but progress has been too slow, and overall emissions continue to rise.

Three years after countries agreed at the UN climate conference in Dubai COP28 to transition away from coal, oil and gas. After the previous COP in Brazil failed to reach a consensus on a concrete plan, Brazil has now presented a roadmap aimed at turning that promise into national plans.

"It is long past time to stop treating the transition away from fossil fuels as taboo or intractable," United Nations Secretary-General Antonio Guterres said.

## Intelligent women get less help at work, study finds

https://p.dw.com/p/5PITBPrefer DW on GoogleImage: DWAdvertisementHigh intelligence is usually counted as an asset at work. But a new study suggests that for women, it can also lead to worse treatment from colleagues.

Across 826 people in the United States and China, women who scored higher on an intelligence test were rated by their teammates as more hostile, received less help and were undermined more often. Men with high scores did not receive such treatment, and in one group, they were treated better.

The study examined whether people's cognitive ability affects how their teammates treat them and whether that differs between women and men.

Since it was published this year, Nitya Chawla, an associate professor at UCL School of Management in London and the researcher behind the study, says women have written to her saying the findings matched their own experience at work. Some told her they had wondered whether they were to blame for hostility.

The researchers ran two studies. The first followed 432 business students at a US public university working in small teams on a consulting project for real organizations. The second surveyed 394 full-time employees working in teams at a financial company in China.

Intelligence here means cognitive ability measured with a standardized test of reasoning and problem-solving that employers use in hiring. Teammates were not told of anyone's scores. They rated one another, and a few weeks later each person reported how much help they had received and how often they had been undermined, for instance by having information withheld.

The effects were small, and the study shows a link, not proof that colleagues' perceptions caused the treatment. Participants reported help and mistreatment themselves, and the study did not look at race or ethnicity.

In the student group, the difference between women and men was no longer statistically significant when two participants with extremely low scores were included.

The researchers point to social role theory, the idea that people expect men and women to fit traditional roles: men as capable and assertive, women as warm and supportive. A woman with high cognitive ability breaks that expectation, and colleagues may assume she lacks warmth.

"It's that automatic, implicit assumption that takes place alongside the presence of intelligence that then drives these more negative interpersonal behaviors," Chawla told DW.

In both groups, women with higher scores were rated as more hostile. Among the employees in China, they were also rated as less warm. Women seen as more hostile reported less help, both with their work and on a personal level, and more undermining.

The team also checked whether the women were simply behaving differently. Higher-scoring women were rated as somewhat more domineering, but that did not explain why they received less help or more mistreatment. The findings point to colleagues' assumptions, Chawla says, rather than to anything the women did.

No, men with high intelligence scores do not face the same treatment. Among the students in the United States, a man's score made no difference to how his teammates rated or treated him.

Among the employees in China, men with higher scores were rated as less hostile, received more help and were mistreated less often. The authors suggest the difference between the two groups may reflect cultural differences in gender norms, but they did not test this.

Small effects still matter, she argues, because they are experienced day after day. Over time, this kind of treatment can lead to anxiety, self-doubt and a sense of not belonging. "These experiences and these feelings can become very, very burdensome over time," she said.

Chawla puts the responsibility on workplaces rather than on women. Managers, she says, should track how team members treat one another, and organizations should hold regular check-ins to see whether employees are getting equal support.

Individuals can ask themselves a simple question, she says: would they react the same way if that colleague were a man?
