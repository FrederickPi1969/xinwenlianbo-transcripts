## Kosovo ex-president Thaci sentenced to 25 years for war crimes

https://p.dw.com/p/5Mg00Hashim Thaci, 58, had said he expected an acquittal Image: Jerry Lampen/ReutersAdvertisementA Kosovo war crimes tribunal on Wednesday found former President Hashim Thaci guilty of war crimes committed during the 1998-1999 war for independence from Serbia.

Thaci, a former Kosovo Liberation Army (KLA) commander, was sentenced to 25 years in prison for crimes including murder, torture and illegal detention.

The tribunal said Thaci was guilty of the murder of  of 96 political opponents and perceived collaborators with Serbian security forces at the hands of the KLA during the conflict. He was also convicted for the illegal detention of 385 people and torturing more than 300 people

Prosecutors alleged KLA forces killed more than 100 political opponents and perceived collaborators with Serbian security forces during and just after the Kosovo war.

This included prisoners held at KLA facilities in Kosovo and Albania, along with politically motivated murders of Serb, Albanian and Roma civilians.

The defense argued that the senior KLA figures could not control or be held responsible for such acts, as the group lacked a strict command structure.

Lawyers had argued there was no direct evidence linking Thaci's command to any of the alleged crimes.

The accused are revered as national heroes in Kosovo, and the KLA and Thaci are seen as symbols of the independence movement. Thaci remained a central political figure after the war and was instrumental in Kosovo's 2008 declaration of independence from Serbia.

The verdict in The Hague was livestreamed in the Kosovo capital, Pristina, where thousands of Thaci and KLA supporters gathered.

This follows rallies over the weekend that drew tens of thousands calling for the men's acquittal.

"Our war was just, a war of liberation," Kosovo Prime Minister Albin Kurti said ahead of the verdict.

"We believe and hope … that the KLA leaders will return free to their country and to their families," he added.

After being indicted in 2020,Thaci resigned as president and has been detained in The Hague since then.

Throughout the proceedings, prosecutors tried to distinguish between Kosovo's struggle for independence from Serbia and the alleged crimes committed by the men on trial.

The tribunal responsible for prosecuting the case, the Kosovo Specialist Chambers (KSC), was set up in 2016 after earlier cases in Kosovo failed due to alleged political interference and witness intimidation.

Although based in The Hague, and staffed by international judges, the KSC was established under Kosovo law specifically to prosecute alleged KLA war crimes.

Nevertheless, the KSC remains deeply unpopular in Kosovo. Out of 15 people indicted so far by the KSC, three are serving sentences.

"Throughout my life, I stood with the people of Kosovo defending freedom, life and dignity. I was always guided by the Western ideals of democracy, equality and justice," Thaci told judges in February as the proceedings concluded.

## German drama &apos;Blindspot Berlin&apos; revisits 2016 Christmas market attack

https://p.dw.com/p/5MeeQThe German miniseries 'Blindspot Berlin' premiered at the Toronto Film FestivalImage: Hubert Mican Jagd/NEUESUPERAdvertisementA decade after the deadliest jihadist attack in Germany's history, a new six-part TV series looks at the institutional failures that allowed it to happen.

"Blindspot Berlin," which had its world premiere at the Toronto International Film Festival, follows the year-long police investigation that failed to stop Anis Amri — a Tunisian whose asylum applications had been rejected, and who was already known to German security services — from driving a hijacked truck into a crowded Christmas market at Berlin's Breitscheidplatz on December 19, 2016.

Twelve people died, including Lukasz Urban, the Polish driver Amri shot before seizing his vehicle. Dozens more were injured; a 13th victim died of their injuries in 2021. Amri fled the scene and made his way across Europe before being shot dead four days later by Italian police, near Milan, during a routine identity check.

"What happened behind the scenes of that attack is in many ways just as disturbing," says "Blindspot Berlin" director David Nawrath . "[It's] a picture of overwhelmed institutions, systemic failure and a staggering blindness to the threat that was developing right in front of our eyes."

Set in the year before the attack, the series follows police investigator Frank Laudin (Albrecht Schuch, of "System Crasher" and "All Quiet on the Western Front") and Isra Al-Husseini (Bayan Layla), a young officer and Arabic-language translator, as they try to infiltrate the Salafist network around Abu Walaa, the Iraqi preacher based in Hildesheim who recruited for extremist group "Islamic State" (IS). Frank recruits Murat (Tamer Trasoglu), who risks his life as an undercover informant. Inside the network, they find Amri: volatile, and getting more dangerous.

But despite repeated warnings, their bosses refuse to take the threat seriously. They dismiss Amri as a possible terrorist, in part because he is also a drug dealer, something the authorities see as incompatible with being a radical Islamist.

The series writers and production team closely based "Blindspot Berlin" on the facts of the Amri case, and the years-long parliamentary investigation into the institutional failures that made the attack possible. The main characters in the series — Frank, Isra and Murat — are fictionalized, but the events play out as they did in real life.

"We tried to keep as close as possible to what is known about the attack and what led to it," producer Simon Amberger told DW. "There is still a lot that isn't known, that is speculation. There we tried to leave things open."

The parliamentary inquiry committee found that the criminal division investigating Amri and other suspects was grossly understaffed, that judicial and security agencies failed to coordinate the exchange of information, and that officials made serious errors of judgment. Amri's asylum claims were rejected, but he was never deported.

At one point the bureaucracy tipped into farce. Amri tried to leave Germany to rejoin his family and was stopped at the border, where police confiscated his passport on the grounds that he lacked the correct papers to go.

The Breitscheidplatz attack came at the end of a run of assaults across Europe: the Bataclan and coordinated shootings in Paris in 2015, the suicide bombings at Brussels airport and Maelbeek metro station, and a similar truck attack in Nice, in 2016.

Despite the lessons learned in the past decade, threat levels remain high. On July 25, a man drove a van into the crowds of Christopher Street Day gay pride celebrations in Berlin, killing one and injuring dozens.

The Breitscheidplatz attack also continues to resonate today, as it raises questions on "how far a free democratic society is willing to go, how much of its freedom it is willing to sacrifice for security," says Amberger.

He's aware, however, that a story of Islamic extremism, and the failure of the German state to protect its citizens, lands differently now, in the wake of recent elections that have seen a surge of support for the anti-immigrant, Islamophobic far-right AfD party.

"There is a danger that this story, this series, could be misused to support a simplistic, populist political agenda," Amberger admits. "But it isn't that simple. [In the series] we show how extremism arises in marginalized groups that feel isolated, unseen and powerless. The solution isn't 'stop immigration.'"

"Blindspot Berlin" premieres in Germany on public broadcaster ARD in October; its international distribution is also being planned.

"I am aware that many perceive the power of Big Tech as overwhelming and impossible to roll back. I disagree," von der Leyen said. "Each age has its own sensitivities. And therefore, each will have its own tailored protection level."

Von der Leyen is expected to formally present the legislation, known as the "KIDS Act," alongside EU tech chief Henna Virkkunen in Strasbourg on Thursday.

## How hotter weather is making us poorer

https://p.dw.com/p/5MbJ4We are already paying a price for burning fossil fuelsImage: Robin Utrecht/picture alliance AdvertisementThe last 11 years have been the planet's hottest on record. This changing climate is manifested in extreme weather events like heat waves and wildfires. But less obvious are the higher prices and lower wages linked to a heating world.

According to a 2026 study by the MIT Sloan School of Management and UCLA School of Law, climate change has already driven up average American household expenses by $900 (€780) a year — rising to above $1,300 for one in 10 US counties.

These costs would not exist in a world without climate change. They include escalating home insurance ($600 higher on average), increasing state and federal taxes due to disaster recovery costs or health impacts from wildfire smoke, for example.

"Climate inaction isn't just an environmental failure; it acts like a tax on every American household," wrote the researchers .

A 2024 report by Germany's Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact estimated that climate damages to agriculture, infrastructure, health and productivity could cost the world economy $38 trillion a year by 2050.

But how have climate-driven weather extremes already impacted salaries and household budgets?

"If we can't figure out what climate change is already costing us with the data we have, projecting the future becomes almost hopeless," said Derek Lemoine, an economics professor from the University of Arizona who has estimated a 12% reduction in US incomes as global temperatures have risen. This is also compared to a non-climate change scenario.

Lemoine shows how temperature extremes in one US county are having cascading economic impacts in distant locations with milder weather due to interconnected trade and supply chains.

"Much work studies climate change as if it changes weather in only one year and one location at a time," Lemoine told DW.

Instead, he wants to understand how, if a heat wave wipes out corn crops across America, other industries that rely on that corn, including livestock and food producers, will be impacted as the commodity becomes scarcer and increases in price.

"As your costs have gone up," said Lemoine of producers impacted across the corn supply chain, "that's going to serve to make the income of everyone who depends on you go down."

Climate's impact on trade is also being felt in Germany, where record low water levels on the Rhine River, the busiest inland trade waterway in Europe, could shave as much as 0.2% off Germany's economic output in the third quarter of 2026, noted the Kiel Institute for the World Economy in July.

Prolonged dry weather linked to climate change is to blame as shipping capacity drops to as low as 15% on the river that connects Europe's largest port in Rotterdam in the Netherlands with much of western Germany.

One Australian study also looks at how broad climate changes, rather than local shocks from single extreme weather events, have gradually led to a decrease in productivity and economic output.

Timothy Neal, a researcher at the Institute for Climate Risk and Response at the University of New South Wales in Australia, co-authored the report showing that global heating reduced economic output in the state of NSW by an average of around 18%, or the equivalent of $21,288 Australian dollars (€12,974, $15,000) per person in 2024.

"The damage has already been quite severe," Neal told DW, adding that in a non-warming scenario, citizens would enjoy "lower food prices" and "lower poverty."

The report refers to droughts in parts of NSW in the mid-late 2010s that reduced productivity across the whole state by up to 10 billion Australian dollars due to lower agricultural yields and farm incomes, higher water costs and increased reliance on government assistance.

These financial impacts compounded through the state economy over time, influencing the cost of food, insurance and infrastructure maintenance.

In the words of the report, these effects also "compound existing economic pressures and contribute to widening disparities between regions," meaning an increase in income and wealth inequality.

"Climate change action should not be seen as a predominantly environmental but also economically sensible," said Frank Jotzo, an economist at the NSW Net Zero Commission, which works to decarbonize the state and commissioned the above study.

Referring to "defensive climate investments" such as reinforcing roads, railways and shipping infrastructure to adapt to a hotter world, Jotzo added that "if it wasn't for climate change, we could be putting that money and that effort into other things."

However, adaptation efforts such as the greening of cities to reduce heat island effects will be vital in order to head off much worse future economic impacts.

The 2026 European heat waves, for example, have already cost around €180 billion, which is the EU's entire projected economic growth for the year, according to one analysis.

Adaptation will also involve strengthening the hospital and medical system to counteract the health effects of heat waves that also reduce productivity as heat-stressed employees tire or struggle to sleep, Jotzo noted.

But Lemoine says that adaptation to climate-driven economic decline is so "costly in its own right" that it could exacerbate income loss and be ineffective if not done comprehensively.

Only when "all places" are insulated from weather extremes, can the cascading economic impacts be addressed, he says.

Nonetheless, both adaptation and a rapid reduction of planet-heating emissions are together required to avoid climate-driven income loss that will worsen if warming outstrips Paris climate agreement targets.

"Unchecked climate change would create a permanent drain on wages and productivity," said Jotzo.

## AI leaders call for regulations, Trump dismisses &apos;hoaxes&apos;

https://p.dw.com/p/5MfYUAnthropic, Google and OpenAI say they are working on a self-regulatory body to supervise the fast-growing industry Image: IMAGO/ZUMA PressAdvertisementSome of the top names in AI are working on creating a standards body they say would regulate the industry amid rising fears about its dangers and an apparent US reluctance to police its speedy development.

ChatGPT-maker OpenAI on Tuesday said it was working with top competitors Anthropic and Google on a self-regulatory body.

This comes amid rising calls for stricter control on the technology that is growing at lightning speed and not without dangerous precedents, calls that have thus far gone unanswered by the Donald Trump administration.

The regulatory body, proposed by Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis, would be modeled after the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, which oversees US brokers and investment firms.

Hassabis had called in July for a federal organization that would test the most powerful AI systems before they are released to the public.

He suggested funding would come from the industry to "attract world-class technical talent and provide the necessary compute resources for large-scale testing."

The proposal comes amid growing concerns about AI safety, which have escalated in recent weeks amid warnings from two former workers at major AI developers.

Researcher Jacob Coxon, a former OpenAI employee, resigned from Anthropic last week, sounding the alarm that AI companies were "gambling with our lives."

On Monday, former Google DeepMind researcher Bilal Chughtai echoed his concerns, warning that "AI has the potential to kill us all, and that we might be running out of time to avoid this outcome."

On Saturday, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei called for a slowdown in the industry's development, with OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, xAI's Elon Musk and Hassabis all supporting the sentiment.

Trump has dismissed the calls to better curb AI's development as "hoaxes" and a "conspiracy," while a divided Congress, narrowly controlled by Trump's Republicans, has failed to act.

The looming midterm elections make it unlikely any action will come soon despite AI and the power-hungry data centers needed to run it being a concern for many voters.

Trump's dismissal has been applauded by Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, whose chipmaking company powers the vast majority of AI systems. Huang thanked the US president for "seeing through" the warnings on AI.

The dire prediction that AI could destroy humanity "is not grounded on science," Huang said.

Amid Washington's persistent apathy toward regulation, smaller AI companies have warned any self-regulatory measures would come at their expense.

Canadian AI company Cohere's chief, Aidan Gomez, has accused the dominant American AI labs of forming a "cartel" under the guise of safety.

"AI needs guardrails. That is not the dispute and never has been. The dispute is over who writes them, who gets to participate and whose interests the rules are protecting," Gomez wrote in a blog post on Sunday.
