## Russia&apos;s giant new radar in Kaliningrad raises alarm in NATO

https://p.dw.com/p/5Op3XPrefer DW on GoogleVladimir Putin, seeking to to peer into almost every corner of Europe, as well as right across Ukraine?
Image: Vyacheslav Prokofyev/AFPAdvertisementThe Kremlin is getting closer to opening its 'new eye' on Europe. Construction of a towering radar complex is almost complete in Russia's highly militarized exclave of Kaliningrad, according to reports.

From above, the three radiating arms of the sprawling facility, each more than 500 meters (1,640 feet) long, look like some sort of alien lifeform has left its mark on an otherwise undisturbed stretch of forested nature reserve. You could actually compare it to a landing platform or launch pad for Moscow's massive intelligence-gathering network.

While Russia has not confirmed it is building the structure, security expert Vaidotas Malinionis said the radar will be so big, it will be able to see beyond the earth's curvature. The retired commander from Lithuania told DW the over-the-horizon (OTH) radar network near Gvardeysk will have a surveillance range of about 3,000 kilometers (1,864 miles), allowing Russia to peer into almost every corner of Europe, as well as right across Ukraine.

The system would reportedly allow Russia to intercept satellite communications and detect aircraft movements and ballistic missiles.

"The goal and the purpose of this radar is observation of long-distance targets and early warning capabilities. We still don't know if it is operationally capable of doing that, but it is definitely being constructed right now," Malinionis added.

The British newspaper The Times reports that the systems are primarily intended for the detection and jamming of electronic signals, citing the Finnish-Polish space and defense technology company ICEYE.

"They would be able to use it in offensive operations, to get more information about specific targets," explained Malinionis.

"And if they were able to get an early warning about the movement of NATO troops, in the case of an invasion on NATO's eastern frontier, then they would gain more time for decision-making."

Speaking to DW from the shores of the Baltic Sea, Latvian geopolitical analyst Evija Djatkovica warned there is a significant downside for NATO to this old Soviet technology being revived when it comes to the increased use of new tech in the warfare of today.

"It will help Russia close existing loopholes in detecting low-flying objects, making the rapidly developing integration of drones into regional defense doctrines less effective," she claimed.

The Times report goes on to say that ICEYE defense company sees Russia's expansion of its OTH radar system as proof that Kaliningrad is being developed into a hub for advanced military sensors and electronic warfare systems.

"The new radar signals an intensifying contest between Russia and NATO for military supremacy and control of the Kaliningrad region," Djatkovica underlined.

"This is a potentially dangerous situation, since further militarization on both sides could be expected and, at some point, a preemptive strike becomes an option to deny the other side an unacceptable advantage over the region,” she warned.

Russia has already alerted NATO it would be ready to resort to nuclear weapons if the Western alliance attempted to blockade Kaliningrad. The potential flashpoint and Russian outpost is situated on the Baltic Sea, wedged between NATO member states Poland and Lithuania– on the very doorstep of the alliance's eastern flank.

The exclave is only 200 kilometers from west to east and 100 kilometers from north to south. However, from what Malinionis asserted, Russia's smallest federal region or oblast packs a punch.

"According to some sources, they have the capability to deploy up to 100 nuclear warheads in Kaliningrad. And some sources say they have already started deploying some there – although that is yet to be confirmed," the former commander pointed out.

ICEYE claims to have discovered another large circular antenna complex some 50 kilometers west of Gvardeysk, near the city of Chernyakhovsk, according to the German daily Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung (FAZ). More than 20 signal and electronic warfare facilities are reportedly peppered throughout the Kaliningrad region.

Satellite images also reveal new shelters for fighter jets at the Kaliningrad-Chkalovsk military airfield, according to the FAZ report. Other images show launch pads at the mobile S-400 surface-to-air missile site in Baltiysk, meaning the systems could well be operational.

"These factors clearly depict the strategic importance of territories around Kaliningrad for both Russia and NATO," said Djatkovica. "For Russia, among other things, Kaliningrad's geographic position and nuclear arsenal stationed there allow it to project threats against most European capitals."

This is where the gargantuan radar comes into play, according to her. "Because of the radar's long reach, other European regions from North to West will also become more exposed to Russia's surveillance and military actions."

She sighed, saying the good news is that every escalatory step that Russia takes, with repercussions for European countries even further away from its borders, the more the whole continent takes Moscow seriously as a direct threat.

"Russia's malign signals should motivate the West to keep up with rearmament, which, in turn, will make NATO and Europe's collective deterrence more credible."

Djatkovica used the example of Poland, which has been transforming its offshore wind farm in the Baltic Sea into NATO surveillance infrastructure.

In another step towards integrating newer technologies, German arms producer Rheinmetall is building Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) satellites in conjunction with the firm ICEYE. They beam down precise pictures of troop movements and enemy positions, even at night or in bad weather.

The images will be used on NATO's eastern flank and by the Bundeswehr brigade stationed in Lithuania. The first SAR satellites are set to be launched into orbit on a SpaceX rocket in early 2027.

Malinionis agreed that adaptation is the most important lesson to be learnt for European leaders in times of Russian aggression. But he had another point to make. "There is also one disadvantage for this new Russian radar. Because it's so huge, it's a pretty easy target!"

## Ethnic Germans in Kazakhstan face legacy of the Soviet Union

https://p.dw.com/p/5OdfcPrefer DW on GoogleSoviet dictator Josef Stalin deported ethnic Germans from across the USSR to camps during World War IIImage: Volker Witting/DWAdvertisementShe wants to keep history alive. Yunna Kinereish, 22, is studying public relations in Kazakhstan's modern capital, Astana. She describes herself as a Kazakh German.

"I am German by ethnicity. But I also feel that I'm Kazakh, because I keep the traditions with my family, and I have this mentality," she told DW.

Kinereish, who belongs to the Wiedergeburt (Revival) Association of Germans in Kazakhstan, is one of 223,000 Kazakh Germans living in the Central Asian republic of Kazakhstan today.

Kinereish recently acted in a play at the association's youth theater that told the fate of her Russian German forebears.

From the 18th century onward, Germans were incentivized to leave Germany and settle in the Russian empire, including in Ukraine's Black Sea region.

But after the armies of Hitler's Nazi Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941, Soviet dictator Josef Stalin started deporting hundreds of thousands of Germans living in Russia and Ukraine to Siberia and Kazakhstan.

Innocent people who had nothing to do with Nazi Germany's invasion and atrocities were collectively held responsible by the Soviet power and denounced as spies and collaborators.

There were more than a million deportees, of whom at least 200,000 died as a result of expulsion, hunger, and forced labor.

"My family is part of this history, these terrible times. They were driven out of the Ukrainian village Kleinwärter, a settlement of ethnic Germans in Ukraine."

Yunna Kinereish says she was very moved by the personal stories she and her friends had brought to the stage at the House of Friendship, where the association has its office. "It's not easy for me, because it all touches my heart very deeply. I actually feel like crying. But when I'm on stage ..., I have to be strong."

The modern-looking House of Friendship in the capital Astana is the center for German culture in the city. Financed primarily by the German government, it offers language courses, engages in social work and fosters a culture of remembrance, focusing on the themes of deportation and repression under the Soviet regime.

Yunna Kinereish doesn't only present the history of her people on stage: She is also documenting it on film. In 2025, she made a film about German women incarcerated in ALZhIR (Akmolinsk Camp for Wives of Traitors to the Motherland), among the most infamous in the Soviet Gulag system.

During Stalin's brutal campaign of political repression and state-sponsored terror in the 1930s, a period known as the Great Terror, the Soviet Union established its biggest camp system in Kazakhstan. 18,000 women passed through the ALZhIR camp alone. These women were imprisoned there only because their husbands had been convicted of being traitors or enemies of the people. Among them were 167 ethnic German women, whose stories are told in the film.

The memorial for the ALZhIR women's camp is located just outside the gates of Astana, which only became the capital in 1997.

Astana's roads are permanently clogged with traffic. There are almost no pedestrians, and no cyclists at all. The city is growing, with new buildings shooting up all along the outskirts of the city. Beyond them is the barren steppe; the village of Akmol is about an hour's drive away.

Before entering the actual ALZhIR museum, the visitor passes through the Arch of Grief, a huge steel construction that commemorates the suffering and forced labor the women endured. The museum itself contains personal items that belonged to the female prisoners, models of their primitive quarters, original documents and photographs.

One of the photos is of Gertrud Platais, a woman with German roots who spent years incarcerated in ALZhIR. Platais was a print worker from Lower Silesia, part of modern-day Poland. She married Karl Eduard Platais and became a Soviet citizen. In 1938, during the Great Terror, her husband was arrested. Gertrud was convicted, too as a "relative of a traitor to the homeland."

She was transferred to ALZhIR, in a region where temperatures hit 40 degrees Celsius (104 degrees Fahrenheit) in summer and minus 40 degreees Celsius in winter. Here, she was forced to work as an embroiderer until 1946. After this, she was banished for life to the huge Karaganda Corrective Labor Camp, known as Karlag. Gertrud Platais wasn't rehabilitated until 1956. Hers is just one story documenting the terror of the Soviet system on Kazakh soil.

Karlag was by far the biggest camp system, and its complex covered an area about 22 times the size of New York City. Karlag was not just a prison camp, but a huge economic and agricultural complex. Almost one million people passed through it between 1931 and 1959.

Around 10,000 prisoners are believed to have died here between 1940 and 1950 alone: of starvation, cold, sickness, and the effects of forced labor.

The museum sits in the middle of the Kazakh steppe, near the city of Karaganda, about 250 kilometers south of Astana. It is located in the building that used to house the camp system administration. Visiting German historians and specialists are slightly puzzled: People were not tortured or tormented in this building, it was simply where the administration was done.

Here, interrogation and torture cells have been reconstructed, and everything is underscored by melodramatic music pouring from ubiquitous loudspeakers. It comes across as staged rather than authentic. As at the ALZhIR memorial, the Karlag museum has few visitors. Even well-educated young Kazakhs probably don't know about it."

Many people in Kazakhstan, and certain areas of politics, have almost no interest in coming to terms with the country's often brutal history," says Katherine M. Erdman. An American anthropologist and archaeologist at the Nazarbayev University in Astana, she has been researching the Gulag system for about a decade now.

"We wanted to find out where exactly the camps were, and how they can still be recognized today." Erdman and her students relied on modern technology such as drone flights, but conducting interviews and research on the ground was also a part of it.

"In many regions, the landscape has been shaped by forced labor," Erdman says. Factories were often constructed alongside the primitive living quarters. Houses were built using bricks made by forced laborers.

The researchers share their findings directly with neighbors and with local administrations. "Often we're just met with apathy, or flat-out rejection," says Erdman. At a time of economic and social advancement, many don't want to be reminded of the inglorious aspects of the past.

Erdman says there is a desire to demonstrate to the international community that the country is dealing with the dark side of its history, but that she often experiences lack of interest and even rejection. The academic has been conducting research in Kazakhstan for a decade, but there is no state funding for a further lectureship.

The Kazakh German Yunna Kinereish is one of the young people in Kazakhstan who want to ensure that the past is not forgotten. "My ancestors survived the Gulag system in Kazakhstan," she tells DW. "So it's important to me that we don't forget our roots, and that what our forefathers experienced and suffered is passed on."

This article, originally published in German, was written in the context of a study trip organized by the Federal Foundation for the Study of the Communist Dictatorship in Germany. Since 1998, the foundation, funded by the German taxpayer, has dedicated itself to studying and reappraising the former communist dictatorships in Germany, the GDR, and other countries.

## Germany&apos;s search for a nuclear waste site

https://p.dw.com/p/5Ov0IPrefer DW on GoogleFinland recently became the first country in the world to approve a nuclear waste final storage facilityImage: Lisa Louis/DWAdvertisementHardly any environmental and political challenge is as complex as the disposal of radioactive waste from nuclear power plants. In almost all of the world's nuclear-powered nations — some 31 countries currently operate or once operated nuclear power plants — the issue remains largely unresolved. This is despite the first large-scale nuclear power plant going online 72 years ago in the Soviet Union.

Finland recently became the first country in the world to approve a repository for radioactive nuclear waste. Located 400 meters (1,300 feet) underground on an island in western Finland, it is situated at the site of one of the country's two nuclear power plants. The project took around 40 years to complete.

Yet in every other country, including Germany, the problem remains unresolved. To make an everyday analogy: The garbage has already been placed outside for collection, but the garbage truck has not yet arrived. There is simply no landfill to take it to. It is not a pleasant sight, yet hardly anyone wants to live near such a site.

Germany has made some progress: The specially established Federal Company for Radioactive Waste Disposal (BGE) has now been searching for a suitable site for almost ten years, one that, like the Finnish repository, would be located deep underground.

Most experts have long agreed on one point: Nuclear waste, especially the spent fuel elements from nuclear power plants, which will remain radioactive for many thousands of years, must be stored underground.

Germany hopes to identify a site for a final repository by 2050. But that is still a long way off, which is why Environment Minister Carsten Schneider of the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) wants to examine whether the search process can be accelerated.

And, the government says, at least there is a roadmap in place. "Russia, the United States, the United Kingdom, and Japan have not yet advanced their procedures as far as we have," ministry spokesperson, Stephan Gabriel Haufe, said at a regular government press conference this week. "Finland is certainly the country making the fastest progress worldwide. But Germany is among the leading countries."

This week, the BGE announced that only 5% of Germany's land area, spread across 11 of the country's 16 states, remains under consideration as a potential site for a final repository. The agency is consulting geologists, nuclear experts, and physicists, as well as citizens and politicians. What is already clear, is that the nuclear waste generated during decades of nuclear power production will remain in Germany.

Most of the waste is currently stored at or near the sites where it was generated. These include the 19 former nuclear power plants currently being decommissioned, as well as several research facilities.

In 2011, following the reactor disaster in Fukushima, Japan, Germany decided to phase out the commercial use of nuclear energy, and the last nuclear power plant was shut down in 2023. The waste, however, remains in interim storage.

"It must be shown that a repository will remain safe over a very long period of time," said BGE spokesperson Dagmar Dehmer. "In Finland, that means 100,000 years; in Germany, Switzerland, and France, one million years. But no matter how far into the future scientists project, the effort involved is enormous. It requires a detailed analysis of developments over time, the interactions between geology and technology, and the decay of radionuclides, as well as an assessment of how these factors affect safety."

The procedure is indeed complex: First, potential sites are evaluated for their geological suitability. Will the rock formation remain stable at a depth of 400 meters? Could groundwater become contaminated? The goal is to identify a location where the repository would be surrounded by a rock layer around 100 meters thick, providing a natural barrier. Suitable geological formations include layers of clay, granite, and rock salt.

As the requirements become progressively stricter, fewer and fewer potential sites remain under consideration. Other factors, such as proximity to major cities, come into play only much later in the process. Few communities are likely to welcome a repository for radioactive waste, whether in an urban area or in the countryside.

Building and maintaining trust is also part of Dehmer's job. She often has to explain why it is important to deal with the legacy of the nuclear age, regardless of where people stand on nuclear energy.

Finland has succeeded in doing so, Dehmer believes. "There is a high level of trust in government action in Finland," she said. "The country's supervisory and licensing authority, STUK, is one of its most respected institutions. Germany can learn from Finland by remaining consistent, avoiding frequent changes in direction, and demonstrating the patience needed to achieve a successful outcome."

The disposal of nuclear waste is extremely expensive. Even today, the search for a final repository and the storage of nuclear waste at former reactor sites cost around €1.4 billion ($1.6 billion) per year.

Experts estimate that the final repository itself will cost roughly €14 billion ($16.4 billion) once completed. If all goes according to plan, 28,000 cubic meters of radioactive waste will eventually be stored deep underground on a permanent basis.
