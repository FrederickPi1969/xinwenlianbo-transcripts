## Ethiopia: Who could bring the warring sides to the negotiation table?

https://p.dw.com/p/5NTDNThis picture of Ethiopian soldiers patroling parts of Tigray was taken during the 2020-2022 war in Ethiopia, but is increasingly likely to become a reality on the ground in various parts of the country once moreImage: Ben Curtis/AP/dpa/picture allianceAdvertisementJust four years after a landmark peace deal officially halted the devastating Tigray war, fighting has escalated once again in northern Ethiopia in recent weeks.

Just days before full-scale fighting resumed, several armed groups which had previously considered each enemies formally announced an alliance with a common goal: To bring down Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's federal government.

But this time, the lay of the land looks quite different from the previous conflict: Parties outside the country have been accused of meddling in the situation, with each having their own motives to want to destabilize Abiy's government.

The 2022 Pretoria Peace Agreement ended the conflict between the Ethiopian federal government and the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF), which cost hundreds of thousands of lives in the course of two years.

Negash Mohammed, a journalist with DW's Amharic department. believes that the deal collapsed for three key reasons — or rather because three key actors in the conflict were largely neglected at the negotiation table.

Mohammed explained that the ultimate agreement didn't factor in the role of Eritrea in the conflict, which had fought alongside Ethiopia against the TPLF; it ignored the part that Amhara militias — known as Fano — played in the war, who later felt sorely excluded from the negotiations; finally, it ignored harliners within the TPLF who viewed the peace peace as nothing but military defeat.

Tensions between the federal government and the Fano escalated further when Addis Ababa moved to forcibly disarm the militias instead of addressing their grievances.

"The federal government has its own shortcomings," said Mohammed. "Instead of convincing the other side, it went on to aggravate the grievances they [already] had."

Mohammed also emphasized the fact that this coalition against the federal government includes several groups which have otherwise little to do with each other.

It includes the Oromo Liberation Army, a faction of the Ogaden National Liberation Front as well as multiple smaller armed groups from the western Benishangul-Gumuz region — in addition to ethnic militant factions from Afar, which borders Tigray.

Eritrea meanwhile appears to have shifted sides in the current situation: While not officially a member of the alliance, the neighboring country is believed to be providing support to the rebels.

Patricia Rodrigues, a senior analyst at Control Risks, a global intelligence and security consultancy, referred to the level of strategic coordination among these various groups in the current conflict as "quite significant" and "definitely alarming for the federal government."

She noted, however, that cracks might eventually appear: "How long this coalition lasts, because all of them have very different interests and represent very different ethnic constituencies, remains to be seen," she told DW.

Rodrigues added that, based on Control Risks' assessment, the fighting is likely to remain limited to Ethiopia's northern regions, particularly those situated along the borders between Tigray, Amhara, and Afar.

Furthermore, she highlighted that the idea of it spreading across the entire country was rather unlikely due to the difficulty of nationwide coordination, taking into consideration the rough terrain of the nation and the fragmentation of other opposition groups.

She noted that while the Pretoria agreement saw some early proof of implementation — including the TPLF's surrender of heavy weaponry in 2022 and 2023 — international attention had soon shifted to other global crises, leaving both sides free to quietly rearm and conscript supporters.

"It was more of a stopgap or a ceasefire, but people were quickly rearming in the background," she told DW.

Rodrigues also noted that the United States had recently lifted sanctions imposed during the 2020–2022 conflict — a step which, according to Mohammed, diminishes the West's leverage in the conflict to push combatants toward coming to the negotiation table.

In the meantime, civilians are bearing the cost of the warring parties: People caught up in the crossfire have been fleeing the conflict once more, adding further to a population of internally displaced persons (IDPs) that has not recovered from the previous war.

"Since 2020, they have not returned to their areas. They are still in makeshift IDP centers," Mohammed said, adding that there have also been isolated reports of drone strikes hitting civilian areas and of rebel groups deploying military hardware inside schools and clinics.

Telephone and internet connections have also been cut in parts of Tigray and Amhara, electricity has been disrupted, and flights have been suspended to several cities including Kombolcha and Lalibela.

Despite official assurances from all parties that civilians would be protected, Mohammed says that the reality on the ground tells a different story: "Words and practice are very contradictory."

Mohammed also noted that outgoing UN Secretary-General António Guterres dispatched a special envoy to the Horn of Africa, charged with mediating between the African Union, regional powers, and Egypt — many of the same parties involved in brokering the Pretoria agreement.

However according to Mohammed, meaningful negotiations are unlikely to bear fruit until at least one of the sides involved in the conflict "comes to understand that this violence will not help them to achieve what they want."

Rodrigues agrees, stressing that she cannot be optimistic about "an international or a regional mediation being the solution to the current fighting," adding that "intense, but geographically contained" fighting will continue for the foreseeable future.

German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul said that the Western Balkans country's ambition to join the bloc was being "heard in Brussels and in all European capitals."

Wadephul made the comments after traveling to Montenegro on Wednesday to attend the annual meeting of European foreign ministers as part of the so-called Berlin Process.

"Montenegro has made remarkable progress towards EU membership – it thus sets an example for the Western Balkans," Wadephul said in a post on X.

Anita Kunwar, an 18-year-old college student from city's East Delhi district, said a lot has changed after what happened at Aastha Kunj Park.

"Women and girls across the city are incredibly wary, anxious, and deeply concerned about their safety," she told DW. "An incident like this, especially in a public space where people go to relax, shatters your sense of security."
"Reports of these ghastly sexual assaults seem to have risen in recent years," said Deepika Singh, a university student in New Delhi. "It is as if these crimes are being normalized. It is sickening."

"The assaults have also become more brutal and aggressive," the 19-year-old told DW. "But I am not going to change the way I live my life because of them."

Hundreds of students, many of them women, marched in New Delhi a few days after the alleged assault, demanding greater safety in public spaces.

Students at nearby Lady Shri Ram College have also highlighted poorly lit roads, limited police presence and gaps in surveillance around the campus.

Across India, sexual violence is a ​major issue, with more than 80 rapes reported to police every day, according to data from the National Crime Records Bureau.

Women's rights groups, including People Against Rape in India (PARI), have long argued that official crime statistics do not capture the full scale of sexual violence because many assaults go unreported.

The gang rape and murder of a student in New Delhi in 2012 sparked nationwide protests and led to sweeping legal reforms, including tougher penalties for offenders and the creation of fast-track courts.

But the changes have done little to reduce sexual violence. Women's rights groups say entrenched patriarchal attitudes, understaffed police forces and delays in the justice system leave many perpetrators believing they can avoid punishment.

"Women's safety is non-negotiable. Women must be safe on every street, at every hour," said Delhi Lieutenant Governor Taranjit Singh Sandhu, while ordering the creation of a dedicated "Pink Force" of women personnel to enhance the safety of women and children in education establishments, metro stations and other locations across New Delhi.
The Supreme Court has now stepped in. On September 28, it took up the recent sexual assault cases on its own initiative and said they raised serious concerns about the way authorities were protecting women in public spaces.

The apex court has directed Delhi Police to identify vulnerable locations, improve patrolling and conduct audits of lighting and CCTV coverage in parks and other public spaces.

But Yashvardhan Azad, a retired senior police officer, says the problem starts well before the police are called.

"The failure is first the society where any woman going alone at night or with a friend is viewed with suspicion," Azad told DW. "The political climate in many states favour moral policing."

From early May through late July of this year, researchers surveyed some 2,400 German-speaking citizens aged 14 and older.

The cost of living is again their top concern, as it has been in the survey for many years in a row. This study has been conducted annually since the early 1990s.

Last year, 52% of those polled cited the rising cost of daily life as their greatest concern; in 2026, that figure has risen to 59%.

At a press conference in Berlin, Isabelle Borucki, a political scientist from Marburg who served as a consultant on the study, explained why.

"The price of key items — energy, housing and food — has risen noticeably. This reduces the financial leeway for leisure, vacations and purchases. People can afford less and less."

The study also shows that people are well aware that Germany's social security systems are in a serious crisis. Both the state pension fund and long-term care insurance are chronically in the red.

Most people also seem to realize that taxes and contributions will rise. That is why the fear of higher taxes is stronger than in previous years.

"Behind the fear of higher taxes and benefit cuts lies the question of fairness, with people wondering who will bear the costs of modernization," Borucki said. "And they worry, 'Can I still rely on getting benefits I am entitled to?'"

Only 45% of respondents fear that US President Donald Trump's policies are making the world a more dangerous place, researcher Grischa Brower-Rabinowitsch said.

## Gaza: NGO reports dire camp conditions, says Israel is moving &apos;Yellow Line&apos;

https://p.dw.com/p/5NWf3With much of Gaza either severely damaged or behind Israel's 'Yellow Line,' more than 80% of residents are estimated to be living in makeshift displacement campsImage: Dawoud Abu Alkas/REUTERSAdvertisementA new report on conditions in the Gaza Strip says that the roughly 1.8 million Palestinians in Gaza, or 86% of the population, living in displacement sites often face inhumane living conditions.

The Danish Refugee Council (DRC) published its findings on Thursday, almost three years after the October 7 terror attacks against Israel that triggered the war in Gaza, and a year after the phased October 10 ceasefire plan that was supposed to usher in the war's end.

"The October 2025 ceasefire in Gaza was supposed to end civilians' suffering. Nearly a year on, it exists largely in name only. Displacement in Gaza has not ended; it has changed shape," the report says.

The findings are based on group discussions with 195 people living in internal displacement camps across five governorates in Gaza.

Entitled "Confined by Design: The Human Cost of Protracted Displacement in Gaza," the report accuses Israel of "a policy of protracted displacement" since the ceasefire deal was reached.

One means of ensuring this, the DRC says, is the so-called "Yellow Line," which demarcates the area of eastern Gaza near the border that is controlled by the Israeli military and is, according to the report, creeping toward the coast.

"In addition to protracted displacement for hundreds of thousands of people, the defining feature of the so-called ceasefire is the Yellow Line which continues expanding westward and swallowing up more land, effectively squeezing families into 30% of Gaza's territory," said the NGO's country director for the occupied Palestinian territories, Alan Moseley.

Other groups, including the UN, have also reported the westward movement of the Yellow Line, as has the Israeli government, although some details differ.

Last month, Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu put the share of Gaza's territory inside the Yellow Line at 60% rather than 70%, but he also said the plan was to expand the area.

The DRC report also said aid organizations "describe being unable to operate within several kilometers of the line, either due to access constraints or security concerns."

It said that this led to crucial aid and supplies failing to reach displacement camps nearer the boundary.

The DRC and the Agricultural Development Association (PARC) highlighted a series of major shortcomings in the often informal tent shelters and makeshift camps that have sprung up amid Gaza's rubble, where more than eight in 10 residents are believed to be living.

The Danish Refugee Council also laid particular emphasis on lacking site management mechanisms at displacement camps.

It said only 36% of sites currently had a designated site manager, typically a national NGO, coordinating services and managing conditions.

Sites with such partners "have a structured community channel through which a gap, such as a collapsing cesspit, a missing water delivery, or an unsafe walkway, can be identified, escalated, and resolved," the report said.

The DRC and PARC issued a number of appeals and recommendations, some to "all parties to the conflict," most to "the Government of Israel, as the occupying power of Gaza" and others to "the international community" and "donors and humanitarian actors."

The groups called on Israel to "halt the further expansion of the Yellow Line" and to "disclose its current and future route transparently."

They also urged Israel to cease the construction of permanent military infrastructure in the area and to provide "credible assurance that current troop positions are not intended to be permanent."

They appealed for fewer restrictions on aid access, particularly so-called "dual-use" restrictions affecting medical supplies, water, sanitation and hygiene items, pest control, rubble removal and fuel.

The report also warned that political and donor interest in the situation had declined markedly in the year since the ceasefire, even though conditions had not improved proportionately.
