## Perspective: Midterm money madness

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

Campaign spending for these midterm elections passed $10 billion this week, according to the tracking group AdImpact. Maybe we should repeat that number - 10 billion. The Senate race in Ohio, for example, has already cost more than $460 million in combined ad spending. The Texas Senate race has surpassed half a billion, and there's 260 million more dollars spent in the Maine Senate race. The most expensive House race in the country isn't in California or New York, but Michigan's 7th Congressional District, with nearly $60 million in ads. Contests in Iowa and Florida are just under 50 million apiece.

There are limits on personal campaign contributions in the United States but, since the Supreme Court Citizens United decision in 2010, no limits on donations to super PACs, which can publicly support or oppose candidates but can't directly coordinate with campaigns. Rich donors have used that ruling to steer hundreds of millions of dollars to support both parties up and down the ballot. Elon Musk alone has given 276 million to Republican groups since July. George Soros has given more than $100 million to Democrats this election cycle.

Meanwhile, voters whom politicians are trying to win over with all these ads tell pollsters and reporters that their top issues this fall are the economy and the cost of living. Inflation is up. Gas prices have spiked since the start of the Iran war. Groceries cost a third more than they did in 2019. It is hard not to wonder - what could those hundreds of millions of ad dollars accomplish if they went toward food, gas or shelter for those in need or to help people with healthcare costs? Could some of it pay for school nurses, music teachers and librarians, subsidize addiction care, clean up public parks?

Maybe a candidate could win support these days by declaring, and I vow to spend less money on my campaign than my opponent. After all, as the Los Angeles Dodgers might remind us this week, spending the most money doesn't always deliver victory.

## Fraud, affordability and Trump policies drive friction in Minnesota Senate debate

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

Tight Senate races in Texas, Iowa and Kansas are getting a lot of attention during this year's midterm elections. But Minnesota also has a close race, and Peter Cox from Minnesota Public Radio joins us now. Thanks so much for being with us.

PETER COX, BYLINE: Good morning, Scott.

SIMON: Please tell us who's running and why this race has maybe been a little overlooked nationally.

COX: So Peggy Flanagan, a progressive Democrat who's been lieutenant governor in Minnesota for the last eight years, is up against Republican Michele Tafoya, who spent years as a sideline reporter for Monday Night Football and Sunday Night Football. And they're battling for a seat that's been held by Democrats since 2009. Recent polls have shown the two neck and neck, though Flanagan had a 4% edge in the most recent one.

But normally, such a tight margin would mean political parties would be pouring money into this race. But that's not what's happening here. This election, Republicans are facing challenges in what would be reliably red states, so they've been putting their money into shoring up those races instead. And that seems to be a big reason why Minnesota's race has been much quieter, even though it's one of several that could determine which party controls the U.S. Senate.

SIMON: President Trump indicated this week he might make a trip to Minnesota. How big a factor has President Trump been in this race?

COX: Yeah. Like many of these midterm races we're seeing nationwide, President Trump is a major factor. Tafoya, who has never held public office before, said when she announced her candidacy she'd be honored to have his endorsement. But in recent months, she's tried to distance herself from Trump. And that became tougher this week when Trump was speaking at a campaign rally in Ohio, and he railed against members of Minnesota's Somali community who he said were looting Minnesota welfare programs. He then implored people to get out and vote for Tafoya.

During a debate yesterday, Tafoya was asked whether she'd campaign with Trump if he comes to Minnesota, and she didn't answer the question. Peggy Flanagan has consistently said Tafoya would be a lapdog for Trump and was quick to point out how Tafoya only recently has tried to distance herself from the president in this campaign.

## Dozens of critically endangered orangutans rescued from Indonesian wildfires

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

The endangered orangutans of Borneo are holding on, but just barely. This year's super El Nino has made Indonesia extra hot and dry, and wildfires have burned 2.2 million acres of land this season.

JAMARTIN SIHITE: And it's destroyed the orangutan habitat, orangutan home.

SIMON: That's Jamartin Sihite, chairman of the Borneo Orangutan Survival Foundation, trying to pull orangutans to safety away from the fires and smoke. Huge swaths of orangutan habitats are burning.

SIHITE: If I look at the maps from the fire and the map from the population of orangutan in the forest, I thought more than 50 to 60% of the habitat of orangutan now getting burned or at least got impact from the smoke.

SIMON: The humans who are breathing the smoke will be vulnerable to asthma, heart attacks and even a shortened life expectancy, and orangutans, who share so much of our DNA, face the same health crises.

SIHITE: Like a human, acute respiratory infection. This is the biggest problems now for human and for the orangutan.

SIMON: He says that at least humans can wear masks on our own. Orangutans cannot. Sihite's organization is one of several in the area working to rescue and rehabilitate orangutans who are being affected by wildfires. They ran a similar rescue operation in 2015 when massive forest and peatland fires burned inside Indonesia.

Wendy Erb is a biological anthropologist who specializes in primate behavior. She's watching these fires from Ithaca, New York, but in 2015, she was in a field station in Borneo studying orangutan behavior.

WENDY ERB: My very favorite orangutan showed up in front of camp, and I couldn't resist just spending one more day with him. So I extended my stay by a day, and then that day turned into an extension of a month as the fires decided to pop up in our study area that very day that I was supposed to leave.

SIMON: Like many who are fighting today's fires, Erb and her team dug small wells into the ground that could be used with a generator to pump water up from several meters below the peatland, till finally, a month in, they got enough rain to put out the fires and to flee. But even when the fires are gone, Sihite says the survival struggle is not over.

SIHITE: After the fire, we still face the problems in the forest. We will face, like, scarcity of food because there is no pollination. There is no fruit.

SIMON: Sihite's team rescued a 5-year-old female orangutan called Novita last month. Novita was found in the forest canopy surrounded by smoke and flame. Twenty percent of her body was burned, including her hands and feet. She was treated with a nebulizer to help her breathe and is now recovering at a rehabilitation center.

How many like Novita still need help? Rescue agencies have pulled dozens of orangutans out of danger, including multiple mothers with infants and young males who had been stranded. They are still trying to figure out how to help the 104,000 Borneo orangutans that are left in Indonesia. There is no doubt that the fires are destructive, but Erb believes the orangutans will find a way to survive.

ERB: Because orangutans are quite resilient and pretty flexible, as well as incredibly intelligent and ecologically resourceful, they really can sort of make a go at even a pretty degraded habitat.

SIMON: That's biological anthropologist Wendy Erb, who studies orangutan behavior at Cornell University. We also heard from Jamartin Sihite, chairman of the Borneo Orangutan Survival Foundation.

## South Africa confronts unfinished business from the apartheid era

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

More than three decades after the end of apartheid in South Africa, the search for justice for many is far from over. Now renewed legal efforts seek to uncover the truth about some of apartheid's worst atrocities and, where possible, hold those responsible to account while some survivors and alleged perpetrators are still alive. Kate Bartlett has this report.

(SOUNDBITE OF FOOTSTEPS)

KATE BARTLETT, BYLINE: In this flat, windswept township cemetery, an hour from Johannesburg, one date appears again and again.

VINCENT THAMAE: Sharpeville, 21 March 1960. And underneath are the names of the victims.

BARTLETT: Sharpeville, the site of one of apartheid's South Africa's defining atrocities, when police shot dead at least 69 people protesting against apartheid-era pass laws, laws that restricted the movement of Black South Africans. The massacre became a turning point in the struggle against white minority rule in South Africa.

THAMAE: It's written, lest we forget, they died for true freedom.

BARTLETT: Vincent Thamae leads Voices of Sharpeville, a community group involved in the legal fight for justice for victims.

THAMAE: It's an African belief that they can still hear us. That is why when we come, we first greet and introduce ourselves, and we then go on with our business of the day.

BARTLETT: We are walking through the cemetery with four people whose fathers were killed or injured on that murderous day. Sixty-six years on, they and other descendants are seeking to bring a class-action lawsuit that could pave the way for reparations.

THAMAE: (Non-English language spoken).

UNIDENTIFIED GROUP: (Non-English language spoken).

THAMAE: (Non-English language spoken).

UNIDENTIFIED GROUP: (Non-English language spoken).

BARTLETT: Africa is our land, Thamae and others chant, invoking the struggle for liberation. Paulina Mathinye was 5 when her father was shot dead. Now 71, she says his death has shaped her life ever since.

PAULINA MATHINYE: I'm still angry, more especially when it comes to 21 of March. My anger comes out because that's the day my father was shot.

BARTLETT: Without her father, the family's main breadwinner, she couldn't finish school, and that's also had a knock-on effect on the lives of her children and grandchildren.

MATHINYE: So we struggled a lot. And we are still struggling now because nothing has been happening.

BARTLETT: It's unclear whether any of the police perpetrators are still alive. The families aren't seeking prosecutions now, but reparations. But first, their lawyers are asking the high court to overturn an apartheid-era law brought in after the massacre that still prevents any civil or criminal suits being filed. If this goes ahead, Paulina Mathinye says she'll use any money to fix up her modest home and pay for her four grandkids' educations.

DANIEL LEADER: It's crucial that these issues of deep injustice are properly dealt with and that South Africa confronts them and does not sweep them under the carpet.

BARTLETT: Daniel Leader is an international human rights lawyer advising the South African legal team representing the survivors and families.

LEADER: The Sharpeville case is part of the current trend of numerous other cases that have been brought to finally get accountability for apartheid-era crimes.

BARTLETT: And the cases keep coming. Last year, a court ruled that Chief Albert Luthuli, South Africa's first Nobel Peace Prize winner, was beaten to death by apartheid police, overturning a finding that he'd been hit by a train.

Now a state inquiry is investigating whether this case and hundreds of others recommended for prosecution by a post-apartheid commission were deliberately shelved in the name of reconciliation.

## How a retired Navajo Nation doctor earned a MacArthur genius grant for public art

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

Think of street art and what might come to mind is city buildings with giant murals, subway cars tagged with spray paint or stenciled sidewalks. Well, Chip Thomas creates street art in rural places - large-scale photographs on abandoned buildings across the Navajo Nation. This year, the MacArthur Foundation gave Chip Thomas what's known as a genius grant. KSUT's Adam Burke has this profile.

ADAM BURKE, BYLINE: North of Flagstaff, Arizona, the ribbon of highway disappears into a sea of sagebrush and yellow grasses. Chip Thomas steers his hatchback off the road to an abandoned cinder-block building.

CHIP THOMAS: This is the natural evolution of a piece on the desert. It's breaking down.

BURKE: Lean, bearded, dreadlocks tucked inside a canvas cap, Thomas approaches the tiny structure, which might have been a storage shed at one time. The roof and the door are gone now.

THOMAS: This is part of a sight-and-sound installation. Looks like the sound may have stopped.

BURKE: Inside, a 10-foot-wide black-and-white image covers the back wall. It's an art piece, spotlighting the devastating health effects of uranium mining among Navajo workers during the Cold War.

THOMAS: My images of Kee John, who died at a young age from a cancer related to his work in a uranium mine.

BURKE: In the photo, a young Navajo man wears a hard hat with a headlamp and operates uranium mining equipment, but the image is fading. Pieces of the installation are peeling off the walls.

(SOUNDBITE OF GEIGER COUNTER)

THOMAS: Oh, here we go. The sound loop is working.

BURKE: Then suddenly a solar-powered speaker kicks on, and we hear the garbled sounds of a Geiger counter. This is what's left of the installation's audio.

THOMAS: This is on its last leg (laughter). But it's impressive. It's been running 3 1/2 years now nonstop.

BURKE: Since 2009, Chip Thomas has been putting up artwork in places like this across the Navajo Nation, working against the unrelenting wind and sand.

THOMAS: The work is ephemeral, you know, is paper versus the elements, and the elements - Mother Nature always wins.

BURKE: On a landscape of disorienting scale and inscrutable distances, his black-and-white installations offer outsized visions of daily life on the rez - men on horseback, grandmothers herding sheep, the delighted faces of children playing. And for Thomas, any building is a canvas - deserted gas stations, old trailers, water tanks.

THOMAS: Structures that are close enough to the road that are big enough where I can put an interesting, hopefully engaging piece.

BURKE: Twenty-five minutes down the highway, we stop at a roadside stand where vendors are selling table loads of turquoise and silver jewelry.

TORREY CHARLEY: Good to see you, friend.

THOMAS: Yeah. You too.

CHARLEY: Nice to see you.

BURKE: Torrey Charley (ph) is working one of the stands. She's 29 and first started seeing Thomas' artwork as a teenager.

CHARLEY: I was like, oh, wow, you know, there's somebody that's interested in livestock and kids or, you know, the elders within the community.

BURKE: Charley is Navajo, or Dine, as Navajo people call themselves.

CHARLEY: I think, as a teenager, I was like, dang, I wonder who this is. And I probably assumed that it was a young Dine artist doing photography and finding out that he was a doctor. That was interesting. That was cool (laughter).

BURKE: Born and raised in North Carolina, Chip Thomas moved to the Navajo Nation in 1987 to work as a family physician in a remote, underserved region.

THOMAS: I had thought because of the isolation, I would just be there initially for two years, but my two years turned into 36.

BURKE: He spent time with patients outside of the clinic, helping them haul water, stack firewood and shear sheep. And as he grew closer to Navajo people, Thomas started photographing them.

THOMAS: There was, you know, a degree of trust, and I could stick a camera not literally in someone's face, but I could get closer physically than a stranger could and tell a more intimate story.

BURKE: Then in 2009, a friend showed him the work of the French street artist JR, who covered the walls of urban buildings with giant images. Thomas was 52 at the time.

THOMAS: It was like, oh, my God, this is it. This person has figured out how to take a documentary black-and-white photograph, blow it up to scale and to use it as street art. My mind was blown. It's like, I have to figure this out.

BURKE: This was the era of Banksy, Shepard Fairey and countless masked anonymous street artists who put up work in public spaces without permission.

THOMAS: It's getting away with something.

BURKE: At first, Thomas worked in secret too.

THOMAS: Sneaking around usually at night is a rush, and I was living the life of my street art heroes.

BURKE: For outsiders, this is a landscape that holds points of interest, like Monument Valley, Lake Powell and the Grand Canyon. But Thomas is concerned with the spaces in between and the community life that's often invisible to tourists.

KEVIN MOORE: People tend to look at a landscape like that as just bleak and empty and worthless.

BURKE: Art historian Kevin Moore curated a retrospective of Thomas' work in Cincinnati a few years ago.

MOORE: To drive across the desert and see a black-and-white photograph pasted to a building of a smiling girl or an old couple, it's striking. It's surreal. It's actually haunting.

BURKE: Moore says it's notable that Thomas' artwork is not for sale and free to everyone.

MOORE: It feels mysterious. It's sort of like who did this? Why did they do this? You expected to try to go to work on you in a way. But as you sit with it and contemplate it, you realize you don't understand what it's trying to do to you, and then you start to understand that maybe it's just trying to exist, maybe it's just trying to get you to exist with it. Maybe that's a good place to be with art.

BURKE: Every photograph is a window, every camera, a mirror. Chip Thomas uses his lens, his work to reflect the beauty and resilience of the people who live on this land. And at 69, he says he's not done yet.

THOMAS: It's still a passion play for me to create new work, tell new stories, and I like to do it in a way that engages people. My primary audience for the work on the Navajo Nation is Navajo people, and I'd like to think that there will be continued growth reflected in the work I create.

BURKE: That work will almost certainly be big and bold, like the land itself, visions that materialize for a short while and then gradually dissolve again into the desert.

For NPR News, I'm Adam Burke.
