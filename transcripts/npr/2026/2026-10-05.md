## Attacker intended to crash plane into Tel Aviv airport, ongoing investigation finds

MICHEL MARTIN, HOST:

An investigation into last week's midair attack aboard a commercial flight from Dubai to Tel Aviv has found that the co-pilot who tried to take control of the plane intended to crash it into Israel's Ben Gurion Airport.

A MARTÍNEZ, HOST:

That's according to a person familiar with the matter who is not authorized to speak publicly. In a few moments, we'll speak with a retired FBI special agent about the ongoing investigation. We begin with the news.

MARTIN: For more, we're joined by NPR's Kat Lonsdorf in Tel Aviv. Hi, Kat.

KAT LONSDORF, BYLINE: Hey, good morning.

MARTIN: So there are several countries conducting investigations into this attack. So what do we know?

LONSDORF: Well, over the weekend, officials in the United Arab Emirates, which is leading the investigation, announced that they had deemed the incident a terrorist attack. That assessment agreed with what Israeli officials had been saying earlier. We still don't have a clear motive for the attack. This morning, a source familiar with the investigation told NPR that the co-pilot who attacked the captain intended to fly the plane to Ben Gurion Airport, which was its destination, but then crash it into the airport. This all happened in the morning here when Ben Gurion would have been full of people. That source also said that it's likely the co-pilot acted alone. And just to be clear, this investigation is still very much ongoing.

MARTIN: Do we have any more detail about what actually happened on the plane?

LONSDORF: Yeah. We're starting to get a clearer picture. Investigators said that the co-pilot of the FlyDubai plane carried out the attack with what's called a crash ax. That's a lightweight emergency tool stored in the cockpit. It's used to break through panels or jammed doors in the event of a crash. The pilot was then able to open the cockpit door during the attack, and then passengers rushed in and subdued the attacker, while another pilot, who happened to be on board the plane, helped land it. Both the pilot and the co-pilot are alive and they're in the UAE, now being questioned.

MARTIN: Do we know any more about the attacker, the co-pilot?

LONSDORF: Yeah. The co-pilot is from Oman, according to Israeli officials, and that's raising questions here in Israel about how he was allowed to fly a plane heading to Israel, which doesn't allow pilots from countries it doesn't have diplomatic relations with to fly into its airspace. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said that this pilot must have gotten through a, quote, "loophole."

MARTIN: Kat, what has been the reaction from Israelis as this has all been playing out?

LONSDORF: Yeah. Generally, Israelis are praising the passengers and the captain of the plane, who's from India, as heroes who averted a major disaster. But there's also this larger debate playing out about who dropped the ball to allow for something like this to happen. The Ministry of Transportation has been pointing blame at the security services and vice versa, while Netanyahu's opponents have been saying that this is a huge failure on his part, calling for a larger investigation. After a lot of public pressure, Netanyahu announced last night that there will be some kind of formal examination, but there haven't been much more details on that.

MARTIN: And as you've been telling us, this is all happening as national elections in Israel are just weeks away.

LONSDORF: Yeah.

MARTIN: Elections that will determine whether or not Netanyahu remains prime minister. Is there any sense that this incident will affect voters?

LONSDORF: Well, Netanyahu is certainly making the media rounds about this attack, spending time with the passengers who stormed the cockpit and putting out press releases, going on foreign media. The polls here show that this is a very tight election coming up, and also that Israelis are largely already decided on how they're going to vote. So something like this might just entrench voters further, but it could be enough to motivate people who weren't planning to vote to go vote, and that could have an impact.

MARTIN: That is NPR's Kat Lonsdorf in Tel Aviv. Kat, thank you.

LONSDORF: Thank you.

## Retired FBI special agent discusses the FlyDubai attack investigation

A MARTÍNEZ, HOST:

Let's take a closer look at the FlyDubai flight investigation with Lara Burns. She's a retired FBI special agent with more than 23 years of experience in international terror investigations. She's now the head of terrorism research at the Program on Extremism at The George Washington University.

Lara, I think one of the things that has drawn me into this story, along with a lot of people, is that when we think about an airplane being taken over, we think of the threat outside of the cockpit. This one came inside of the cockpit. I mean, are we entering a brand-new world here?

LARA BURNS: Yes. I think we are. The most troubling question - you know, as you alluded to - it's not simply how an individual gained access to a plane, but how someone with prior security concerns was able to move through the aviation system at large and ultimately obtain access to a passenger aircraft cockpit, which has traditionally been seen as the safe area of the plane. You know, post 9/11, the safety measures that were put in place were designed to protect against threats emanating from outside the cockpit.

MARTÍNEZ: Now, what concerns you the most at this point in the investigation? What are you thinking about the most?

BURNS: I'm thinking about how we prevent another incident like this because, again, you know, our safety mechanisms and our, you know, view on threats to aviation have changed tremendously. So the question is, how do you identify a terrorist or extremist threat when the individual isn't trying to get onto the airplane but already has a seat in the cockpit? How do you vet for extremist behavior?

That vetting needs to happen to complement your conventional background checks. And it can't stop when a pilot gets his license. That vetting process must continue. You know, radicalization can happen at any time. And so I think that we really have to change our view on how we look at the threat to aviation overall, but looking very seriously at vetting procedures for pilots who have access to those cockpits.

MARTÍNEZ: Yeah. So The Associated Press reported that UAE officials shared a - with Israel a list of passengers and crew for the FlyDubai flight. And that list did not seem to raise any red flags at all with Israeli authorities. Why do you think that is?

BURNS: You know, I - it sounds like there were likely some failures in the vetting procedures, right? The individuals are supposed to be vetted prior to having access to an aircraft that's going to fly into Israel, and that apparently did not happen. What I worry about is the finger-pointing, right? Everyone wants to blame everyone else. But in reality, what needs to happen is we need to have open, honest investigations to determine where the security failures occurred, where the vetting failed, so that those areas can be identified and then improved, right? It's not helpful for everyone to be blaming everyone else. We need transparent accounting of where the failures occurred so that then the - those failures can be addressed and prevented the next time.

MARTÍNEZ: So, Lara, officials also told the Associated Press that the suspect was an Omani national who had been barred from piloting by Oman Air because of extremist views. How do you think he wound up up co-piloting the flight on FlyDubai? I mean, do airlines typically share this kind of information?

BURNS: You know, that is a great question. You know, we're talking about a situation where you have an individual with security concerns that didn't just gain access to a plane. He somehow managed to move through the aviation system, obtain a job as a pilot and then gain access to a passenger aircraft cockpit en route to Tel Aviv. And, you know, that's a great question. Like, was information shared between countries? Was there information shared between airlines? And we don't have those answers yet, but certainly authorities are going to be investigating to try to determine how this individual passed whatever security and vetting procedures were put in place, right? What did FlyDubai do to vet this individual before hiring him? What did they do for ongoing vetting procedures to ensure that this pilot and others, you know, were up to the standards that a pilot should be, that they did not pose an insider threat? But the broader question is going to be, was there information sharing between the country of Oman and the country of the UAE and other countries in the region regarding this individual's extremist past and radicalization?

MARTÍNEZ: Should that be something that countries are forced to do somehow? And if so, how would it be able to be enforced?

BURNS: You know, there is an information sharing process among countries that have diplomatic relations. The problem is that not all countries have diplomatic relations. And when it comes to individuals that have extremist pasts and are believed to be security concerns, that information really does need to be passed whether or not there are diplomatic relations between countries. If an individual migrates from one country to the next, one would hope that the security concerns that the originating country had would be passed to the, you know, country of destination. That's not always the case.

MARTÍNEZ: It just seemed like opening the door was the only thing that saved everyone from just certain death, I'm assuming, if the plane crashes. Is there something that maybe needs to be done to have communication to outside of the cockpit for anyone in the cockpit that is being threatened?

BURNS: Absolutely. I mean, you know, there are measures put in place. Like, there is that release inside the cockpit.

MARTÍNEZ: Yeah.

BURNS: But what happens if, as in this instance, the co-pilot is disabled? So there are going to have to be enhanced measures put in place that allow for an emergency response when one or both of the captains inside the cockpit are disabled.

MARTÍNEZ: Lara Burns is a retired FBI special agent now at the Program on Extremism at The George Washington University. Lara, thanks.

BURNS: Thank you so much.

## Mahmoud Khalil’s memoir is a peek inside immigration detention and his fight to stay

LEILA FADEL, HOST:

Mahmoud Khalil thought that he had found a safe place to call home when he became a permanent resident of the United States. He graduated from Columbia University. He got a job. His wife was pregnant. Then in March of 2025, he was detained by plain-clothes, masked immigration agents and clandestinely whisked from New York City to rural Louisiana.

MAHMOUD KHALIL: A lot of people look at my case as a pure immigration case or a pure First Amendment case. In reality, it is a combination of Palestine, First Amendment and immigration. They weaponized immigration law to stifle my speech because I spoke about Palestine.

FADEL: He became a national story, the government trying to paint him as a danger while providing no proof. Khalil, his lawyers and human rights groups all argue this is about silencing. He's telling his own story in his book, "No Land To Stand On: Notes From Detention." I sat down with him to talk about it in our New York bureau.

Mahmoud Khalil, welcome back to MORNING EDITION.

KHALIL: Thank you so much for having me here.

FADEL: So I wanted to start with, you write about your story within the story, you say, of generations of displacement and detention. You see your detention as part of that continuing story?

KHALIL: Absolutely, my grandfather was exiled from Palestine, from Tiberias to Syria about 15 years ago. My parents also fled Syria to Europe. I fled to Lebanon and later to the United States. Although I am considered now in one of the safest places on Earth, still like...

FADEL: Here in the United States?

KHALIL: Here in the United States. But still, like, violence found a way to displace me, state violence here. And it's a story about, like, you know, how borders change also.

FADEL: You call your detention and this case against you state violence. You see it that way?

KHALIL: Absolutely. I never imagined that this anti-Palestinian bigotry would be institutionalized, that the federal government would actually go and depend or collaborate with these private actors to go after noncitizen protesters. So I really felt very betrayed.

FADEL: You spend a lot of time writing about your own case, yes, but also about the detainees around you. Why did you focus on these other men that were going through completely different stories than yours?

KHALIL: Because what's happening behind ICE detention doors is a stain on the U.S. Constitution, a stain on the U.S. consciousness. It was so normal to see men cry in detention because they were just like, what did we do to warrant the administration call us criminals? At first, when I got into detention, I felt that my story is the saddest story. You know, I never committed a crime. I had a green card. My wife is American. I'm expecting a baby in a month.

But a day in, I discovered that I'm actually privileged. I have lawyers. My wife is safe. But for the many of the people around me, were faceless. Like, they did not have anyone paying attention to their stories. And I felt as if I was sent there for a reason, to bring attention to what they are going through.

FADEL: Is that in part why you wrote the book?

KHALIL: Absolutely. I feel that we must act now because this is still happening right now.

FADEL: You write about feeling flattened in this case, that you became a headline, and that hurt you. Walk me through the gap between what you think the world knows about you and who you are, like, being flattened in that way.

KHALIL: During detention, I could see the headlines about me. I can see protests holding my pictures. While in reality, you know, I was just trying to get out, to be with my wife or to call Noor and be - you know, just like to get what would happen with the baby? Would I be out for the baby? But also, I was looking for mayonnaise, like, to put on my sandwich in detention. I was looking for a better bunk bed. And that...

FADEL: Right, because you write about being close to the bathroom when you first get there...

KHALIL: Exactly.

FADEL: ...And being excited when you get near the wall.

KHALIL: Absolutely. There was these two realities. The juxtaposition between these things was stark to me. Palestinians, at the end of the day, you know, we have the same aspirations as anyone else. We have the same also, you know, deficiencies. And that's one thing that flattens, that you're either perfect or not.

FADEL: Another part of your book that was interesting was the way detainees, you all comforted each other and helped each other. You write about the way they tried to celebrate for you after your son, Deen, was born. Tell me that story.

KHALIL: I tried not to tell anybody that my wife, Noor, was delivering. But my bunkmate and others, like, noticed that I would be, you know, going back and forth to the phone booth. And, you know, next day, out of the blue, I see, like, a Russian detainee bringing me cake, or prison-made cake, which is, you know, mashed up from, like, whatever leftover from these cookies that we get, with some of the dried oranges as well. And that was, like, worth of at least two weeks of leftovers, you know? It was like one of, yeah, the beautiful moments in detention where we really cared about each other, because there you feel that you're forgotten.

FADEL: The title of your book, "No Land To Stand On." You chose those words. Why?

KHALIL: You know, I was born and raised in a Palestinian refugee camp in Syria. When I came here, it was the first time I had something permanent, permanent residency. And I felt, you know, like I got it. And then, you know, all this happened to me. It made me realize that I actually don't have a land to stand on. But also, it comes from, you know, a conversation I had with my mother.

She told me, like, for what? I did everything in Syria to build you, like, a big house. Like, we had - she literally, like, paid 30 years of work into that. And then she had to flee that, like, in a day. And she literally felt, like, that defeated that she told me, like, do I want to build a life here to only then have to leave everything and go to another place? Sort of she lost, like, that purpose. And that really got into me, around the impermanence of just, like, our lives.

FADEL: Mahmoud Khalil is the author of "No Land To Stand On: Notes From Detention." Thank you for speaking with us.

KHALIL: Thank you so much, Leila, for having me.

## In Virginia, voting rights restoration is tough. A ballot measure could change that

MICHEL MARTIN, HOST:

Now to Virginia, which has been one of the toughest places for someone convicted of a felony to reclaim their voting rights. There, the governor decides who gets those rights back after they get out of prison. Virginians are voting on a measure that would take the issue out of the governor's hands. Jahd Khalil from VPM News reports.

JAHD KHALIL, BYLINE: Amber Bowman (ph) needs a little help voting. It's her first time casting a ballot since she went to prison.

AMBER BOWMAN: I feel like I'm a teenager, just turning 18, getting my voting rights for the first time.

KHALIL: Bowman is 38 and last voted about two decades ago. She was one of about 250,000 Virginia residents who couldn't vote because of a conviction until her voting rights were restored by Governor Abigail Spanberger. Now Bowman and others in Virginia are voting on changing the state Constitution to automatically restore voting rights of those convicted of felonies.

BOWMAN: Well, I think if you've served your sentence, why shouldn't you get your rights restored? Like, why is it a life sentence in Virginia if you get a felony conviction?

KHALIL: Virginia's practice traces back to the Jim Crow era. In 1902, delegates rewrote the constitution Virginia used to rejoin the Union. They sought to remove Reconstruction-era protections for Black Virginians. Nicole Porter is senior director of advocacy with The Sentencing Project, which supports making rights restoration automatic.

NICOLE PORTER: The constitutional record from the early 1900s was clear in terms of the intent to disenfranchise Black electorate, using felony disenfranchisement to dilute Black political power.

KHALIL: The debate over voting as a consequence of breaking the law often focuses on criminal justice arguments instead of the history. But Porter says keeping people from voting is bad public safety policy.

PORTER: And we know that people who have prior felony convictions, who do vote, who do participate in their community, who do have stable housing, who do have stable employment, are less likely to reoffend and less likely to return to prison.

KHALIL: The amendment wouldn't just change who can cast a ballot. Unless voting rights are restored, people who were convicted of a felony can't serve on a jury, run for office or work the election polls. Porter says those are key parts of being in the community. But critics of the proposed amendment say that it's best to assess whether a person has been rehabilitated on a case-by-case basis. Sandra Alcaide is a law professor at Regent University.

SANDRA ALCAIDE: When an individual has broken the law and then gets to vote on the law after they've broken the law, in a serious manner, you know, there's this idea of should that person be able to automatically vote on something that they didn't really have much regard for initially?

KHALIL: The debate over the amendment was also thrown a curveball. A federal judge said that Virginia was breaking a 19th century law that limited which crimes would disenfranchise a voter, and that about 235,000 people should have been able to vote all along. The state now says only people convicted of murder and manslaughter would need to apply for their voting rights. Yet proponents of the amendment say voting rights need a more durable safeguard than a court ruling, proponents like Democratic state Senator Mamie Locke.

MAMIE LOCKE: It's evolving the Virginia Constitution to declare that Virginia's democracy is alive and well and that it belongs to everybody, even those who have been imprisoned and have served their time and are being reintegrated into society.

KHALIL: Back at her polling location, Amber Bowman is about to finally vote again.

BOWMAN: So I've worked really hard since I came from incarceration, and just being able to get my voting rights back, it feels like, OK, people are letting me move past this conviction.

KHALIL: She says steps like these help people like her to come home from incarceration and not go back. She said voting was easy once she got in the door.

For NPR News, I'm Jahd Khalil in Richmond, Virginia.
