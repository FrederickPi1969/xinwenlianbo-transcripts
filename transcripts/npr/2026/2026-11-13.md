## Up First briefing: Nvidia CEO; Ukraine-Russia talks; Chris Van Hollen

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)

MICHEL MARTIN, HOST:

Nvidia founder Jensen Huang is out as CEO after alleged affairs with two employees and a suspected Chinese spy.

A MARTÍNEZ, HOST:

Can the company powering the AI boom stay on top without him?

MARTIN: I'm Michel Martin. That's A Martínez, and this is UP FIRST from NPR News.

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)

MARTIN: Huang had unusual access to President Trump, from Mar-a-Lago dinners to trips abroad. Now lawmakers from both parties want answers about what the administration knew about Huang's affairs and when. How much did the relationship influence U.S. chip policy towards China?

MARTÍNEZ: And after separate meetings with Presidents Vladimir Putin and Volodymyr Zelenskyy, U.S. envoys say European governments can join the next round of Russia-Ukraine talks. Could that mean progress in ending the war? Stay with us. We've got news you need to start your day.

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)

MARTÍNEZ: Nvidia's CEO, Jensen Huang, is out, and the company's chief financial officer is taking over as interim CEO.

MARTIN: This comes after allegations that Huang had multiple affairs, including with a woman who may have been recruited by China to spy on him.

MARTÍNEZ: NPR tech correspondent John Ruwitch has been following the saga. He joins us now to discuss Nvidia's future. So, all right, John, Jensen Huang is out. Who is his in-house replacement?

JOHN RUWITCH, BYLINE: The board said the company's CFO, Colette Kress, is stepping into the role while they search for a permanent replacement for Huang. Kress is a longtime Nvidia executive. She joined Nvidia 13 years ago after working in finance at tech companies for many years before that. You know, Jensen Huang was very much the public face of the company, but Kress has been prominent. She has, for instance, been on dozens of earnings calls alongside Huang when the company reports its quarterly results. So she's a known quantity inside Nvidia, with its investors and with companies it does business with.

MARTÍNEZ: All right. So right now, it feels like Nvidia is in a big mess. I mean, how substantial are the challenges she faces right now?

RUWITCH: Yeah, they are immense. Nvidia is a linchpin of the AI economy and, by extension, the U.S. economy because it makes the most widely used chips powering the AI revolution. But it just lost a visionary leader who deserves a lot of credit for building the company and making it into the juggernaut that it is. I mean, it has an 80- to 90% market share in chips called graphics processing units, or GPUs, that AI runs on. Tammy Madsen is a professor at Santa Clara University Leavey School of Business, and she says a major order of business for Kress is going to be reassuring the world that Nvidia still has its mojo, especially at a time when the share price has been falling because of the crisis.

TAMMY MADSEN: And I think for her, it's important to sort of provide confidence back to the market that we are going to turn this around. And here's the steps we're taking to move forward.

RUWITCH: Nvidia shares have been trending downward and are off more than 10% since last Friday, before the whole saga blew up over the weekend. Going forward, Kress has a balancing act managing some serious short-term challenges and Nvidia's long-term strategy.

MARTÍNEZ: So what might that look like?

RUWITCH: Well, short term, there will be fallout from Jensen Huang's downfall. There's an internal investigation into Huang's affair with the Chinese woman that'll play out at Nvidia. Congress is also preparing to probe the spy allegations, which could put the company under a hot spotlight. Then there are all the relationships with Nvidia's partners and the deals that Huang handled that are going to have to be tended to. That includes some of the so-called circular financing agreements where Nvidia invests money in companies that then turn around and buy chips for data centers. Now, luckily, Kress, as CFO, will be intimately familiar with those arrangements, so that's a plus. On the tech front, Nvidia is king of the hill on AI chips because it makes really popular gold-standard products, and Kress is going to have to make sure that Nvidia stays on point to protect market share.

MADSEN: It is a very healthy organization. They've been moving forward on a strong strategy. But I think what needs to remain strong is that long-term vision.

MARTÍNEZ: It sounds like Kress has an overfull plate. I mean...

RUWITCH: Yeah.

MARTÍNEZ: ...Is she set up for success or not?

RUWITCH: Well, the company's in a strong market position. Demand for its products is not expected to drop off anytime soon, just because more and more people are using AI. One question to watch is how much trust and leeway the board gives Kress as she runs the company. They're making the right noises, though. In a statement online, the board chairman said they have, quote, "full confidence" in her and the leadership team, and that they expect the company's operations, customer commitments, and product roadmap to continue without interruption.

MARTÍNEZ: All right, that's NPR's John Ruwitch. John, thanks a lot.

RUWITCH: You're welcome, A.

MARTÍNEZ: Former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's resignation is much more than a tech story. It also has consequences for members of the U.S. government.

MARTIN: The president has been very friendly with Huang this term, and now there are questions about who knew what and when about Huang's extramarital relationship, what the affair partner's motivations may have been and how all this affects U.S. policy.

MARTÍNEZ: NPR White House correspondent Danielle Kurtzleben is here to talk about this. So, Danielle, let's find out the latest on this because the two men are very, very close. So what's the president been saying about Huang?

DANIELLE KURTZLEBEN, BYLINE: Well, Trump has remained very supportive. On Monday, he said he had talked to Huang that day, quote, "just to see if he's feeling all right." And after Huang's resignation, Trump said he had done a fantastic job. Now, as to what comes next, Trump said he wants former Apple CEO Tim Cook to take over the company. Of course, it's not up to Trump, and we haven't heard a response from Cook yet. But however Trump feels about Huang, all of this is now raising some really serious questions for the White House, especially Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick.

MARTÍNEZ: Why that?

KURTZLEBEN: Well, because commerce handles imports and exports, and the big question here is on export controls. Should the U.S. have more tightly restricted the chips it exports? And so now, lawmakers from both parties have been saying they want more information from Lutnick, especially about timing. The big question here is when did he know that Huang was in contact with a suspected Chinese asset, and what did he do in response? And also, last night, South Carolina Republican Senator Tim Scott, chair of the Banking Committee, said he is summoning Huang and Lutnick to a December 3 hearing. Now, Trump, by the way, for his part, said this week he didn't recall receiving the information about the suspected asset in a memo.

MARTÍNEZ: When it comes to Trump and Huang, I mean, what's their relationship look like?

KURTZLEBEN: Well, at least publicly, it's been a love fest. Trump has praised Huang a lot. Huang has spent a lot of time with the president in turn. He's visited the White House and Mar-a-Lago. He went to the U.K. for the president's state visit last year, and he went with the president to China this year. But there's more than visits at work here. Huang and the White House negotiated that the government would get a 15% cut of the sales of certain Nvidia chips to China. And there's also this big question of what Huang's influence is.

Here's one example. In spring of 2025, Huang attended a $1 million-a-head dinner at Mar-a-Lago, and as my colleagues, Emily Feng and Bobby Allyn, reported at the time, the Trump administration had been expected to restrict sales to China of an Nvidia chip called the H20. Well, then after that dinner, the White House put the H20 restrictions on hold and Nvidia promised new investments in U.S. AI centers. Now, we can't draw the direct cause-and-effect line there, but there's good reason to think this relationship has been great for Huang and Nvidia.

MARTÍNEZ: And now the president will be visiting China next week. I mean, how does all that affect this trip?

KURTZLEBEN: Well, Trump was asked about this this week, and he didn't have much to say on the subject, but it is possible that this doesn't really impact Trump and Xi's conversations much at all. Kurt Tong is managing partner at The Asia Group - that's a consulting firm - and he told my colleague Deepa Shivaram this this week.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED NPR CONTENT)

KURT TONG: I think the main impact is here in the United States, where there are calls for accountability with respect to Mr. Huang's activities. And the net result of that is to provide a tailwind to persons who are interested in tightening up the technology-denial regime toward China.

KURTZLEBEN: Now, AI is always a major topic when the two leaders meet, so they'll discuss it. But for now, all eyes are really on that accountability piece and those potential restrictions.

MARTÍNEZ: All that's NPR's Danielle Kurtzleben. Thanks a lot.

KURTZLEBEN: You're welcome.

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)

MARTÍNEZ: White House envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner have been racking up the miles this week.

MARTIN: All in a pursuit of an end to Russia's war in Ukraine. First, they led peace talks in Abu Dhabi. Then they were on to Moscow for a meeting with Russian President Vladimir Putin, followed by a trip to Ireland, where they met up with Ukraine's president, Volodymyr Zelenskyy.

MARTÍNEZ: To discuss what progress was or maybe wasn't made toward peace, we're joined now by NPR's Charles Maynes in Moscow. Charles, so let's start with their visit with Putin. What struck you from that meeting?

CHARLES MAYNES, BYLINE: Yeah. Sure, A. You know, both sides noted positive momentum on the humanitarian track of negotiations, including a mass prisoner swap that's in the offing. But the real focus this week has been on this idea of building a new security framework between Russia and Europe. Kushner, in particular, sees this as the way to unlock concessions towards ending the actual war. But it's meant also trying to get Europe to the negotiating table, which Russia has refused to do, citing Europe's military support to Ukraine. So fundamentally, in coming to Moscow, Kushner was there to make a pitch.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

JARED KUSHNER: We've seen a big change with the Europeans, and we think there's a very strong momentum and desire to engage together.

MARTÍNEZ: All right, so how did Putin respond?

MAYNES: Well, in his public comments, Putin wasn't particularly receptive. He accused Europe of prolonging the war through its military support for Kyiv. He categorically ruled out this idea of European peacekeepers on the ground to help guarantee a future security in Ukraine, saying Russia would regard these soldiers as legitimate military targets. Here's Putin speaking through a Kremlin interpreter.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

PRESIDENT VLADIMIR PUTIN: (Through interpreter) After the peace deal is done in Ukraine, they're going to deploy their troops, which would mean a continuation of the war. So this is a vicious circle.

MAYNES: Now, A, Witkoff later said Putin had agreed to include Europeans in the future talks. This is what the U.S. was after. Although, a Kremlin aide who briefed reporters afterwards made no mention of that. But I'll say this, you know, Putin has always framed the war in Ukraine around grievances over Russia's treatment after the end of the Cold War and NATO's expansion east. So European security is clearly an issue Russia wants on the table, at least in some form.

MARTÍNEZ: OK. The next stop then was Ireland, where they met with Ukraine President Volodymyr Zelenskyy.

MAYNES: Yeah, talk about a change of venues.

MARTÍNEZ: Yeah.

MAYNES: Witkoff and Kushner went from the Kremlin grand hall to meeting Zelenskyy in a small lounge at a military airbase in Dublin. Now, Zelenskyy welcomed the news that the Europeans will apparently be part of future negotiations. That's something Europe also has pushed for really out of fear that Russia and U.S. could cut some deal without Europe's involvement. Zelenskyy also said he hoped the new European presence would somehow help achieve a ceasefire.

MARTÍNEZ: OK. So with Europe now in the negotiating room, I mean, is that progress, technically?

MAYNES: Well, you know, Kushner certainly seems to think so, because the U.S.'s attempts to negotiate ceasefires with Russia or push for territorial concessions from Ukraine really haven't worked. You know, but this focus on European security has also exposed a split between the Trump administration and its European allies, one where the White House now takes these Russian complaints about the threat posed by NATO seriously or at least thinks they can't be ignored if this war is to somehow be negotiated to an end. While the Europeans say, hold on, you know, why should Putin be rewarded with a say in our security, particularly given all the damage he's done in Ukraine?

MARTÍNEZ: That's NPR's Charles Maynes in Moscow. Thanks a lot, Charles.

MAYNES: Thanks, A.
