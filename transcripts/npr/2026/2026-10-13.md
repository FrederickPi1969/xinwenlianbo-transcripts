## Up First briefing: Pro-Trump ads; GOP campaign rallies; Ilhan Omar

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)

A MARTÍNEZ, HOST:

President Trump's rallies for other candidates sound a lot like rallies for Trump himself.

STEVE INSKEEP, HOST:

Other Republicans share the stage briefly, overshadowed by a president who says it's like he's on the ballot. How much are candidates getting out of that?

MARTÍNEZ: I'm A Martínez. That is Steve Inskeep, and this is UP FIRST from NPR News.

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)

MARTÍNEZ: Congresswoman Ilhan Omar says the Justice Department made her a political target. Federal agents searched her home on Monday, 22 days before the election. It's not clear what investigators are looking for, but they work for an administration that has claimed immigration fraud.

INSKEEP: Also, U.S. allies do not like the president's plan to buy Russian diesel. They're tightening sanctions on Moscow instead. How far apart are the U.S. and Europe? Stay with us. We've got the news you need to start your day.

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)

MARTÍNEZ: President Trump has picked up the pace of his campaign travel ahead of the midterm elections.

INSKEEP: The president held a rally in Youngstown, Ohio, last night and travels to Alaska today before doing still more events later in the week.

MARTÍNEZ: NPR White House correspondent Danielle Kurtzleben was in Youngstown last night. Danielle, so tell us how the president is trying to fire up his base.

DANIELLE KURTZLEBEN, BYLINE: Well, there's this one line he keeps saying in his speeches.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

PRESIDENT DONALD TRUMP: Just pretend that I'm on the ballot because, in a way, I am on the ballot 'cause they will try and destroy everything we've done.

KURTZLEBEN: Now, it does feel like he's on the ballot when he says this in the sense that these events resemble very much his 2024 presidential campaign rallies. He gets up there and talks about his accomplishments. He calls out his superfans, the Front Row Joes. He insults Democrats he dislikes. But in addition, last night, I'll add, he was interrupted twice by protesters. It appears that one really got kind of roughed up before law enforcement removed them. Trump, for his part, made fun of the protester's weight. So, yeah, it was an eventful rally, but also very much a Trump rally like many others.

MARTÍNEZ: Now, to be clear, the president is not on the ballot. But...

KURTZLEBEN: Right.

MARTÍNEZ: ...Do the candidates who are on the ballot get any attention at these events?

KURTZLEBEN: They do - some. Ohio GOP gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy spoke for a few minutes, as did GOP Senator Jon Husted, who's in a tight race against former Democratic Senator Sherrod Brown. But still, it's striking. Trump is out campaigning for others, but these rallies are so much of Trump being Trump - the insults, the crassness, using a swear word or two. It all just emphasizes that the GOP is still his party, even while his approval continues to sink. So you got to wonder, for a candidate like Ramaswamy or Husted, what's the return here on appearing with Trump?

MARTÍNEZ: Yeah. And that's the thing, because a lot of these rallies are being held in states which Trump won by a lot. So what's the logic behind even doing these events?

KURTZLEBEN: You know, Trump has said recently that he thinks these events really do help get out the vote. In addition, earlier this month, he was asked about going to red states, and he said he was going, quote, "just to make sure." Now, in recent weeks, like you said, he's spoken in Ohio and Texas, which both have tight Senate and governors races. In 2024, he won those states and Alaska, where he's headed today, by more than 10 points. And he's been doing rallies in even deeper red states, like Tennessee and Alabama. Now, of course, we don't know how this helps turnout, but it's also possible he's just hoping for good optics with these events to show himself drawing enthusiastic crowds of thousands.

MARTÍNEZ: All right. So that's where Trump is going. But what about the rest of the GOP? Where are they putting their resources?

KURTZLEBEN: Well, looking at ad money - that's a way to show what parties and PACs are thinking. Now, the most expensive races in that sense are no surprise. It's Senate races in Texas, Ohio and Michigan. But lately we've seen a GOP ad-spending uptick in some other places, like Iowa and Kansas, both of which have been pretty red in recent years. And that possibly signals new concerns about those red states. Plus, there's been a pullback in spending in the North Carolina Senate race, which the Cook Political Report now considers a likely Democratic win. Now, none of this tells you who's going to win, but it can give some hints as to what party operatives are thinking.

MARTÍNEZ: All right. That's NPR's Danielle Kurtzleben. Thanks a lot.

KURTZLEBEN: Yeah. You're welcome.

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)

INSKEEP: Minnesota Representative Ilhan Omar says the Trump administration has made her a political target.

MARTÍNEZ: Omar is a Democrat. On Monday morning, federal immigration agents searched her home in Washington, D.C.

INSKEEP: NPR's Luke Garrett was there as federal agents took over a city block to execute their search warrant, and now he's in our studios. Welcome to 31.

LUKE GARRETT, BYLINE: Morning, Steve.

INSKEEP: So what happened?

GARRETT: So early Monday, federal agents searched Congresswoman Omar's home in northeast D.C. One of her neighbors, 79-year-old Sharon Jackson, said she woke up to loud knocking at 4:30 a.m.

SHARON JACKSON: I got awakened by a boom, boom, boom.

GARRETT: Jackson says she walked out her front door. She said she saw vans and agents with gloves walking to and from Omar's home, just across the street. And she said she was worried.

JACKSON: If you talk to Miss Omar, tell her that your neighbor with the other teddy dog says she's in my prayers.

GARRETT: By the time I got there around 10 a.m., federal agents had closed off the entire block. Neighbors, other journalists, myself - we were all watching.

INSKEEP: OK. So why were federal agents doing this?

GARRETT: The agents there that I talked to didn't comment, but the Department of Justice says agents with Homeland Security Investigations, quote, "executed a judicially authorized search warrant." Now, HSI is an arm of U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, often known as ICE. The DOJ says the search of Omar's home was part of what they call an ongoing federal criminal investigation.

INSKEEP: OK. I guess useful to know that they say it's a criminal investigation, but what's the alleged crime or suspected crime?

GARRETT: So, Steve, we just haven't gotten the full picture yet, but a current official not authorized to speak publicly about this told me this search of Omar's home is part of a multiyear probe that involved numerous whistleblower complaints. And a former official who also wasn't authorized to speak publicly but who had been briefed about this very investigation says the home search deals with allegations of immigration fraud. Now, Vice President JD Vance has previously alleged Omar committed immigration fraud. It's something she denies. And, you know, Steve, Omar is an American citizen. She came to the U.S. as a refugee from Somalia.

INSKEEP: OK. What is this United States citizen saying?

GARRETT: Right. Congresswoman Omar put out a statement and went on social media Monday afternoon. She called the search an attempt to target political enemies and weaponize federal law enforcement. She also wrote that Trump has targeted her for a decade with what she called smear campaigns. She also said she wouldn't be silenced by this.

INSKEEP: Trump certainly has been vocal about Omar. What is he saying now?

GARRETT: Yes. He continued that trend. On Monday night, Trump called Omar totally crooked just before boarding Marine One at the White House. He said he had nothing to do with the actual investigation into Omar. That's up to the DOJ, Trump said. But, Steve, we do know that senior DOJ leadership, including acting Deputy Attorney General Trent McCotter - you know, they all were consulted before this search, and sources, you know, familiar told our colleague Ryan Lucas that the DOJ approved this.

INSKEEP: And we should note the Trump administration has been explicit that the Department of Justice should follow Trump's orders in all matters. I want to ask about the timing of this - 22 days before the midterm election, and there is a rule at the Department of Justice that they should not make public moves in investigations that relate to officials or candidates within 60 days of the election. How do they explain this?

GARRETT: So you're right, Steve. There is a rule, but it's just not legally binding. It's been more of a norm, an unwritten understanding. It's all meant to protect elections from, you know, politicized use of law enforcement. But the DOJ has really been inconsistent about this over the past few election cycles.

INSKEEP: OK. NPR's Luke Garrett, thanks for coming by.

GARRETT: You bet.

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)

INSKEEP: Leaders in Europe do not like President Trump's latest moves in the war in Ukraine.

MARTÍNEZ: Yeah. Trump demanded that Ukraine stop its attacks on Russian oil refineries. Trump also said that there was a ceasefire on attacks against energy infrastructure, which neither Ukraine nor Russia seem to know about. The president also lifted sanctions on Russian diesel fuel, even as Europeans impose new sanctions on the Russian military complex.

INSKEEP: Teri Schultz is following all this from a European security conference in Warsaw, Poland. Welcome.

TERI SCHULTZ: Thank you.

INSKEEP: So what is it about the president's statements that concerns allies of Ukraine?

SCHULTZ: I think there's just really no faith in these moves by the White House, Steve. You know, President Zelenskyy spoke here remotely in Warsaw and said that even as this ceasefire was announced by President Trump, Ukrainian oil refineries were being bombed. So European officials and experts treated this basically as one more attempt by the White House to pressure Ukraine in favor of Russia. Now, one of the strongest reactions came from Trump's former Ukraine envoy, Keith Kellogg, who shared what he would advise Zelenskyy to do in quite colorful language.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

KEITH KELLOGG: I'd burn down every one of the goddamn refineries that Russia has today. Take them all out. And I said (ph), you make them pay the price. You take it home. It's a military target. Take those targets out.

SCHULTZ: Now, Kellogg left his position in January and has been pretty outspoken about his support for Kyiv since.

INSKEEP: Now here's an interesting thing. President Trump has said that Ukraine should get a new president, that Zelenskyy should leave office. And I understand Zelenskyy responded with a proposal of sorts.

SCHULTZ: That's right. Zelenskyy said if Trump gets Russian President Vladimir Putin to agree to a 60-day ceasefire, he, Zelenskyy, would work on getting the laws changed to hold elections in Ukraine. I asked former Estonian defense minister Hanno Pevkur what he thought of Trump's demand.

HANNO PEVKUR: Zelenskyy was very clear about it. He's not afraid of the elections. He's ready to do that, but Putin needs to keep the word. And we've seen throughout the history that Putin is never keeping the word.

SCHULTZ: So Pevkur thinks it's a long shot that this will happen.

INSKEEP: OK. So how are Europeans responding to this other development? The United States has very high diesel fuel prices, and the president suddenly announced an agreement of some sort for the U.S. to buy diesel fuel from Russia.

SCHULTZ: More of the same from the European perspective, but in the meantime, the European Union even intensified its efforts to stem the Kremlin's war effort. Now, it takes a long time to tee up sanctions, so this is not in reaction specifically to these latest U.S. moves, but the EU just imposed its largest-ever package of sanctions against almost 1,600 individuals and companies related to Russia's military industry. And while it appears that no one knew in advance about Trump's diesel deal, analyst Majda Ruge from the European Council on Foreign Relations says it wasn't exactly a surprise.

MAJDA RUGE: Trump's personal desire for economic reset with Russia and announcing business deals has really been the only consistent element of Trump's Russia-Ukraine policies.

INSKEEP: Let me come back to this security conference where you're at. What are NATO officials saying, the North Atlantic alliance?

SCHULTZ: Well, there's a lot of concern in Europe about U.S. plans to withdraw troops and military assets. So U.S. Air Force General Alexus Grynkewich - he's NATO's supreme allied commander Europe - used this conference to make sure there's no misunderstanding in Moscow that NATO's resolve is weakening. He unveiled what he called the theory of victory, describing how NATO would use its entire range of capabilities to respond to any attack.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

ALEXUS GRYNKEWICH: Our alliance exists to preserve peace, but let there be no doubt, should war come, we will block, blunt any attack. And then if it does not cease, we will break the forces conducting aggression against us. We're rehearsed, and we're ready.

SCHULTZ: And Grynkewich's speech comes as Europe is experiencing an increasing number of Russian threats, including sabotage, cyberattacks and disinformation. And that's in addition to the concerns about the U.S. commitment. So this speech was definitely meant to reassure allies that NATO's got things covered in all contingencies.

INSKEEP: Teri Schultz is reporting from Warsaw. Thanks so much.

SCHULTZ: You're welcome.
