Humans are losing the war on rats - not that we aren't trying. Many cities use poison and traps. Chicago and Boston are trying birth control. Washington, D.C., just launched its own rat contraceptive program. It is just not enough, though. Here in Washington, NPR's Ava Berger went out with a group teaching easier and less poisonous solutions.

AVA BERGER, BYLINE: Normally, it would be hard to organize 24 people who don't know each other to wander down a dark alleyway on a Wednesday night - not for the Rat Rangers.

HOLLY GERBERICH: All right, everyone, my name is Holly Gerberich.

BERGER: Holly Gerberich is an anthrozoologist and urban rat specialist.

GERBERICH: What I really want you to, like, reframe the conversation is, how do we make our environment less welcoming to rats? Because the reason that they're here is because we provide an ideal environment for them.

BOBBY CORRIGAN: It's like planting a land mine, you know, for wildlife.

ZAC WOOD: I mean, we're animal lovers. So, I mean, it's not just seeing, like, the foxes and the hawks and the owls and the animals that everyone loves suffer. I don't want the rats to suffer, either. And if there's a more humane solution, I always want to try that.

Let's pick up on that point and go further on this with Dana Stroul, who's with the Washington Institute for Near East Policy and served as deputy assistant secretary of defense for the Middle East during the Biden administration. Welcome to the program.

STROUL: Absolutely. So U.S. allies in the region, particularly Jordan and our Gulf countries who host U.S. military bases, have been attacked by Iran from the beginning of this war. So not only at those military bases, but then Iran expanded its retaliation by targeting civilian energy infrastructure, civilian hotels, desalination plants. And then, as Aya mentioned, we also had some of Iran's proxies, like the ones based in Iraq, targeting Saudi Arabia's oil infrastructure, that East-West pipeline.

INSKEEP: So U.S. allies, instead of being more secure because they have a U.S. base, are less secure?

STROUL: They are certainly suffering the consequences of Iran seeking to punish the region for the war that was started by Israel and the United States. And its support for terrorists all across the region have made and continue to make the region less safe.

INSKEEP: Oh, there was reporting early in the war that Saudi Arabia was supportive of the United States, even though Saudi Arabia was suffering. It sounds like you buy that line of reasoning by the Saudis.

STROUL: I think for the leadership of Saudi Arabia, what they need is stability in the Middle East in order to deliver economic growth and social reform for their country.
