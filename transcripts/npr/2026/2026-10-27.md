## Up First briefing: Midterm poll data; Voter fraud; Israel's elections

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)

MICHEL MARTIN, HOST:

Democrats maintain their edge in the race to control the House.

STEVE INSKEEP, HOST:

An NPR survey gives Democrats an 8-point lead among likely voters. But younger voters are not enthused. Does that leave the Senate out of reach?

MARTIN: I'm Michel Martin. That's Steve Inskeep, and this is UP FIRST from NPR News.

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)

MARTIN: President Trump routinely claims fraud in election after election. His allies never found widespread evidence. Now he's using the power of the federal government to amplify his claims. How are election officials preparing to respond?

INSKEEP: Also, Israelis vote in the first election since the October 7 attacks. Planeloads of people have flown home to cast their ballots. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu is under pressure. What coalition, if any, could replace him? Stay with us. We've got the news you need to start your day.

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)

INSKEEP: Election Day is one week away. A new NPR/PBS News/Marist poll is out this morning with insights into how likely voters are viewing this election.

MARTIN: Here to break down the numbers is NPR senior political editor and correspondent Domenico Montanaro. Domenico, welcome.

DOMENICO MONTANARO, BYLINE: Hey. Good morning, Michel.

MARTIN: So what does the poll say about where things stand?

MONTANARO: Well, it's a lot of what we've been seeing. You know, Democrats have an 8-point advantage among likely voters in this poll on what's known as the generic congressional ballot. That's when pollsters ask, who would you vote for if the election was held today in your district? Historically, that 8-point edge is a pretty big number that Democrats need to do well. President Trump continues to be unpopular with an approval rating among likely voters at 40%, which is actually a tick up or two from what it's been with registered voters. But it's not surprising when voters continue to say that the cost of living is their top issue, as they do in our poll. And Democrats also retain an enthusiasm advantage overall. Democrats are 10 points more likely than Republicans to say that they are, quote, "very enthusiastic" about voting.

MARTIN: Would you say that this puts Democrats in a strong position heading into election night?

MONTANARO: I mean, generally, yes, you know, with some caveats. You know, everyone would rightly be shocked if Democrats don't win the House at this point. You know, the polling error would have to be bigger than almost anything we've ever seen before. But there could be limits to how big those gains are that Democrats would make. And the Senate is genuinely a toss-up. When we look at the targeted seats in the house, 9 in 10 are in districts that Trump won. And in the Senate, remember, just because the landscape has shifted toward Democrats doesn't mean those toss-ups are definitely going to go their way at the end of the day. Toss-ups are toss-ups for a reason, after all. So Democrats could net a seat or two in the Senate, which would not be enough. They need four. Or they could win six or seven. Plus, again, Democrats are playing on red turf. All five Senate toss-up seats are in states that Trump won by double digits on average in 2024.

MARTIN: So Democrats have a solid outlook, but is there anything in the numbers on our poll that might give them some caution and Republicans some hope?

MONTANARO: Yeah. I mean, there is. I mean, when you look at key groups for each party that are very enthusiastic to vote, for example, some key Democratic pillar groups drop off. Young voters - just 42% say that they're very enthusiastic about voting. Independents who have soured on Trump - just 48% said that. Non-white voters - just 53%. All are lagging behind some more pro-Trump groups like MAGA voters, white women without college degrees and white evangelical voters.

That happens in midterms, where traditionally younger voters and Black and Latino voters turn out at disproportionately lower rates than in presidential elections. That said, there's been a political realignment in this country under Trump where college-educated whites have been siding with Democrats, and they show up at, you know, much larger numbers, proportionally, to vote. So midterms, even more so than presidential elections, are something of a volatile stew of uncertainty, really.

MARTIN: So we mentioned at the top that there's one week until Election Day, but we might be waiting for results, you know, longer than that, right? So did the poll get at that?

MONTANARO: Right. You know, patience is really going to be key here because the outcome of lots of races probably won't be known before people go to bed on election night. And there's nothing nefarious about that. That's just how vote counting goes, especially when we have so many close races in this country.

You know, we asked about trust in elections, and we found that large majorities of all political groups said that they thought their state and local governments would run a fair and accurate election. Republicans, though, were 20 points less likely to say so than Democrats, and they were also 20 points less likely than Democrats to believe that their candidate should definitely accept the results of the election. You know, that could lead to a huge mess if Trump again stokes the flames of false election conspiracies, as he did after the 2020 election that led eventually to January 6 and the violence at the Capitol.

There's also a lot in here in this poll on guns, immigration, government efficiency and AI that's all worth reading. All of it's posted to npr.org.

MARTIN: That is NPR senior political editor and correspondent Domenico Montanaro. Domenico, thank you.

MONTANARO: You're welcome.

INSKEEP: If Democrats should win Tuesday's voting, their next task will be securing the vote.

MARTIN: The Trump administration has used the federal government's power to highlight the president's constant claims of voter fraud. Today, a judge hears arguments over the administration's effort to force California to set aside some ballots. The administration wants that treatment for people it claims are likely not citizens. An appeals court last night declined to get involved in that case.

INSKEEP: NPR congressional reporter Eric McDaniel is here in Studio 31 to give us an update on how Democrats are preparing to respond to all this. Eric, good morning.

ERIC MCDANIEL, BYLINE: Good morning. Good morning.

INSKEEP: OK. What do the preparations look like?

MCDANIEL: Well, I'll just say, for Democrats and this constellation of good governance and nonpartisan groups, the election disruption effort is ongoing, right? They point to things like a million-dollar bounty offered by President Trump yesterday for election fraud tips that lead to a conviction, though it's unclear how or if that would be paid, Department of Justice lawsuits in Alaska and California, like you mentioned, the failed effort by the administration to restrict mail-in voting. It's all been the subject of legal fights and will be.

Next, these folks are worried about things like a federal election emergency declaration, which is something that doesn't exist in law, troops at the polls - illegal - or the president saying without providing any evidence that a foreign enemy has hacked voting machines. So they've been trying to link up with local election officials, pre-write legal filings and get all of their talking points ready to go out and say the truth, which is that people should feel comfortable casting their ballots.

INSKEEP: OK. Understand that. You just mentioned a number of things that the president might say or do that would be illegal if he said or did them. What is the pattern about how the president talks about elections?

MCDANIEL: Well, I mean, I think you've mentioned this. The president's claimed fraud in every election he's been involved with. Just for the benefit of folks who are too young to remember, in 2016, Trump said he'd won the popular vote - he just won the Electoral College - when you discount millions of illegal votes. In 2018, he claimed there was electoral fraud in states like Arizona. The January 6 insurrection was about fraud claims in the 2020 presidential election. He told CNN that there was rigging going on in 2024. It goes on and on.

Last week, I talked to Representative Jim Himes. He's a Democrat on the House Intelligence Committee, the ranking member there. He reminded me that he was actually in the Capitol during the January 6 insurrection and said it would be irresponsible for him and other Democrats not to be ready to combat fraud efforts.

INSKEEP: And I guess we should just mention once again, even the president's allies have never been able to substantiate the various claims of voter fraud in 2020 and other elections. But how does the White House describe what it's doing this time around?

MCDANIEL: Well, they say all of their work is to secure elections, which I should point out election experts say is - are already secure. Like Domenico mentioned, folks trust elections and election officials. I spent a good amount of time, though, this year reporting on the effort to get the SAVE America Act passed, which the White House claimed would protect the election by restricting mail-in voting and requiring folks to show proof of their citizenship status in order to stay registered to vote. It failed due to a lack of Republican support in the Senate. Earlier this month, a reporter actually pressed the president on if he would honor the results. Listen to this.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

PRESIDENT DONALD TRUMP: What do you think the answer is to that?

UNIDENTIFIED REPORTER: I don't know, Mr. President.

TRUMP: What do you think (ph)?

UNIDENTIFIED REPORTER: I'm asking you.

TRUMP: Of course I will. But we don't want cheating.

MCDANIEL: And he went on to talk about - I don't want cheating. I don't want cheating - in an extended back-and-forth, which is part and parcel for how he's talked about this the whole time. And again, just to make it really explicit, there has never been credible evidence of voter fraud anywhere near the level to impact results of elections in the United States.

INSKEEP: NPR congressional reporter Eric McDaniel, thanks so much.

MCDANIEL: Thanks, Steve.

RUTH SHERLOCK, BYLINE: Hi, Steve. I'm at a polling station near Tel Aviv. And, you know, we're seeing that the sense is that this is a really vital election for Israelis. We're expecting a very high voter turnout, well over 70% of the voting-age population. And Israelis abroad are flying in to vote for what polls suggest is going to be a very close race. At this polling station, we're meeting people like Itamar Ariel (ph), who supports Netanyahu.

ITAMAR ARIEL: Bibi Netanyahu - the best. (Speaking Hebrew).

SHERLOCK: He says, yes, there are problems in Israel, but nobody can replace Netanyahu. He says for him, this is about security for Israel.

But here's the thing, Steve. In the country, there are many people here who believe that it's for the security failures that Netanyahu should be voted out. They want to see a full investigation into exactly how the October 7 Hamas attacks in Israel could happen, something Netanyahu has blocked. And they're tired of the wars he's championing. Netanyahu posted an AI-generated image at the weekend of an atomic bomb exploding over Tel Aviv to remind Israelis of what he sees as the nuclear threat from Iran. One man at the polling station here said - he called that sick, saying that he thinks Netanyahu was trying to win votes by scaring Israelis.

INSKEEP: I want to underline this, though, Ruth. There's clearly a lot of Israelis who don't like the prime minister, yet he's won election after election after election. He finally was pushed out. He got his way back in. So what are the options for Israelis who don't support him?

SHERLOCK: Well, there are many parties running, but his primary challenger is Gadi Eisenkot. He's a former military chief of staff who's actually been part of Netanyahu's war cabinet. One of his sons and two of his nephews died in the war in Gaza. He now leads a center-right opposition, and that's promising more diplomacy, less extremism, an end to this Israeli - increasing isolation of Israel abroad. We spoke with Ido and Itay Ohev Ami (ph), a young man and his grandfather. Itay was voting for the first time. And they both support Eisenkot for change.

It's also here about domestic questions, like whether Orthodox Jews should serve in the military.

INSKEEP: Yeah. And I heard you mention cost of living, which is something that Americans are thinking about here in our election. So what's the timeline? When do we find out who won?

SHERLOCK: We should have a clearer picture by Wednesday morning who - which side has the most seats in parliament, but the vote count can continue for days. And we won't know who the prime minister is because that will be determined by the parties in days and possible weeks of negotiation.

## What Trump’s Mississippi rally says about the midterms

SCOTT DETROW, HOST:

President Trump kicks off his final week of campaign travel before the midterms today in Mississippi. The deeply conservative state has a U.S. Senate race that has seen millions of dollars in last-minute spending, as NPR's Stephen Fowler explains.

STEPHEN FOWLER, BYLINE: On paper, Mississippi seems like the perfect place for an upset in this midterm environment. It has the highest share of Black voters in the country. It's rural, agriculture heavy and one of the poorest states, so higher prices are felt harder. But President Trump won the state by 23 points in 2024, and it's extremely racially polarized.

SCOTT COLOM: The reality is Mississippi's never been a lost cause. We've been a betrayed cause. The Democratic Party has not been trying to win Mississippi. Let's stop kidding ourselves, right? They spent hundreds of millions of dollars in other states. They've spent almost nothing in Mississippi.

FOWLER: That's Scott Colom, the Democratic U.S. Senate nominee. He's a district attorney and challenging incumbent Republican Cindy Hyde-Smith, who blocked his nomination to be a federal judge during the Biden administration. In recent weeks, Democrats and Republicans have spent millions of dollars in Mississippi as polling shows a pathway where Democratic enthusiasm, elevated Black turnout and fewer Republican base voters makes deep red races deeply competitive this cycle, and the general vibe of voters feeling down about those in power, regardless of party.

CELESTINE RICE: Before, we didn't realize how bad it was, but now it's obvious. So, yeah.

FOWLER: What's bad?

RICE: Everything, everything - the moral compass of this country is gone.

FOWLER: At a rally Thursday night in Columbus for Colom, Celestine Rice took a break from dancing to the pre-speech music to share her thoughts.

RICE: Now is the time, I think, that people are starting to realize that. They're starting to realize that, hey, big money - it doesn't trickle down like they said.

FOWLER: The Democratic money and manpower is driven by a growing number of those who argue the party's future runs through places like Mississippi. That includes former Vice President Kamala Harris, who headlined that rally, along with figures like Georgia Senator Raphael Warnock and former transportation secretary Pete Buttigieg. The Republican reaction has highlighted the investment and attention from national Democrats as proof Colom is too extreme for Mississippi and have spent heavily on ads saying as much like this one from Hyde-Smith.

(SOUNDBITE OF POLITICAL AD)

UNIDENTIFIED NARRATOR: The most radical Senate candidate in our lifetime - Scott Colom, twice nominated by Biden, beholden to the far left. As DA...

FOWLER: But Democrats have spent even more in recent weeks with a message that there's no daylight between the unpopular president and downballot Republicans. Colom thinks voters agree.

COLOM: Yeah, I mean, the reality is if somebody's doing a good job, it's hard to beat them, right? I mean, if Cindy Hyde-Smith was focused on the voters, you know, it'd be tough.

FOWLER: The race carries weight beyond Mississippi or even control of the Senate. It's become a stand-in for conversations about the power of Black voters in the South. Many Southern Republican-led states have rushed to redraw congressional maps that remove opportunities for Black voters after a Supreme Court ruling earlier this year. In Mississippi, Colom, who is Black, said it could go further.

COLOM: It could go to school boards, go to judicial elections. I mean, we elect a lot of positions in Mississippi. And so what that tells us is you're trying to, you know, take away power from the voters and give it to the leaders. That's not how democracy is supposed to work. The voters have to pick the leaders, not the leaders pick their voters.

FOWLER: In the statewide race that can't be gerrymandered and in an environment favorable to Democrats, history and math still prove to be a challenge. The last Democratic senator from Mississippi came in the early '80s. The last successful Black statewide candidate was during Reconstruction. In the 2018 blue wave, Cindy Hyde-Smith won a special election for the seat by roughly seven points. Still...

ASHLEY KENNEDY SHELTON: I think that you are going to see voters truly show up because folks are just tired. Like, this doesn't work, and it hasn't been working for quite some time. And I think you're going to see it at the ballot box.

FOWLER: Ashley Kennedy Shelton runs a nonpartisan civic engagement group in Louisiana and said she's hearing from people of all stripes who say things are bad, and it's time for a change. That feeling, plus a notable surge in Black voter turnout in the primaries and in early voting so far, is one reason why President Trump is holding a rally in the northeast corner of Mississippi in the final week of the election. Stephen Fowler, NPR News, Columbus, Mississippi.
