What does his leadership purge mean for a military already seven months into a war with Iran? Stay with us. We'll give you news you need to start your day.

MARTIN: Passengers on a flight from Dubai to Tel Aviv are describing what happened on board a Flydubai airplane that caused them to make an emergency landing in Saudi Arabia.

MARTÍNEZ: The plane signaled a distress call on Wednesday after one of the pilots apparently stabbed the other pilot and seemed to be trying to crash the plane with everyone on board. Many of the passengers were Israelis, and officials in Israel are calling it an attempted terrorist attack, although the motive is still unknown.

MARTIN: NPR's Kat Lonsdorf is with us now from Tel Aviv. Good morning, Kat.

KAT LONSDORF, BYLINE: Hey. Good morning.

MARTIN: It sounds like a really harrowing situation on that plane. What can you tell us?

LONSDORF: So, yeah, like you said, the plane was operated by Flydubai. That's an airline based in the UAE. There's still a lot we don't know, but Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu put out a statement after speaking with some of the passengers, who told him that the plane went into a spin and began to plunge as one of the pilots was trying to sabotage the aircraft systems after stabbing his copilot. He said some passengers sprang into action and were able to enter the cockpit after the pilot who was stabbed unlocked the cockpit door during the attack. Those passengers managed to subdue the attacker. Israeli officials released a video of Netanyahu talking to one of those passengers, a man named Yaniv Hayun (ph), once he landed back in Israel. Hayun's clothes were still covered in blood in the video.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

YANIV HAYUN: (Non-English language spoken).

LONSDORF: He described how he put the attacker in a chokehold and pulled the plane's controls up to try to right it. Hayun says he's a plumber, but he thought to do that after seeing it in a TV show. He also said that there happened to be another flight crew on the plane who jumped in to help land it.

MARTIN: That's incredible.

LONSDORF: Yeah.

MARTIN: You were at the airport in Tel Aviv last night as the passengers arrived there. What was that like?

LONSDORF: Yeah. It was really hectic. The arrivals area was filled with journalists and family members. The passengers slowly emerged, dragging their suitcases behind them. Some were crying, some smiling, looking relieved, and some just looked really tired. There were also quite a few families with children.

I talked to one passenger named Dan Zoler (ph). He was traveling with his wife and two small children on the flight. He confirmed a lot of what Netanyahu said - that he saw three other passengers throw themselves on that copilot who was attacking his colleague and capture him. And he said he was trying to just distract his son during the incident, to tell him that what was happening wasn't normal.

DAN ZOLER: I told him that it's something that happened, like, once in 25 years, that I want him to be calm. And we are happy to come back.

LONSDORF: That seemed like the overwhelming feeling from people - just happy to have two feet back on solid ground.

MARTIN: Yeah. I hear people, I think, singing in the background, I think...

LONSDORF: Yeah.

MARTIN: ...Sort of to welcome people back. Do we know anything about the copilot who carried out this attack?

LONSDORF: We don't know much. Both the pilot and the copilot are alive. They're being interviewed by investigators in Saudi Arabia. The UAE has also opened an investigation, and Netanyahu has said Israel will be involved. The pilot who was stabbed is from India. Both Netanyahu and India's prime minister have commended him. And the copilot who did the attacking is from Oman. It's still unclear if he was acting alone.

MARTIN: And what's been the response in Israel to all of this?

LONSDORF: This is the top story here, with the four passengers who entered the cockpit being hailed as heroes who saved the lives of 175 people on board, according to Israeli officials. You know, it comes at a particularly political time here in Israel, as national elections, which will determine whether Netanyahu remains prime minister, are less than a month away. He's been campaigning on this idea that he alone can protect Israelis from the threats that Israel faces, so this incident on the plane will almost certainly be a talking point in the coming weeks.

MARTIN: That is NPR's Kat Lonsdorf in Tel Aviv. Kat, thank you.

LONSDORF: Thank you.

MARTIN: NPR White House correspondent Franco Ordoñez will be traveling with the president, but he's stopped by to talk to us first. Good morning, Franco.

FRANCO ORDOÑEZ, BYLINE: How are you?

ORDOÑEZ: Well, I mean, in some ways, it's just likely knocking some rust off and workshopping his campaign speech in front of a more friendly environment before heading to other states. I mean, it's certainly a place where he can get an enthusiastic response to this week's announcements for, for example, a $15 billion steel plant being built in Iowa, as well as a $54 billion investment by South Korea into a project to export natural gas from Alaska. And both of those are battleground states.

Now, Alex Conant, a Republican strategist who worked in the George W. Bush White House, told me that the trip to Oklahoma and then actually to Alabama next, you know, reminds him of the 2006 midterms when Bush campaigned almost exclusively in red states.

ALEX CONANT: You can't go to a district or a state where there's just as many Democrats as Republicans or where there's a lot of independent voters because you're going to do more harm than good when your favorability numbers are this low.

ORDOÑEZ: And that's because in a swing state, Trump's as likely, and perhaps more likely, to stir up Democrats and independent voters who are not happy with him to go out and vote against Republicans.

MARTIN: Now, we mentioned that Oklahoma is comfortably red. But could this also be about making sure it stays that way, I mean, sort of stabilizing the base, as it were?

ORDOÑEZ: Yeah, for sure. I mean, Trump endorsed the Republican gubernatorial candidate Mike Mazzei, for example, and he barely won his primary. This is partly making sure that his supporters show up - Trump supporters. The challenge that Republicans have long had - and we've talked about this - is turning out low-propensity voters when Trump is not on the ballot. And this trip to Oklahoma and Alabama is part of an effort to make sure those voters do come out next month for the midterms.

MARTIN: But Trump's message to the base has been - you know, we've heard him say this explicitly - to pretend that he is on the ballot. But isn't that a risky message, considering how poorly he's doing in the polls?

ORDOÑEZ: Yeah, but I'm not really sure Republicans have much of a choice. I mean, there is no question that some Republicans are trying to distance themselves, as we saw many skip the midterm convention. But I was talking with Jon McHenry, a veteran Republican pollster, and he told me that Trump is so tied to the Republican Party right now that lawmakers really have to tread carefully.

JON MCHENRY: The thing that we've learned over the last couple years is that it's impossible to run away from the president. This president has a way of throwing his arms around you. And you can't turn too far away from President Trump without turning off his voters.

MARTIN: So what does the White House or the Republican National Committee, which is helping to lead the campaign - what do they have to say about this?

ORDOÑEZ: Yeah. An RNC official who asked for anonymity because they're not authorized to speak publicly told me the president is holding his rally in Durant, Oklahoma, at least in part because it's also near the Texas border and the Dallas-Fort Worth media market. The idea is to help turn out base voters in Texas, as well. Now, that goes for Alabama, also. The visit to Mobile, Alabama, is near Florida's panhandle. And they assured me that Trump will later visit battleground states like Florida, like Texas, Ohio and also Michigan.

MARTIN: That is White House correspondent Franco Ordoñez. Franco, thank you.

ORDOÑEZ: Thank you, Michel.

MARTÍNEZ: Yeah. That is what he told hundreds of junior military leaders in a speech Wednesday at Quantico in Virginia. Leaning into culture war flash points, Hegseth says he has revived a warrior ethos in the military.

PETE HEGSETH: The ideological clowns are out. The patriotic cowboys are in, with testosterone testing on top.

MARTIN: So what exactly does Hegseth want to do with the military leadership?

LAWRENCE: Well, he says he's cut 10% of these flag officers already and he would like to try to reach 20%. And he smiled when he said in the past year, these generals or admirals who disagreed with him aren't there anymore. It's pretty unusual. Hegseth has been reaching way down into the promotion list to prevent some colonels from becoming generals and just not giving any explanation.

MARTIN: You know, some of these generals were highly regarded. I mean, they had vocal support from Democrats and Republicans. And so this has been controversial. So is it the number of positions he's cutting that critics object to or who he seems to be going after?

LAWRENCE: No. I mean, a lot of people agree that there might be some bloat at the top of the military ranks, but it's these inferred reasons or lack of any explanation for the names that he is cutting - Air Force General CQ Brown, Army generals Randy George and Chris Donahue, who were widely considered among the best. And he's fired a number of senior Black and female officers, and Hegseth has implied in the past that women or non-white generals are DEI hires. He said in his speech that recruitment is up, although that often has a lot to do with the economy as much as anything else.

MARTIN: Did he have anything else to say about the war with Iran?

LAWRENCE: Well, he said Iran's conventional forces are destroyed, and so is their nuclear program. He spent just as much time railing against the press corps for reporting things like low morale on these long deployments to Iran and damage to bases overseas, depleted munition stocks. Here's what he said.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

HEGSETH: A free press is, of course, important. But partisan activism that intentionally smears the troops and the historic mission that they're undertaking is fundamentally anti-American. It's sick. It's a national betrayal. It's treason, actually.

LAWRENCE: It's worth noting that the Pentagon hasn't had a news briefing since May, which is also very unusual. Nothing like this during 20 years of sometimes very intense war in Iraq and Afghanistan. And it's also unusual, though I think we're getting used to it, just how partisan this speech was to a military that really prides itself on avoiding politics.

MARTIN: And I'll just note here that he seems to be objecting to reporting that is true. Did he have anything to say about actual Pentagon policy?

LAWRENCE: Yes. He said he's going to elevate the Chaplain Corps, and he said that faith was a core strength of the U.S. military. And this week, he also was announcing that he's going to be cutting civilian tenure-track jobs at the U.S. military academies, all within his idea of focusing on lethality. But he also announced a new relationship with five colleges, including one historically Black college, to help promote military enlistment.

But the biggest news is the creation of a four-star command of autonomous warfare, essentially a drone command, and a project to study innovations on the battlefield, much of which we've seen in Ukraine, and also an effort to disrupt and supercharge the Pentagon's really glacial procurement process. And that is going to involve a couple of names you'll recognize - Newt Gingrich, the former House speaker, and then two tech billionaires, Palmer Luckey and Elon Musk, who led the president's project DOGE to reform U.S. government spending last year.

MARTIN: That is NPR's Quil Lawrence. Quil, thank you.

LAWRENCE: Thank you.

## Israel's PM says a pilot tried to crash a plane traveling to Tel Aviv

JUANA SUMMERS, HOST:

A commercial flight from Dubai to Tel Aviv made an emergency landing in Saudi Arabia this morning after one of the pilots allegedly stabbed the other pilot and was apparently trying to crash the plane with everyone on board. The plane was carrying many Israeli passengers who now have arrived safely back in Israel. Officials there are calling it an attempted terrorist attack. NPR's Kat Lonsdorf is in Tel Aviv and joins us now. Hi there.

KAT LONSDORF, BYLINE: Hey.

SUMMERS: Kat, what do we know so far?

LONSDORF: Yeah. It's been a very dramatic day here, as we've all been trying to figure out more information about what exactly happened on this flight. This was a Flydubai plane with many Israeli passengers on board, like you said, and it made an emergency landing at the Tabuk Airport, which is near the border with Jordan in Saudi Arabia. Saudi authorities said they received a distress call from the flight before it landed and then said both the pilot and copilot were taken to the hospital for injuries sustained on board once it did land. All passengers were confirmed to be safe and accounted for.

There's still a lot we don't know, but Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu put out a video statement saying he spoke with one of the passengers who told him the plane went into a spin and began to plunge as one of the pilots was trying to sabotage the aircraft systems after stabbing his copilot. Flydubai, the airline, confirmed that the plane landed after a, quote, "altercation" occurred in the flight deck.

Netanyahu said that there happened to be another flight crew on the plane who managed to stabilize the situation and then land it. He said the attacker is being questioned now by Saudi authorities, and Saudi officials have confirmed that they have opened an investigation into the whole thing.

SUMMERS: Kat, I know it's still early, but any sense yet about the motive behind this?

LONSDORF: Well, Israeli officials are calling this an attempted terrorist attack, but a statement put out by Flydubai urged caution on that, saying that the motives behind the incident are unknown and remain subject to a formal investigation. This all comes at a particularly political time here in Israel, as national elections are less than a month away. Those elections will determine whether Netanyahu remains prime minister. He's been campaigning on this idea that he alone can protect Israelis from the threats that Israel faces. So this incident on the plane will almost certainly be a talking point in the coming weeks as that election grows closer.

SUMMERS: Kat, I know that you were at the airport today as the passengers finally arrived in Israel. Tell us what you heard and saw.

LONSDORF: Well, it was really hectic. The arrivals area was filled with journalists waiting for the passengers to arrive, and there were also family members there waiting, some with, you know, balloons or flowers to welcome their loved ones back after such a scary experience. The passengers were flown into Tel Aviv from Saudi Arabia on a different Flydubai flight, and they arrived many hours after their original scheduled landing.

The passengers slowly emerged from the arrivals door, dragging suitcases behind them. You know, some were crying, some were smiling, some were looking really relieved, and others were just looking really tired. There were also quite a few families. You know, these flights between Israel and Dubai happen several times a day. It's a main connecting flight for people going on vacation.

I talked to one man named Dan Zoler. He was traveling with his wife and two small children on the flight, and he told me he saw three of the other passengers throw themselves on the copilot who was attacking his colleague and capture him. He said he was trying to distract his young son throughout the whole ordeal, telling him that this is not normal.

DAN ZOLER: I told him that it's something that happened like once in 25 years, that I want him to be calm. And we are happy to come back.

LONSDORF: That seemed like the overwhelming feeling from people, just happy to have two feet back on solid ground.

SUMMERS: Yeah. I'm sure - just a lot of relief. Kat, do we know what happens next?

LONSDORF: Well, it's an ongoing investigation, and there's still so much we don't know about motive, about what exactly happened on the plane. And those details should hopefully become clearer in the coming days.

SUMMERS: NPR's Kat Lonsdorf in Tel Aviv, thanks.

LONSDORF: Thank you.
