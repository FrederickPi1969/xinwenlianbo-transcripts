## It's Election Day. Here's what to watch

AILSA CHANG, HOST:

There's a cliche in politics that the only poll that matters is the one on Election Day. Well, that day is finally here. And we're about to get a whole lot of answers, real answers based on actual votes cast. Here to talk us through what to expect as the results come in are NPR senior political correspondents Tamara Keith and Mara Liasson. Hey to both of you.

TAMARA KEITH, BYLINE: Hello.

MARA LIASSON, BYLINE: Hi there.

CHANG: Hello. OK. So we're finally here, but I do want to skip forward for a moment to talk about how NPR declares who wins. Now, we rely on the Associated Press to make race calls. Tell us how this works.

KEITH: Yeah. The AP will have more than 5,000 journalists working before and after polls close, gathering returns from every state, county, city, township and parish. It is a massive journalistic undertaking.

CHANG: Yes.

KEITH: And they supplement reports from the field with data delivered by elections officials through their websites and other means. We had David Scott, who runs AP elections, on the NPR Politics Podcast today, and he said they used the term race call instead of calling it a projection because their standard is that when they call a race, it is certain that the candidate has won.

DAVID SCOTT: The certainty required for us is that word. It's certainty. We are certain, based on all the data on all the evidence that we are looking at, that the trailing candidates can't catch up.

KEITH: And they've been quite accurate. He told us that of 12,000 races they've called this year in primaries and other elections, they only got four of the calls wrong.

CHANG: Wow.

KEITH: So they aren't always first, but they always want to be right, and that is also NPR's philosophy.

CHANG: Yes. We value accuracy utmost. Mara, what are the top questions for you about tonight that you hope to get answered?

LIASSON: Well, addition to the results and finding out whether the Democrats take the House and/or the Senate, and if they do, by what kind of margin, I think tonight is going to answer a handful of questions. No. 1, were the polls wrong? If so, how? No. 2, which is more powerful - the structural advantages that Republicans had, like partisan gerrymandering and tremendous amounts of money, or the fundamentals, the fact that the incumbent is so unpopular? Donald Trump is at about 38% approval rating. That is the bare metal base (ph) of the Republican Party. His two most important initiatives are both extremely unpopular - tariffs and the war in Iran.

And we're going to find out, in addition to that, whether Black Americans turn out in massive numbers and help Democrats. We're going to find out whether noncollege white voters stay home and hurt Republicans or turn out and help them. Right now, white noncollege voters' approval rating of Donald Trump is around 50%. That is very low for your most loyal demographic.

CHANG: And, Tam, what about you? What are you watching most closely tonight?

KEITH: Well, everything Mara mentioned, of course. But in particular, I'll be looking at those districts that were redrawn as part of the president's push for mid-decade redistricting. They've already proven to be some of the most competitive and expensive House races this cycle. So how do these races turn out, and will it have been worth it for Republicans in the end?

I'm also watching some key races where there are more left-leaning, populist progressive candidates running. Some are explicitly associated with the Democratic Socialists of America, or DSA. Others are not. We know that they are going to win in bright blue, heavily Democratic districts in New York City and Philadelphia. But what about Michigan's 7th Congressional District, which is a swing district in a swing state, or California's 22nd Congressional District in the Central Valley of California, where I grew up, which is a very narrowly divided district.

CHANG: And a few states - I'm talking about, like, Florida and South Carolina - they're going to be reporting early in the night, of course, because of where they're geographically located. So they could give us a hint of how the night is going to go, right, Mara?

LIASSON: That's right. You don't have to stay up all night. You can get a pretty good indication of whether it's going to be a good or great night for Democrats from these early states. Now, in Florida, Democrats might have a hard time winning the Senate race and the gubernatorial race there, but if they keep those margins very small, that could mean a good night for Democrats. In South Carolina, another really red state, if the Republican, Darline Graham, who was Trump's handpicked successor to her brother, the late Lindsey Graham, if she does poorly or even loses, that tells you it's going to be a very rough night for Republicans.

KEITH: And I will just add about Florida, that is one of those states that redrew its lines to favor Republicans.

CHANG: Right.

KEITH: So there are some key races, including Florida 14 and Florida 25, where the Democratic incumbents in those districts are running in places where President Trump won by 10 points two years ago. If they win there, that gives us a strong sense of how the night is going for the parties.

LIASSON: And if that happens, that would be an example of a gerrymander that backfires and turns into a dummymander (ph).

CHANG: A dummymander, OK, good word. All right, after Florida, after South Carolina, what are the states to watch for?

LIASSON: Well, polls close in Ohio at 7:30. They close in Texas at 8. Those are two marquee Senate races. If Democrats have any hope of taking the majority in the Senate, they need to win at least one or both of those. Texas is one of two states, along with North Carolina, where many Republicans feel Donald Trump made their lives a lot harder. He forced both Republican Senate incumbents out of the running in those states. He threatened Thom Tillis in North Carolina with a primary. He defeated John Cornyn of Texas with the primary.

If he hadn't decided to go after the Republican incumbents in both those states because he felt they weren't loyal enough to him, we might not be talking tonight about Democrats taking the Senate. And that tells you how much this election has been about Donald Trump, about his whims, his grievances and his desires.

CHANG: Can we talk about that real quick? Because midterms usually are seen as referendums on the president. What have the dynamics been like when it comes to President Trump?

KEITH: The president and his political team had this novel theory that his base of voters is only excited by him, so they had to convince Republicans to vote as if he was on the ballot. The question tonight is whether everyone is going to vote as if Trump was on the ballot and whether that's a good thing or not.

LIASSON: You know, Donald Trump is a turnout machine for both parties.

CHANG: That is NPR's Mara Liasson and Tamara Keith. Thank you to both of you so much.

KEITH: You're welcome.

## Japan faces diplomatic dilemma amid joint pressure from nuclear neighbors

AILSA CHANG, HOST:

And now to Russia's war in Ukraine. This war has raised tensions not only in Europe but also in Asia. Ties between Russia and neighboring Japan are at their worst in decades. Tokyo would like more support from Washington, but as NPR's Anthony Kuhn reports, some Japanese people are concerned they may not get it.

ANTHONY KUHN, BYLINE: In a video released by Russia's federal security bureau, FSB agents charge into a hotel room on Sakhalin Island in Russia's Far East on October 13.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

UNIDENTIFIED FSB AGENT: (Non-English language spoken).

YUKI IMAI: (Non-English language spoken).

KUHN: The agents pin Japanese journalist Yuki Imai to the ground and handcuff him. They question him and take him to Moscow to face trial on charges of espionage. Japan's government denies the charges. The FSB says Imai was reporting on soldiers from Russia's Far East who were deployed to Ukraine. Shinji Hyodo, a Russia expert at the National Institute for Defense Studies in Tokyo, argues that the journalist was just doing his job.

SHINJI HYODO: (Through interpreter) I believe the Russian side intends to use this as a political bargaining chip to exert pressure on Japan.

KUHN: Hyodo says that underlying the current tensions are Japan's sanctions on Russia for its invasion of Ukraine. Russia now sees Japan as hostile, and bilateral relations have gone into a tailspin.

HYODO: (Through interpreter) I think it's fair to say that Japan is currently facing its greatest diplomatic challenge since the end of the war. This is no longer just a bilateral issue between Japan and Russia.

KUHN: There's also what Hyodo calls the China factor. China has been putting pressure on Japan since last year, when Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi said that a Chinese blockade of Taiwan could justify a Japanese military response. Russia relies on China's support in its war on Ukraine, and it's joined China in pressuring Japan. Another factor is a long-running territorial dispute between Russia and Japan.

(SOUNDBITE OF FACTORY MACHINERY)

KUHN: In August, Russian President Vladimir Putin visited a seafood factory, hospital and school on his first visit to one of four disputed islands called the Southern Kurils by Russia and the Northern Territories by Japan. The Soviet Union seized the islands at the end of World War II. They never signed a permanent peace treaty because of the dispute and because the U.S. warned them not to. This episode was called the Dulles intervention. James Brown, an expert on Japan-Russia relations at Temple University in Tokyo, explains.

JAMES BROWN: This was the suggestion from John Foster Dulles, the secretary of state, in 1956 that if Japan were to make an agreement with the Soviet Union, settling the territorial dispute, well, then perhaps the United States would remain forevermore in holding, administering Okinawa.

KUHN: So the Soviet Union kept the islands, and the U.S. returned to Japan the island of Okinawa, which remains a key U.S. military hub in Asia. Brown says Japan's media generally don't mention the U.S.'s role in the territorial dispute so as not to offend its only ally as it faces three nuclear powers - Russia, China and North Korea.

BROWN: Japan's situation is essentially they have no Plan B, which means that they have to rely on the United States. They have to flatter the Trump administration. They have to do everything possible to avoid abandonment.

KUHN: The U.S. has criticized Russia's arrest of the Japanese journalist, but Shinji Hyodo says that the U.S. generally doesn't seem very interested in Japan's conflict with Russia. And he says Japan is concerned about the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen, China, this month.

HYODO: (Through interpreter) There is a possibility that a trilateral summit involving the leaders of the U.S., China and Russia could take place, potentially leading to the U.S. being swayed toward the positions held by China and Russia.

KUHN: It's unclear whether Japan's Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi will meet with either Russian or Chinese leaders at the summit. Anthony Kuhn, NPR News, Seoul.

## Chicago entrepreneur turns empty downtown office space into vertical gardens

AL ORENDORFF, BYLINE: I'm standing outside one of downtown Chicago's office towers. Ever since the post-COVID world made a return to the office possible, cities like Chicago have recovered some of their empty office space, but not all.

RUSSELL STEINBERG: There's a line of sight on solving some really complicated problems, three of them specifically - office vacancy, public health crisis and food system challenges.

ORENDORFF: That's Russell Steinberg, founder and CEO of FARMZER0. His company uses empty downtown office space to grow edible greens that he then delivers to restaurants, caterers, other office buildings and folks who live in nearby high-rises. The only prerequisite is vacant space. He says there's about 45 million square feet of that in Chicago.

STEINBERG: The amount of square footage that we would need to convert to feed everyone in the entire city of Chicago the adequate vegetables is, like, 2 million square feet out of the 45 million.

ORENDORFF: And while there's always water running to keep the plants hydrated, it's not as much water as you might think. Steinberg says less than what might be used in a fully functioning office. ## How painter Maira Kalman finds 'Joy' in darkness

AILSA CHANG, HOST:

Joy has been a frequent subject in Maira Kalman's long painting career - joy in the form of cozy chairs, gardens in full bloom or impossibly fluffy dogs. And yet the 77-year-old artist thought that a book dedicated to joy would be a tough sell.

MAIRA KALMAN: I thought I'm going to have to explain myself to people and explain that I'm really miserable a lot of the time. And then once in a while, I feel joy.

CHANG: Her book, which is aptly titled "Joy," is one of more than 30 that Kalman has published. It explores why we can be so quick to dismiss the light that appears with the darkness. NPR's Elena Burnett has more.

ELENA BURNETT, BYLINE: For Kalman, Joy begins early in the morning with a cup of coffee and the daily obituaries.

KALMAN: It gives you a sense of proportion. This will end, so take advantage and do as much as you can that you love to do.

BURNETT: And that means leisurely strolling through her Manhattan neighborhood and looking for inspiration.

KALMAN: The only criteria is if my heart stops, which - not for too long, hopefully. But if it kind of puts a ping in me. And it's all instinct. It's all feeling, and it's all emotion.

BURNETT: Her book, "Joy," is filled with many of those heart-stopping observations, rendered in vivid gouache with her trademark whimsy. There's a woman applying crimson lipstick at a bright Paris restaurant and another surrounded by four small dogs and florals in her living room. There's a man playing a violin under a spring-green cherry tree and another listening to a record, surrounded by the pink and orange rubble of a war-torn apartment. And on the cover, a serene lake of green is interrupted by a pink shoreline and a small figure in a red bathing suit coming up for air mid-swim. That figure is based on Kalman's cousin, Iris, who swam the English Channel.

KALMAN: I don't know how much joy she was having at the time. Probably not much. But joy arrives unexpectedly and probably when you're alone and probably in the smallest of moments.

BURNETT: And just how unexpected Joy can be is clear from Page 1, a blank page with the word joy followed by a question mark. In fact, the majority of the writing that accompanies Kalman's paintings are questions.

## Legendary photographer on getting that perfect election night picture

MARY LOUISE KELLY, HOST:

This is ALL THINGS CONSIDERED from NPR News. I'm Mary Louise Kelly.

SCOTT DETROW, HOST:

And I'm Scott Detrow. Tonight, all over the country, candidates will be gathering with their supporters, their families and their staffs to see whether they won or lost. When those results come in, there will be joy, there will be sadness, there will be shock, there will be disappointment. And all of those human responses to the results, well, they can make great photographs. Tonight, photographers will also be in position ready to snap these history-making moments.

That is something Pulitzer Prize-winning photographer David Hume Kennerly has done for much of the past six decades. And many of those election nights, as well as a whole lot else, are collected in Kennerly's new book, "In The Room: 60 Years On The Front Lines Of History." Kennerly covered Vietnam. He covered Ali v. Frazier. He also covered the 2000 presidential campaign between Texas Governor George W. Bush and Vice President Al Gore.

And I want to talk about one particular moment. Bush has thought he's won this election. Al Gore has called him to concede. You take several pictures of him preparing his victory speech. And then, suddenly, Al Gore calls. He retracts the concession. Tell me what happens next.

DAVID HUME KENNERLY: (Laughter) This is - you're describing what makes for really good pictures in my world. I will back it up a little bit from there. I had come over with Dick Cheney, and I'd covered the last, I think, week or two of the campaign with Cheney. So we went over to the governor's mansion when it looks like he was going to win, or they declared him the winner, and everything was great. There were a couple other photographers in there. And he got the phone call from Gore, but he took it upstairs. So he left. I didn't see him do that. But I went into the kitchen to get a drink of water, and Bush came down the back stairway and said - looked at me. He looked kind of shell-shocked, and he said, he took it back. He took it back. I said, who took what back? And he said, Gore just called me and took back his concession. And I went, well, that sucks. I didn't know what else to say.

DETROW: (Laughter).

KENNERLY: And so he went into that other small room, and you have this tableau of the Bush dynasty, former President Bush 41. You had Jeb Bush, who's the governor of Florida, and George W., who's the governor of Texas and thought he won the election. And so that picture caught it all. And if you look closely at it, which you can see it in the room, you see Mrs. Bush's hand is kind of crushed into her palm and the tension is palpable...

DETROW: Yeah.

KENNERLY: ...In the room. And you've got Cheney's there, and Don Evans, who was the campaign chairman.

DETROW: You described it pretty well. But to me, here's the tension of the picture. George W. Bush is sitting in a chair in the center. He's kind of hunched a little bit over with his elbows on his knees, looking forward in thought. And then his brother Jeb is standing above him. He's the governor of Florida at the time. This is all his problem. And he looks incredibly tense. And then, off in the background, you have the former President George H.W. Bush making phone calls. So there's a lot emotionally going on here.

KENNERLY: Well, there was one other element to that. So Jeb had been celebrating with having a drink or two. And you can see in that picture he looks concerned, at least, but he was totally sobered up at that point. He thought they'd won, and now they might lose. That was probably - I think probably my best political photograph.

DETROW: What do you think a photograph tells us that video doesn't? I mean, we consume politics these days through social media, through reels, through short video clips, you know? But there's so much emotion that comes through in a still picture, and I think it's different. How do you think about that?

KENNERLY: Well, for one thing, you're never going to see video in the room when it happened because video, then you would need sound with it, and conversations are being recorded. Photographers are incredibly discreet, and we - our currency is trust. So what you're seeing in still photos is the real picture of what's going on. You'll never see a video clip of that ever.

DETROW: How do you think about positioning? Is it just instinctual at this point? Because you're clearly - you're in the room. You're trying to make people comfortable in the room. You're trying to get the right shot and be in the right place. How do you think about kind of your own movements as you're scanning and looking for the moments to capture?

KENNERLY: Oh, that's a good question, and it's instinctive in my case. I remember when I first started with President Ford, the first three or four days, every time somebody would come into the Oval Office. He would say, oh, Senator Goldwater, meet David Kennerly, my photographer. And I had to train him. I said, please don't do that. I'm, like, missing pictures here. So pretty soon, I ended up being invisible, literally. And that's the trick. I mean, you can't teach that in school. Back to the Bush photo, that's a great example of it. They're so concentrated on watching this little TV about what's going on, no one was paying any attention to me. You can see that. I mean, that is a picture, like, taken by the invisible man, essentially.

DETROW: You said at the beginning of the conversation that you thought that election night 2000 picture is your best political photograph. Do you have a favorite beyond that?

KENNERLY: Well, yeah, I do, actually. And it's political, and it features George W. Bush. And it was at the African American Museum opening. And Michelle Obama, the first lady, came over to Bush before they started the ceremony and gave him a hug. And I nailed that picture. That one is a really important shot, particularly in the light of what's going on today where everybody throws rocks at each other. And here you have an African American woman, a white guy, a Republican, a Democrat, in this fantastic moment that was genuine. And I think that's sort of a fairy-tale picture in a way. We long for the days when politicians got along. And I - in the book, you'll see, I have a lot of examples of that. George Herbert Walker Bush as VP with Tip O'Neill yucking it up. John Kerry and John McCain, Democrat, Republican, Vietnam vets.

DETROW: Going back to that 2016 image, I'm looking at it. And this - like, this really did kind of change both of their public images in terms of I feel like from that point forward, when they were on stage together, everyone was looking at them to see how they'd interact. They both look so comfortable. And his eyes are closed, and he's almost nuzzling into Michelle Obama, and it's just the sign of closeness. It's remarkable.

KENNERLY: Well, it's also an example of where the picture doesn't tell the entire story, although that was a genuine moment, and I nailed that. However, when I give talks, I show the film clip of that happening. The picture conveyed a little more warm and fuzzier than it was. But it's an honest picture, however. I think that was the important thing.

DETROW: Yeah. That's David Hume Kennerly. His new book is "In The Room: 60 Years On The Front Lines Of History." Thanks so much for talking to us.

KENNERLY: Thank you.
