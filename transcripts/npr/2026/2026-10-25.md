## More dads are taking paid family leave for a new baby
: NPR

AYESHA RASCOE, HOST:

The U.S. doesn't require paid time off for new parents. Instead, it leaves it up to states or employers to provide. In states that do require paid parental leave, an increasing number of fathers are taking time off to care for and bond with their children. In California, men now make up about half of those applying for the leave. Last year, they even surpassed women for the first time since the state started offering the benefit in 2004. Elly Yu of LAist News brings us this report.

UNIDENTIFIED CHILD #1: Give me the ball.

(SOUNDBITE OF PLASTIC BALL BOUNCING)

KARLO CAMPANA: Ooh, lay it.

ELLY YU, BYLINE: It's Saturday night, and Karlo Campana is outnumbered. His two young girls take turns shooting a plastic ball into the hoop attached to their bedroom door. Campana goes in for a dunk.

(SOUNDBITE OF PLASTIC BALL BOUNCING)

UNIDENTIFIED CHILD #2: Ooh, did it (ph).

CAMPANA: Ooh. Just threw it, and it came back.

UNIDENTIFIED CHILD #2: Yay.

YU: On the floor, their baby brother watches in awe from his bouncy chair.

CAMPANA: Want to shoot it, papas? Not yet?

YU: Campana is a dad of three in Orange County, California. He works at an ad agency. And when his son was born this spring, he took paid time off just as he did when his two older daughters were born.

CAMPANA: You need that adjustment. Like, nobody gives you a playbook.

YU: He says having the time off not to think about work or worry about losing income during that time was crucial for him and his family.

CAMPANA: I get to spend time with each of them when they were just, like, in the infant stage, the baby stage, and I get to hold them and, you know, change the poopy diapers, the pee-pee (ph) diapers.

YU: Research shows that paid parental leave benefits the whole family. It's linked to lower infant mortality rates, and paternity leave in particular is linked to better mental and physical health outcomes for moms.

CAMPANA: My wife isn't doing it on her own. I think people don't think about the mental strain. Like, you're - one, you're a little bit sleep-deprived. Like, both parents are involved - mom and dad. It's like, OK, now it's like a team is taking care of this.

YU: California offers up to eight weeks of bonding time for parents after the birth or an adoption of a child. That's on top of six to eight weeks of disability leave moms get to recover after birth. It's one of 14 states and D.C. that have enacted paid bonding leave programs, and more dads are opting in - in California, Colorado, and Washington, sometimes more so than moms.

MOLLY WESTON WILLIAMSON: I think we shouldn't discount the impact of culture change.

YU: Molly Weston Williamson is with the advocacy group Paid Leave for All. When California first started its program two decades ago, men made up fewer than 20% of bonding claim applicants at the time. Now men account for about half.

WILLIAMSON: We're in a very different place in terms of our understanding of gender roles, of paternity leave, of dads' roles than we were 20 plus years ago.

YU: Williamson says there are other factors at play, too, for the growth. California boosted the amount of money workers can get. Now, in that state and in others, it can be as high as 90% of their regular salary.

WILLIAMSON: That makes it a realistic option, and I think we're seeing dads and moms responding to that change.

YU: Still, she says, there's a lot we don't know about the new data about dads. Are more dads than moms working and then filing claims? Are single moms able to access the benefit? One thing that's clear, the growth in these states comes as more parents are feeling strained economically. The American Family Survey out of Brigham Young University found that last year, 7 in 10 Americans said raising children was unaffordable, the biggest single-year jump since the survey started more than a decade ago. Jeremy Pope is a professor at BYU who worked on the survey.

JEREMY POPE: I don't think they want government involved in the decision to have kids, but they worry a lot about how much it costs, and they would like to see ways of making those costs smaller or getting help with those costs.

UNIDENTIFIED CHILD #2: Baby. Baby.

YU: Back in Orange County, California, Campana juggles between his three kids. His toddler hands the baby the basketball briefly.

CAMPANA: Don't take it away.

UNIDENTIFIED CHILD #1: Baby.

YU: At first, Campana wasn't sure he should take paternity leave. His dad worked all the time.

CAMPANA: I felt guilty, for sure. I think dads maybe feel they need to be the provider. They need to be the ones that are only helping their family by bringing money or working.

YU: But he says now he sees he's helping his family by being there. For NPR News, I'm Elly Yu in Los Angeles.

RASCOE: This story comes from NPR's partnership with LAist and KFF Health News.

## Democrats hope independent candidates can help their Senate odds

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

About 45% of Americans say they are independents, but there are only three independent lawmakers in Congress. Independent candidates say that the time might be right to boost that number. And as Democrats look to retake control of Congress, they hope these campaigns might help defeat Republicans in states where their own brand is hurting. NPR's Barbara Sprunt as this report.

DAN MORGAN: OK, jump in. Well, the seat's a little dirty. But...

BARBARA SPRUNT, BYLINE: On a crisp, sunny day in Burwell, Nebraska, Dan Morgan drives me across his family-run ranch to where the weaning process is starting.

MORGAN: So they're rounding up two pastures of cows and babies. And...

SPRUNT: The Morgan Ranch runs about 15,000 acres and is home to a thousand cows and thousands of offspring.

MORGAN: There's a lot of mouths to feed, both animals and two-legged.

SPRUNT: We pull over next to the pasture.

Hi, ladies.

(SOUNDBITE OF MOOING)

SPRUNT: Morgan says he's gotten a front-row seat to what he calls the uncertainty of the Trump administration's policies.

MORGAN: To raise a piece of meat takes me three years. OK, look at the changes in the past two years in terms of cost, tariffs, the reciprocal tariffs in other countries because half of our business is export. So it's just hell.

SPRUNT: He's a Democrat. He calls himself a small blue dot in a sea of red. There's no Democrat running for Senate here, but he's excited about Dan Osborn, an independent candidate.

MORGAN: Osborn makes the case somebody needs to represent the working family, and he can effectively do that.

SPRUNT: Osborn is a Navy veteran and industrial mechanic who got into politics after leading a workers' strike at the Kellogg's plant in 2021. Morgan says he hears a lot of questions about how effective Osborn could be as an independent, who doesn't plan to caucus with either party.

MORGAN: Well, guys, you start with one, and then you build two. Somebody has to start the ball rolling. It will snowball, but somebody's got to have courage enough to stand up and get it started.

SPRUNT: Osborn ran two years ago and beat expectations by coming within 7 points of the incumbent, Republican Deb Fischer. To win now, he'll have to impress not just blue dots like Morgan, but conservatives. And to reach them, he's criss-crossing the state on a 93-county tour. At one of the stops, he tells me he's not just running against another incumbent Republican senator, Pete Ricketts.

DAN OSBORN: I'm running against the entire establishment that is stuck in this system that's working exactly the way it's supposed to work for them. And I represent something that is going to throw a wrench in it.

SPRUNT: But Ricketts is making the case that Osborn is actually a secret Democrat. Turn on the radio in Nebraska, and you'll probably hear this jingle from the Ricketts campaign.

(SOUNDBITE OF AD)

UNIDENTIFIED MUSICAL ARTISTS: (Singing) He is a fake - Democrat Osborn. He is a fake - Democrat Osborn. He is a fake. Democrat Osborn is a Democrat.

SPRUNT: Sue McNeil (ph) hears that moniker a lot these days. She's a lifelong Republican in Loup County.

SUE MCNEIL: They're just using that Democrat Dan to scare people because Nebraska is a Republican state, and I feel like a lot of people are a little nervous about supporting a Democrat. But Dan Osborn is not a Democrat. He's an independent.

SPRUNT: She's voting for Osborn and says she's disappointed with the Trump administration and Republican controlled Congress.

MCNEIL: Why did we start the war with Iran? Our fuel prices have skyrocketed. Today, we went to town and filled up our ranch pickup, and it was $149. I'm just, like, how are we supposed to make it?

SPRUNT: Other Republicans aren't as convinced. At a meet-and-greet at a crowded coffee shop in Thedford, Casey Stengel (ph) asks a question.

CASEY STENGEL: On-the-fence voter.

OSBORN: OK.

STENGEL: Give me three things that you can do for us to convince me to vote for you.

OSBORN: OK. Well, I don't take money from Israel or defense contractors. I want to go to the United States Senate because I understand the value of uplifting workers.

SPRUNT: When Osborn heads out...

STENGEL: All right.

OSBORN: See you.

STENGEL: Yeah.

SPRUNT: I ask Stengel if he's convinced.

STENGEL: I mean, there's good ideas there, but one man ain't going to go fix the deal.

SPRUNT: But there's a world in which it actually isn't just one man. About 1,000 miles away from here is Seth Bodnar, one of three other independents running for U.S. Senate seats.

(SOUNDBITE OF PARADE)

SPRUNT: At the recent University of Montana homecoming parade, Bodnar, the former president of the school, says his campaign is part of a larger independent movement.

SETH BODNAR: People who will say I work with either party when it makes sense for Montana, and I'll stand up to either party when it doesn't.

SPRUNT: In Nebraska, the Democratic Party told voters early on they'd be supporting Osborn as their best shot at preventing a Republican from winning. In Montana, there is a Democratic nominee, but major Democratic figures in the state have thrown their support behind Bodnar, who they say can beat the Republican. Some state Democrats say the split between those supporting the nominee and those supporting Bodnar has created resentments on both sides. The Democratic nominee, Alani Bankhead, says she thinks Bodnar-aligned groups expected she would eventually bow out.

ALANI BANKHEAD: The problem is that nobody told me about that plan.

SPRUNT: She compared the dynamic to Nebraska.

BANKHEAD: The Democratic nominee said that they would step aside in favor for the independent if the people voted for them, and that's very transparent.

SPRUNT: Jane Kleeb, chair of the Nebraska Democratic Party and DNC vice chair, says the way Democrats decide to back an independent candidate is important.

JANE KLEEB: We were very honest with voters. If you're going to do this as a Democratic Party, that's what you have to do. You have to be honest with voters and explain that strategy and make sure that the Democrats support that.

SPRUNT: That transparency early on in the process could be the difference between an independent having a clear path to victory or the whole thing backfiring.

Barbara Sprunt, NPR News, the Sandhills, Nebraska.
