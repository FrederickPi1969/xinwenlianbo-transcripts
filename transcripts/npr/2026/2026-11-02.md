## Does this fluoride-free toothpaste offer the same protection?

LEILA FADEL, HOST:

Anyone who is face-to-face with a leftover bag of Halloween candy might want to pay extra attention to your dental health. That includes what products to use to protect teeth from that sugar. Fluoride in drinking water has been credited with fighting tooth decay for 80 years. It's also an ingredient in most toothpastes. Now another toothpaste ingredient is getting some hype. So how does it stack up against the more traditional cavity fighter? NPR's Rachel Carlson reports.

RACHEL CARLSON, BYLINE: Dentist Jin-Ho Phark says his patients have been asking him about a fluoride-free toothpaste. It uses a calcium phosphate mineral called hydroxyapatite.

JIN-HO PHARK: There's a lot of interest from the patients to hear from us, not only from our patients. I hear that also regularly from students. They send me emails and with a picture of, oh, I have this toothpaste here. So what do you think about it?

CARLSON: Phark teaches at the Herman Ostrow School of Dentistry at University of Southern California. Hydroxyapatite promises to help users prevent cavities, whiten their teeth, reduce sensitivity and strengthen their enamel. The products have very small mineral particles.

PHARK: And they basically occupy little crevices or voids, cracks and things like that in the tooth and fill that up.

CARLSON: Fill it up with calcium and phosphate, the same minerals that make up your enamel. That's a little different from fluoride toothpaste, which has been the gold standard since the 1950s. Fluoride is a naturally occurring mineral. It protects your teeth from sugars that can lead to cavities. Bacteria in your mouth feed on that sugar and produce acids that eat away at enamel.

PHARK: The fluoride, what it does is it basically creates a - kind of a new mineral so that is a little stronger and more resistant.

CARLSON: It acts like a shield for your enamel - as long as you keep using it over time. And there's been decades of research on fluoride, compared to much more limited research on hydroxyapatite. One 2023 clinical study, funded by a company with hydroxyapatite products, showed comparable effects from both. It followed 171 people, some using fluoride, some using hydroxyapatite. At the end of a year and a half, the group showed similar levels of new tooth decay.

Dr. Livia Tenuta is a professor at the University of Michigan School of Dentistry.

LIVIA TENUTA: The studies that we have with hydroxyapatite toothpaste, the main problem I see with them, there are very few studies, and they recruited participants of, like, good oral health.

CARLSON: She says more long-term studies with people at higher risk of tooth decay would be helpful. So which toothpaste should you use?

TENUTA: I would recommend as the best option to stick to a fluoride toothpaste because that's what we have a lot of evidence behind.

CARLSON: Health experts widely agree that fluoride is safe and helps prevent cavities and it works best when you, of course, brush twice daily, floss and visit your dentist regularly.

Rachel Carlson, NPR News.

## College admissions offices warm up as students become scarce

LEILA FADEL, HOST:

ALEX ZARET: I'm interested in, like, English and political science.

BECCA JOHNSON: So this is a shirt. It's a bright-green shirt with yellow lettering that says, ask me anything about Skidmore College.

RUSSELL: So how did we get here, where colleges are pulling out all the stops to recruit new students?

ANGEL PEREZ: The reality is we have a demographic crisis in the United States. We have fewer students in the pipeline to higher education.

RUSSELL: That's Angel Perez. He's the CEO of the National Association for College Admission Counseling. Colleges are simply facing a numbers problem. Fewer high school graduates are going straight to college, and birth rates in the U.S. are declining.

PEREZ: If you add on top of that the challenges that international students are facing, enrollment has never been more challenged.

RUSSELL: In addition to leaning on professors to recruit new students, more than a hundred colleges in New York are waiving application fees. In 17 other states, schools automatically admit students who meet certain academic criteria.

But the challenge isn't just getting students to apply. Schools now have to work harder to get them to commit to come. At Skidmore College, the professors have met with or hosted more than 300 prospective students in their classes since last year. That now includes Alex Zaret, who we heard from at the start of this story. Zaret met with the head of the English department after touring Skidmore's campus.

A ZARET: Obviously I have to appeal to colleges and go through the whole application process, but, like, at the end of the day, like, if there's multiple offers, there's still freedom to choose where to go.

RUSSELL: Zaret feels like the power dynamic of the college admissions process has changed for the better. It's still a ton of work. There's a lot at stake and Zaret has more colleges left to tour.

For NPR News, I'm Emily Russell in Saratoga Springs, New York.

## Oceanographer explains the record-breaking king tides happening on both coasts

A MARTÍNEZ, HOST:

In California, Florida and the Carolinas recently, water has pushed over seawalls and onto the streets. It's because of a phenomenon called king tides. This year's highest and strongest tides are triggered by the gravitational pull of the moon. So let's look more closely at king tides with Alec Bogdanoff. He's a meteorologist and oceanographer and CEO of Brizaga, which is an engineering consulting firm focused on flood risk management. He's on the line from Fort Lauderdale, Florida. Alec, so what makes these king tides so powerful this past week?

ALEC BOGDANOFF: So in Florida we already have a king tide season. In the fall, the water is the warmest, and when you have those full moons and new moons, you get king tides. Add sea-level rise on top of it, and we're starting to see it more frequently. But this week, we saw winds from the east that pushed even more water onto land. Those things together created records.

MARTÍNEZ: Is it unusual for it to be happening on the East and West Coasts right around the same time?

BOGDANOFF: Yeah, that happens to be a coincidence. In California, we're dealing with what's on track to be the strongest El Nino on record, so that allowed winds to shift and water to be pushed towards the east and flow up the California coast. So it's two mechanisms that are really happening at the same time.

MARTÍNEZ: Alec, you grew up in Florida. How is this different from what maybe you've seen in the past?

BOGDANOFF: So we've talked about king tides and sea-level rise here for a long time. Communities have worked together. We're seeing the realities now. These tides are higher than we've ever seen. Roads that have never flooded are now underwater. Roads that flooded a little bit are now impassable. Those things coupled together are really bringing sea-level rise home for people. What I tell folks is this is really a coming attraction of what we're going to see in the future if we don't act.

MARTÍNEZ: Wow, a coming attraction of what we might see in the future. That is a terrifying thought (laughter), Alec. Wondering what role does climate change maybe play here?

BOGDANOFF: So climate change is a force multiplier. So what's happening, at least for sea-level rise, is it's heating our oceans. So you can think of it like an old mercury thermometer. The oceans are expanding as they warm. The big cause of sea-level rise in the future, though, is going to be ice on Greenland and Antarctica melting into our oceans, a little like leaving the faucet on and your bathtub overflowing.

MARTÍNEZ: What can communities do to prepare for this? I mean, if this is indeed something that is going to be in our future.

BOGDANOFF: So step one is to start planning. Communities can do assessments, whether they're stormwater assessments, vulnerability assessments, adaptation plans. It's a multiprong approach because it's not just private infrastructure. It's also public infrastructure.
