## Opinion: A sculpture in perpetual motion, removed

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

A big blue presence has vanished from the landscape of Washington, D.C. On Wednesday, a crew removed a 24-foot-tall metal sculpture called Blue by Joel Shapiro from the grounds of the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts. Our family turned out to say goodbye.

Blue is a giant stick figure with what seems to be four limbs, standing on what might be one leg while the other is lifted. To walk? To leap? To dance? The uppermost limbs, which look like arms, are extended. To welcome, twirl, swing or sway? The work was installed on that part of the Kennedy Center's grounds called the REACH, which opened in 2019. I've always thought it seemed an artful fit in that place, which, before the center became a source of controversy during the Trump presidency, abounded just about every night with music, dance and all kinds of shows available to the public for free.

It's about action and risk and performance and energy, sculptor Joel Shapiro, who died last year, said in a video about Blue that was on the Kennedy Center website. It's a celebration of possibility. But Josef Palermo, who was let go this past March as curator of visual arts and special programming at the Kennedy Center, told The New York Times this week that Blue had been targeted early on for removal by the Trump administration, which might have seen something else in the sculpture. Well, isn't seeing something else art?

The Kennedy Center, where President Trump's supporters are now in charge, issued a statement saying, we are deeply grateful to Joel Shapiro for the years Blue has spent animating our campus and connecting visitors to his vision.

I think part of what made Blue so engaging is that the figure, in implied perpetual motion along the Capitol's riverfront, stirred people to imagine for themselves what might have set that big blue figure to whirl, spin, stomp or prance. We met a man at the dismantling who works for the artist's foundation who told us that Blue will be cleaned, reassembled and, he was sure, reinstalled in some new spot. Maybe removing Joel Shapiro's Blue so summarily from its perch at the Kennedy Center will make people even more eager to seek out the towering figure who caused such a stir.

## Trump administration to dig at triumphal arch site despite no final federal approvals

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

The Trump administration says it'll soon break ground on a 250-foot triumphal arch without final federal approval and despite an ongoing lawsuit. The veterans who brought that lawsuit have asked a judge to step in. She did late last night. NPR's Rachel Treisman has been covering this saga and joins us now. Rachel, thanks for being with us.

RACHEL TREISMAN, BYLINE: A pleasure.

SIMON: We're talking about an enormous arch, gold statues on top. It would be built at one end of the bridge that connects the Lincoln Memorial in D.C. to Arlington National Cemetery in Virginia. Did the judge stop it, or is the government forging ahead?

TREISMAN: Well, the government would like to. So as a reminder, Interior Secretary Doug Burgum said Thursday that it would start excavation work within two weeks. And that was surprising to people because the arch hasn't gotten final approval yet. It did get design approval in May from one federal panel packed with Trump appointees, but it still needs the green light from the National Capital Planning Commission, which doesn't meet again until October. And several agencies are still conducting studies on the potential impacts of the arch, including on the nearby D.C. airport.

A White House spokesperson told me they're only doing work that does not require approval and, quote, "helps in the process." Now, lawyers for the government clarified in a filing late Friday that they're talking about archaeological surveys, not construction. After that, the judge ordered the government to give 48 hours' notice before doing anything other than on-site information gathering, so we'll have to wait and see.

SIMON: Rachel, remind us - who is fighting the construction and why?

TREISMAN: Well, broadly speaking, historic preservation experts have accused the Trump administration of trying to rush the review process for this arch basically since it was introduced last October. And then earlier this year, an architectural historian and three Vietnam War veterans sued. They said the arch would desecrate Arlington National Cemetery and requires approval from Congress. The Trump administration disagrees. And it was those plaintiffs that asked the judge yesterday to issue a restraining order to block construction for two weeks.

NICHOLAS SANSONE: We have no sense of why the administration believes it's authorized to begin work on the arch.

TREISMAN: Nicholas Sansone, the lead attorney in the case, says any work at the proposed site is premature. He says once construction starts, it might be hard for a court to offer any real relief. And we saw that play out recently when Trump had the White House East Wing demolished to build a ballroom.

SIMON: Supreme Court issued a ruling on that ballroom this week. Does that have any bearing on the arch?

TREISMAN: Well, it might. Also, on Thursday, the Trump administration filed a motion asking the judge to dismiss the arch lawsuit altogether, and it specifically pointed to that Supreme Court ballroom ruling, which says the plaintiffs in that case didn't have legal standing to sue in the first place. Sansone, the lawyer, says that is definitely not the case with the Vietnam Veterans suing over the arch since they have a deep personal connection to the cemetery. But he says there's a lot at stake, even for people who don't.

SANSONE: A system where the executive branch essentially gets to build first and answer later the question of whether that building is lawful should trouble all of us.

TREISMAN: I should say tens of thousands of critics from all over the country have submitted public comments as part of this review process. They worry the arch would disrupt everything from traffic to funeral services at Arlington to views of the National Mall. And, in fact, the National Park Service even acknowledged in a report last week that the arch would affect dozens of historic sites nearby, but it's still moving ahead.

SIMON: What are you watching for next in the story, Rachel?

TREISMAN: Well, government lawyers have said the work will start no sooner than September 21, and they say any natural resources affected would be restored by the end of October. And that basically brings us to midterm elections in early November. U.S. Representative Don Beyer, a Democrat from Northern Virginia, thinks that might explain President Trump's hurry.

DON BEYER: He has no one in the Republican Party willing to stand up to him on many, many different things. He only might have that trifecta for another couple of weeks.

TREISMAN: Beyer says he has actually yet to meet a constituent who supports the arch, and he expects big protests whenever work does start. Now, the Trump administration has said it expects construction would take two to three years, factoring in a 20-hour-a-day, year-round work schedule that could take us right up until the end of Trump's term.

SIMON: NPR's Rachel Treisman. Thanks so much.

TREISMAN: Thank you.

## Venezuela opposition walks fine line over Trump-backed oil deal

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

The blockbuster oil deal between the United States and Venezuela that was announced last week has opposition leaders in Venezuela treading a fine line. They fear the agreement could help Venezuela's authoritarian regime stay in power, but opposing it could anger the Trump administration, which in effect now calls the shots in Venezuela. For more, here's reporter John Otis.

JOHN OTIS, BYLINE: The agreement would give the U.S. government majority control over 65 billion barrels of Venezuelan oil reserves.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

DELCY RODRIGUEZ: (Speaking Spanish).

OTIS: At a Caracas news conference on Wednesday, Venezuela's acting president, Delcy Rodriguez, said the agreement could bring badly needed foreign investment to the country's ailing oil industry. But many Venezuelans view Rodriguez as an illegitimate president. Until her predecessor, Nicolás Maduro, was captured by the U.S. military in January, she had served as his loyal vice president. Rodriguez fully supported Maduro's repressive tactics, including his stealing of the 2024 presidential election. Today, her government holds more than 300 political prisoners.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

MARÍA CORINA MACHADO: (Speaking Spanish).

OTIS: Citing such authoritarian behavior, María Corina Machado, Venezuela's main opposition leader, says she has profound concerns about the oil deal.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

MACHADO: (Speaking Spanish).

OTIS: "Economic development is impossible without solid institutions and a government elected by the people," Machado said in a video posted on social media. However, Machado, who wants to be Venezuela's next president, was careful not to criticize the oil agreement itself. She covets good relations with the Trump administration, which following the removal of Maduro, oversees Venezuela as a vassal state.

DAVID SMILDE: Nobody really who is aiming for political power has come out against this agreement.

OTIS: That's David Smilde, a Venezuela scholar at Tulane University.

SMILDE: They all know that doing so would basically mean they have no political possibility in the next coming years under Trump.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

UNIDENTIFIED PERSON: (Speaking Spanish).

OTIS: In fact, nearly all of the opposition lawmakers in Venezuela's National Assembly voted this week to approve the oil agreement. They include Congressman Antonio Ecarri.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

ANTONIO ECARRI: (Speaking Spanish).

OTIS: "I want democracy," he says, "but it will come hand in hand with an economic opening and American capital."

But Trump's actions and words make clear he prefers dealing with the compliant Rodriguez rather than a freely elected president who might be more independent. Speaking at the White House this week, Trump brushed off calls for elections in Venezuela.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

PRESIDENT DONALD TRUMP: I just don't think they're ready yet. You know, it's very new. We took them out of a dictatorship, and we're getting along great with the government. Delcy, as you know, is doing a very, very good job, very highly respected.

OTIS: Polls say otherwise. After Rodriguez botched the response to twin earthquakes in June that killed more than 6,500 people, one survey by AtlasIntel put her job approval rating at just 22%. Yet the oil agreement should keep her in Trump's good graces, said Venezuelan economist Ricardo Hausmann, who spoke to CNN.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

RICARDO HAUSMANN: This is a great deal for her. She gives away the oil, and she gets to stay in power.

OTIS: Rodriguez's envoys are holding discussions with the opposition on a democratic transition, but critics claim the acting president is slow-walking the talks and may try to serve out Maduro's term that ends in 2030.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

RODRIGUEZ: (Speaking Spanish).

OTIS: When pressed by journalists at Wednesday's news conference, Rodriguez said Venezuela will hold presidential elections, but she refused to say when.

For NPR News, I'm John Otis.

## Ann M. Martin's 'Baby-Sitters Club' turns 40

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

Since 1986, children have been seeing themselves on the pages of the cherished book series "The Baby-Sitters Club."

GUS LICKTEIG: The "Baby-Sitters Club" member I identify with the most is probably Claudia 'cause she's super artsy, and I really align with that the most.

HANK LICKTEIG: Kristy. I think that we're both athletic. I feel that I'm a leader, like her.

LILA ASHWORTH: Mary Anne, because she really likes reading, and she loves cats.

JUANA SUMMERS, BYLINE: I think when I was younger, I definitely wanted to be a Stacey. Let's be honest. I'm probably a Mary Anne.

LEILA FADEL, BYLINE: I'm shy like Mary Anne. I'm kind of bossy like Kristy. And I'm tall like Stacey, but I definitely wasn't as sophisticated. So I'm a little mosaic.

BECKY ALBERTALLI: Head on the pillow at night, I know that I'm probably a Mallory.

ANN M MARTIN: Mary Anne. Flat-out Mary Anne.

SIMON: Ah, a sampling of fans - Gus and Hank Lickteig, Lila Ashworth (ph), NPR's Juana Summers and Leila Fadel and author Becky Albertalli and Ann M. Martin, the creator of the beloved series.

"The Baby-Sitters Club" turns 40 this year. And its characters - Kristy, Mary Anne, Stacey, Claudia and the rest - have moved seamlessly between books, graphic novels, TV and, someday soon, even a musical. NPR's Barrie Hardymon went to Martin's house to talk about why the series has meant so much to so many generations.

BARRIE HARDYMON, BYLINE: I'm a Stacey with a Kristy rising, and both parts of me are vibrating with excitement as Ann M. Martin walks us through her office in Upstate New York.

MARTIN: Behind...

HARDYMON: The oeuvre.

MARTIN: ...You is - yeah.

HARDYMON: Forty years after Kristy called her first Baby-Sitters Club meeting to order, Martin is still surrounded by the world she created.

MARTIN: "Baby-Sitters Club Super Specials," "Baby-Sitters Club Mysteries," "Friends Forever"...

HARDYMON: The bookshelves in Martin's office are lined with candy-colored "Baby-Sitters Club" books in every kind of format you can think of - like the old ones with the classic block lettering on the front and the girls in varying styles of '80s bangs.

MARTIN: Original novels, and then foreign editions, if...

HARDYMON: Martin's writing career isn't limited to the BSC.

Where are the other novels? You're so prolific.

MARTIN: Oh.

HARDYMON: Where - do you have a...

MARTIN: I do. They're over here.

HARDYMON: Over the past four decades, she's written more than a hundred books and won multiple awards, including a Newbery Honor for the standalone "A Corner Of The Universe." She says she's most proud of that one, a novel called "Rain Reign" and, of course, "Kristy's Great Idea," the first book in the "Baby-Sitters Club" series.

Were you a babysitter growing up?

MARTIN: Yep. Well, I started as a mother's helper when I was about 10, I guess, and it just continued. I was still babysitting when I was in college, so I had plenty of experiences to draw on.

HARDYMON: Populating the club - Kristy, Claudia, Stacey and Mary Anne, and others from the "Baby-Sitters Club" multiverse. Martin drew on the relationships from her own life.

MARTIN: Mary Anne is based on me. She's the shy one. She liked reading. She liked needlework. And then Kristy was based on my best friend, Beth, who was the one who had all the big ideas.

BETH MCKEEVER: This is the 66th time I have wished her a happy birthday.

HARDYMON: That's Beth McKeever. Martin just turned 71. But they were little kids when they met, living on the same street just a couple of houses apart. The two girls became fast friends.

MCKEEVER: I think the thing that made me Kristy is I probably saw things and said, I think we can do this. Although she did use the word bossy to me, so I don't know. I'll have to think about that (laughter).

HARDYMON: They played together all the time - circuses, libraries, plays, forts.

MCKEEVER: Like, OK. It's raining today, but it's icy rain. So what do we do next? We're going to go get our ice skates. That's what we're going to go do next.

HARDYMON: And that was the magic of the books - a can-do spirit, real friendships, plus that grain of responsibility from the babysitting business. The girls were in charge of something. Sometimes Martin wrote about really hard stuff - death, divorce, illness, fighting with your best friend or, like babysitter Stacey, living with diabetes.

MARTIN: When I first started writing the books, I had two good friends who were diabetic. And I got and still get letters from kids who accurately diagnose themselves as having diabetes, just from having read about Stacey.

HARDYMON: Martin did research. It was important to her to get it right.

MARTIN: I felt a great deal of responsibility. And I know how the stories and the characters impacted the readers, not just in terms of babysitting, but in terms of looking to the characters or to me as role models or people that they could go to for advice.

HARDYMON: Becky Albertalli is the author of the "Love, Simon" series. She came into the BSC world through Karen, Kristy's little sister.

ALBERTALLI: My parents separated when I was 7. And Karen was the first time I had ever read about a family that looked like mine. I was a joint-custody kid. It was, like, that first time seeing something about my own life reflected in a book.

HARDYMON: Albertalli is one of the writers chosen to collaborate on the 40th anniversary edition, "The Baby-Sitters Club Fan Edition: Baby-Sitter Summer."

ALBERTALLI: The, like, prevailing feeling of it was like an honor on top of an honor.

HARDYMON: Each author wrote a chapter from the point of view of an original club member. She wrote the Kristy story.

ALBERTALLI: She is a very, very beloved character among LGBT women. You could interpret the original Kristy storylines and character as somewhat queer-coded.

HARDYMON: In Albertalli's chapter, Kristy meets a girl named Rowan, who's openly queer. It's the beginning of a summer of self-discovery for Kristy.

ALBERTALLI: I was a little, like, bisexual kid as I was reading these books, but I had no idea. Like, that was something that I figured out in adulthood, decades later. So this was an opportunity to kind of give this character some of the clarity that I did not have at the time.

HARDYMON: Albertalli had Martin's blessing for the storyline. Kristy does get to kiss a girl. It's just another expansion of a world that, with the exception of the fact that the babysitters still use a landline, has been growing and changing since 1986. Ann M. Martin has now watched generations of her readers grow up.

MARTIN: The legacy that I see or I hope for is that the books have helped to turn kids into readers. I get so many letters from people who read the books and have grown up to become writers, librarians, teachers, bloggers. I think that's fabulous.

HARDYMON: Barrie Hardymon, NPR News.
