## The surprising and often forgotten history of economic populism in Texas

ROB SCHMITZ, HOST:

As the votes came in on election night Tuesday, some of the most shocking results were in Texas. The blue wave did not stop at the border of the Lone Star State. Democrats swept the top races in Texas with James Talarico winning a Senate seat and Gina Hinojosa winning the race for governor.

Austin-based political commentator David Griscom was perhaps not as surprised to see his state flip. Earlier this year, he published a book titled "The Myth Of Red Texas: Cowboys, Populism, And Class War In The Radical South." He joins us now. Welcome.

DAVID GRISCOM: Thank you so much for having me. It's been a really wonderful past few days.

SCHMITZ: What great timing, with a book title like that. Texas, obviously, as you've written, has a reputation for being a reliably red state, but as we've seen, it's a lot more complicated than that. How did Texas Democrats pull this off?

GRISCOM: Well, I think in a lot of ways, you saw Texas Democrats returning to our roots here. You know, many people forget this, but Texas was once the hotbed of kind of left-wing, pro-working-class politics. Going all the way back to the 1890s, we were the heart of the populist movement, and it's no accident that James Talarico, Clayton Tucker and Gina Hinojosa all call themselves populists.

Using this kind of rhetoric, I think, really focuses on the billionaires and the folks who are harming our democracy. And I think that gave a lot of people who might have been waiting for an excuse to maybe buck the Republican Party, which has dominated state politics here for so long, the opportunity to do so.

SCHMITZ: Let's go back in history a little. You know, Texas politics are often associated, you know, with oil billionaires, conservative evangelical voters and the Republican establishment. You know, your book, "The Myth Of Red Texas," tells the story of, you know, working-class, populist tradition that oftentimes goes overlooked. Tell us a little bit about that.

GRISCOM: So there's multiple phases of this. It's an entire kind of generation or tradition in Texas politics that has been forgotten. My book opens up talking about barbed wire, which we all associate with Texas. But when barbed wire first comes to Texas, it was fiercely resisted. There was an episode called The Fence Cutting War, and this happened all across the state, where working-class cowboys got together and they started cutting down these new barbed wire fences because they saw these as a tool that was being used to dispossess them.

This then flows into the populist movement, which was born right here in Texas in Lampasas, where a bunch of farmers got together and they started asking themselves why those who work most get least and those who work least get most. And they started to look around and realize that it was the monopoly powers, the power of the banks, and it was the corruption of the politicians.

SCHMITZ: I want to dig in a little bit on that and the populism aspect of this. Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa has embraced the populist label with open arms. You know, I want to play a clip here from an interview that she did before the election with The New York Times.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

GINA HINOJOSA: Populism is about economic power. We see that things are so stacked against working people in this country. Fighting culture wars at this time is not a winnable fight, and that is not the fight of 2026.

SCHMITZ: And that's really interesting. I mean, she's separating her and Talarico's style of politics from Democrats elsewhere, who have sort of engaged more in identity politics. Is there something uniquely Texan about Hinojosa's or Talarico's approach as Democrats?

GRISCOM: Absolutely. So again, you know, Texas has had major swings before where everyday working people - cotton farmers, Black and white - they were able to unite working people, the vast majority of people, on messages about the ways that they can improve their life. And that's what Gina and James were able to do. They didn't run a partisan message, and that is a long tradition that has existed in this state.

I'd also add, if I could, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez - after 2024, she made a very strong point about how effective some of the anti-trans ads that the Trump administration put out were - you know, they are for they/them, Trump is for you. And AOC said, like, this was a problem with Democrats. When a lot of people were feeling economic pain, they felt that the things that Democrats were interested in weren't immediately important to their lives.

And in this election, the table was kind of reversed because all of the ads in Texas were about anti-LGBTQ things, anti-trans. And when somebody's talking about that - in this case being Republicans - when you're really struggling to get by, it does really show that these folks are out of touch with your economic needs, and that really helped Gina and James, for sure.

SCHMITZ: That is David Griscom, author of "The Myth Of Red Texas." David, thank you.

GRISCOM: Thank you so much.

## Oklahoma tribes say proposed legal changes put ancestral lands in Ohio at risk

ROB SCHMITZ, HOST:

Tribal leaders in Oklahoma whose ancestral lands are across the country in Ohio say sacred sites are under threat. Changes favored by the Trump administration to the National Historic Preservation Act could weaken much needed protections, they say. Reporters at member stations WYSO and KOSU take us to both places.

ADRIANA MARTINEZ-SMILEY, BYLINE: I'm Adriana Martinez-Smiley in Ohio, which is home to hundreds of earthworks sacred to Native Americans, like the Miamisburg Mound, one of the largest conical mounds in the eastern United States, rising 65 feet.

MICHELLE COLLINS: Once you get up there, you can just sit and kind of take in a view, a really fantastic view, of the Miami Valley.

MARTINEZ-SMILEY: Miamisburg Mayor Michelle Collins calls the structure awesome.

COLLINS: You're talking something that's a couple thousand years old.

MARTINEZ-SMILEY: The mound was built by the Indigenous Adena Culture. The National Historic Preservation Act, which has been around for 60 years, protects sites like this. Section 106 of the Act allows states, tribes or local governments to consult with federal agencies on development proposals and redesign projects to mitigate harm to sites.

However, the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation, a federal agency, and Congress are separately proposing changes. Their revisions would narrow what's considered damaging to a site and make consultation optional. Democratic Senator Martin Heinrich of New Mexico says the current rules also cause too much of a delay when it comes to approving energy projects.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

MARTIN HEINRICH: America needs to be able to build things in years, not in decades.

MARTINEZ-SMILEY: Advocates say the proposed changes should streamline the permitting process. However, tribal officials say the changes could undermine the responsibility the federal government has to cooperate with tribal nations.

SARAH LIESE, BYLINE: I'm Sarah Liese in Oklahoma. There are 38 federally recognized tribal nations in the state. For some, Ohio was once their home, but they were forcibly displaced to Oklahoma due to broken treaties and federal policies in the 18th and 19th centuries. Chief Ben Barnes of the Shawnee Tribe says the National Historic Preservation Act should remain as is.

BEN BARNES: The Section 106 consultation process allows the Shawnee Tribe and other tribes to make sure that sacred sites, such as the earthworks in Ohio or ancestral burial grounds or places of sacred memory, are preserved.

LIESE: Barnes says they also want to protect the spaces where they currently live, and it's key that Native Americans still have a say in the process with no optional provision about their involvement.

BARNES: We have a very large gas line that runs across Oklahoma to Springfield, Missouri. And one day, that gas line is going to need to be disturbed or going to need to be enlarged or need to be repaired. And without having Section 106 processes, they can do that without ever consulting the people that gather in that place to worship.

LIESE: Blake Norton, an official with the Citizen Potawatomi Nation, says these sites are much more to them than places needing development. He likens it to stripping away the humanity of his ancestors and disregarding the relationship Native Americans have to these spaces.

BLAKE NORTON: And for what? So a project can be completed? So that capitalism can continue?

LIESE: In a statement, Senator Heinrich says he's speaking with tribal leaders about possible compromises on the bill. For NPR News, I'm Sarah Liese in Oklahoma.

MARTINEZ-SMILEY: And I'm Adriana Martinez-Smiley in Ohio.

I spoke with Mairav Zonszein. She's an Israeli American analyst at the International Crisis Group here in Tel Aviv.

SCHMITZ: That is NPR's Lauren Frayer in Tel Aviv. Lauren, thank you.

FRAYER: You're welcome.

## President of The Heritage Foundation looks ahead to the future of the GOP

ROB SCHMITZ, HOST:

After the midterms this week, Republicans are asking, where do we go from here? The conservative think tank The Heritage Foundation has long provided answers to that question ever since it wrote the mandate for leadership for President Reagan in 1981. The foundation has informed many of the Trump administration's priorities through the policy blueprint known as Project 2025.

Kevin Roberts is a political strategist and was a key architect of Project 2025. He's led The Heritage Foundation as its president since 2021. He joins us now to talk about the path forward for Republicans. Welcome.

KEVIN ROBERTS: Thanks, Rob, for having me. It's a pleasure.

SCHMITZ: So before we get to the midterm results, we had news yesterday. Susie Wiles is out as President Trump's chief of staff. Stephen Miller is in. What does this tell us about the path forward for the Trump administration?

ROBERTS: Well, it's a great promotion of a great patriot, Stephen Miller, because what it signals to not just the radical-left majority coming into Congress, but for all of us on the political right, is that President Trump and his administration are not rolling over. I know that people who are on the political left disagree maybe with Stephen Miller with everything. They probably disagree with me and Heritage with everything. He's an extremely competent, politically conservative, very intellectual policymaker, and for those of us on the political right who are charting the way forward after this week's major defeat, I think Stephen's a perfect choice.

SCHMITZ: Why the change?

ROBERTS: I think what the president is signaling is that it is a new era, and he needs Stephen's acumen, particularly his ability to be on offense even with a large Democrat majority in the House and Senate.

SCHMITZ: So I think it's safe to say here that the midterms did not go the way that the president hoped. How do these results shape the plans that you and The Heritage Foundation are making for Republicans going forward?

ROBERTS: Yeah, even for people who were more pessimistic than I am, I actually thought the midterms would be OK. Obviously, I was wrong about that. But the people who were more pessimistic did not think that it would be a defeat on the scale. And so the first thing that we have to do is just to read reality truthfully. You acknowledge that, at least in terms of messaging, the message from the political right, from the administration, was insufficient.

I happen to think that the Iran war was a major problem even though I'm supportive of the Iran war, I've been saying since April and May that the administration needs to find a way to declare victory because the economic consequences that have aggravated existing inflationary concerns that Trump inherited were a real problem.

And so moving forward, what the administration needs to do is not just handle some of the lawfare and what I'm sure will be some nonsense coming from the Democrat majorities, but articulate a policy vision that gets back to that domestic policy, especially kitchen-table economics issues.

SCHMITZ: So on election night - you know, talking about Iran here - you told my colleague Mary Louise Kelly that larger-scale Republican defeats are not a wholesale rejection of President Trump's agenda but instead a, quote, "complaint by voters about the economic consequences of the Iran conflict." You just mentioned that. What needs to change when it comes to the U.S. war in Iran?

ROBERTS: Well, I've been saying since May or June, along with my colleagues - national security colleagues at Heritage, that No. 1, we're supportive of the war. No. 2, the president could have declared victory given the military achievements as early as May or June. And had he done that, I think we would have been able to see the diminished economic disadvantages that have come from the war in time to affect - in a positive way, from our vantage point - the election. It's really important that the president and the administration make that declaration soon because it will take, according to my economist colleagues at Heritage, several months for the inflationary pressure brought on by energy prices, energy inflation to come down.

I think that what you do - to cut to the chase here, Rob - is you obviously leave some naval and air superiority in Iran, maybe not to the extent of the deployment now. And when the Iranians misbehave, you can zing them with cruise missile attacks, and you also pair that with what has been a really good economic strangulation campaign by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent.

SCHMITZ: The election's been largely understood as a referendum on the economy and the cost of living for average Americans. Gas prices - because of the war with Iran and also because of President Trump's tariffs, which you were initially against before you embraced them - have been a problem. Would you embrace a pivot away from tariffs at this point?

ROBERTS: No, I'm very supportive. We at Heritage are very supportive of a true reciprocal tariff regime. And, you know, if we were building the tariff policy - and we said this going back to the so-called Liberation Day - that we would focus on the 5 to 10 worst transgressors of, quote-unquote, "free trade." And had the administration focused on that, I think the American people, maybe even a bipartisan way, would have really embraced that. I think there's still a future for that policy, but it's going to have to be, you know, more circumscribed than it's been.

SCHMITZ: You know, when we're talking about the economy and the problems of the economy right now, you have sort of boiled it down to Iran. Are there any other problems in the economy that you see?

ROBERTS: Oh, yeah. The point about Iran - so thanks for the opportunity to follow up on that - is not that it, in and of itself, has caused all of our economic difficulties or worries. It's that it's aggravated some long-standing existing problems. It's just objectively true, to put respective politics off to the side, that the Trump administration inherited very high inflation from the previous administration, and all of those economic measures were improving the week before the war in Iran. In fact, our chief economist at Heritage, Dr. E.J. Antoni, has predicted that there would have been a Trump economic boom had he not started the war in Iran. The point is that Iran, especially by increasing energy prices, aggravated those inflationary pressures.

SCHMITZ: You know, another question I have for you has to do with corruption. You know, corruption was also at the top of voters' concerns going into these midterm I was in Hungary earlier this year, where corruption also played a big part in Viktor Orbán's routing there. People see the president and his family benefiting from cryptocurrency, other investments. You know, what is your reaction to how this has damaged Republicans at the polls?

ROBERTS: Well, just looking at the exit polls, it's - American voters are saying, obviously, this is a concern, and you draw a very accurate parallel, Rob, with Hungary. I think, as you know, the Orbán government's policies were very good, but, you know, they had been in power for 16 years, and there clearly was corruption that was a problem.

And so it's really important moving forward - not just for the administration, not just for Republican allies in Congress, but also for the incoming Democrat majority in the House and Senate - to remember that this has been a very anti-power election. The American people are saying, we're tired of this. It isn't just a problem on the right. It isn't just a problem on the left. Thank goodness, Americans are paying attention, and they want leaders who are not engaged.

SCHMITZ: The agenda for the right used to be really clear - you know, smaller government, lower taxes, free trade. Project 2025 was a huge effort to define this new post-Reagan conservative movement. But you've had challenges, I think, keeping everyone aligned. It seems there's, like, sort of a generational civil war happening in the party with influencers and other right-wing think tanks all trying to define the party. Looking ahead to 2028, what is Heritage's core vision? How do you see regaining voter trust here?

ROBERTS: Yeah, you're definitely right about tensions on the right. And to state the obvious, President Trump will be leaving the stage in early 2029, and so all of us who are conservative policy leaders are trying to articulate a vision for the future. For us at Heritage, Rob, we want to do two things. No. 1, we want to focus on those economic matters. We want to actually provide the policy solutions for Americans' legitimate worries about affordability.

And secondly, we are gratified that still a slight majority of Americans think that socialist policies are bad. But we're worried that somewhere between 40- and 45% of Americans are willing to try those policies. We have to go explain why those policies of socialists - legitimate socialists - are problematic rather than just, as has been customary of conservative elected officials this cycle, pointing to socialists, pointing to neo-Marxists and just labeling them as that. The American people actually want answers.

And so that's a way of saying, to give you the bottom line here, that at Heritage, we're trying to keep things simple. We're trying to address the kitchen-table issues. We will continue to preach for a more restrained national security posture, which, until the Iran War, this Trump administration has done a very good job of.

SCHMITZ: So are you going to put a stake in the ground here and say whether you'll support Marco Rubio or JD Vance for 2028?

ROBERTS: (Laughter) Nice try, but that's not something we do or can do at Heritage. We don't get into endorsements. What we can say is, if - whoever the conservative standard bearer is in 2028, whoever the conservative officials are running for office in 2028, we want them to be talking about these issues that address these affordability and economic concerns.

SCHMITZ: That is Kevin Roberts, president of the conservative think tank The Heritage Foundation. Thank you.

ROBERTS: Thank you.

## Housing affordability after the midterm elections

ROB SCHMITZ, HOST:

Well, there is one thing that Republicans and Democrats can agree on. Voters are saying one thing pretty clearly - life is too expensive. And voters expect the Democrats swept into office at all levels to do something about it. The biggest item in most families' budget is housing. Could these elections make a difference there? NPR's Jennifer Ludden is here to sort through all this. Welcome, Jennifer.

JENNIFER LUDDEN, BYLINE: Hey, Rob.

SCHMITZ: So candidates all over the country campaigned on making housing more affordable, but this seems like a pretty big problem to tackle, right?
