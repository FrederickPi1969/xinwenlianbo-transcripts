SCOTT HORSLEY, BYLINE: Good morning, Steve.

INSKEEP: OK, the job market. Hiring was not so great in July, stronger in August. What about September?

HORSLEY: Forecasters think it's going to be somewhere in the middle, that employers added between 80- and 90,000 jobs in September. That'd be a slowdown from August, but not nearly as slow as we saw earlier in the summer. Sarah House is a senior economist at Wells Fargo. She thinks the job market is not stalling or accelerating. It's just kind of chugging along in a low gear.

SARAH HOUSE: You're seeing some modest hiring. The good news is you're not seeing a lot of layoffs, but it's really hard if you are one of those workers who loses their job, or you're new to the labor force or maybe coming back. There's just not a lot of turnover, so it makes it harder to get your foot in the door right now.

HORSLEY: One way to measure the strength of the job market is what's happening with wages. Average wages in August were up 3.1% from a year ago, and House expects a similar gain for September. That's not enough to keep pace with rising prices, so the typical worker is losing some buying power month after month.

INSKEEP: OK. So what is happening with inflation?

HORSLEY: It's still high, although not quite as high as it had been. This week, the Commerce Department released its inflation scorecard for August. That's the one the Federal Reserve pays most attention to. It showed prices in August were up 3.4% from a year ago. Of course, rising gasoline prices were a big part of that. A couple of weeks ago, the Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate to try to tamp down inflation, and Rebecca Venter, who monitors interest rates at Vanguard, says, investors are wondering how much higher borrowing costs are likely to.

REBECCA VENTER: The Fed has come on pretty strong, reaffirming its commitment to bring inflation back to its 2% target. Now the question is, if growth is strong, if inflation is pretty sticky, where do rates need to go for that goal to actually be met?

HORSLEY: Of course, the Fed sets short-term interest rates. Long-term rates are set in the bond market, and they have been going up at a rapid clip. The yield on 10-year treasuries is up around 5.25%, and that's pushing up borrowing costs for everybody else. You know, the average rate on a 30-year mortgage, for example, is now just under 7.25%.

MIKE REID: When you become reliant on credit to fuel your spending, there's an increasing risk that the Fed hikes are going to start to bite sooner rather than later.

HORSLEY: So far, consumer spending has been resilient, but as we said, the job market's not generating enough wage growth to keep pace with rising prices. Some people are having to borrow money to close that gap, and that borrowed money is getting more expensive.

Pike was given two full doses of the lethal injection drug, pentobarbital, and is now getting, quote-unquote, lifesaving care" at a hospital, according to her lawyers.

INSKEEP: From WPLN in Nashville, Marianna Bacallao is covering this story. Good morning.

MARIANNA BACALLAO, BYLINE: Good morning.

INSKEEP: What went wrong on Wednesday?

BACALLAO: There's still a lot we don't know, but Christa Pike was given two rounds of pentobarbital. Usually, just one is considered a lethal dose. Her lawyer, Stephen Ferrell, says that she has problems with blood draws, and that's something they had brought up ahead of time in court.

STEPHEN FERRELL: Her blood draws were difficult. She has a blood disorder that contributes to that, and we knew this was coming. So back in January, we asked that this problem be addressed in a way that would not lead to the torture that happened last night.

BACALLAO: Her attorneys are now calling on Tennessee Governor Bill Lee to commute her death sentence because they call it cruel and unusual punishment. All the governor has done so far is pause executions and call for an independent review of the state's lethal injection protocols.

INSKEEP: I wonder why he would call for that now, given that this is not the first time it's happened. The state had another failed execution earlier this year.

BACALLAO: Right. Reporters directly asked Lee that question, and he did not directly answer that question. He mainly just talked about how important it was to do a review now. He does acknowledge the state has a death penalty and that it's his responsibility to carry it out. But about Pike's failed execution, he said this...

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

BILL LEE: It's a tragedy, and it's deeply disturbing to me that that has happened in this state. The people deserve better than that.

BACALLAO: In that other failed execution back in May, medical staff could not access the veins of a man named Tony Carruthers. And the governor halted that execution. So there's a sense that one is a fluke, but two is a pattern. And speaking of patterns, this is also the second time Governor Lee has paused executions to do an extensive review of the process. He did so from 2022 to 2025. They found that the drugs used in the state's executions had not been properly inspected. The current process is the result of that review. Lee says the Department of Corrections followed this new process, and they don't know what went wrong.

U.S. Senator Marsha Blackburn, the frontrunner to replace him, says she supports the review. But she's also calling for the state to bring back the electric chair. It's still an option here for those who were sentenced before the year 2000, like Christa Pike. Her Democratic opponent, Jerri Green, says she would extend the moratorium and just have death row inmates serve out a life sentence.

Governor Lee says, even if he won't be governor, the review should take as long as it needs to take to make sure something like what happened to Christa Pike doesn't happen again.
