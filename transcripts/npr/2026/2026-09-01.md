## Made in America: How AI keeps this U.S. factory competitive

MICHEL MARTIN, HOST:

American factories are under extraordinary pressure to be perfect. They have to turn out flawless products efficiently in order to compete with rivals overseas. It used to be up to people to get those results. Now manufacturers are leaning heavily on AI to up their game. NPR's Andrea Hsu reports.

ANDREA HSU, BYLINE: At a GE Appliances plant in northwest Georgia, all kinds of advanced technology is at work, autonomous vehicles delivering parts, robots gluing together glass cooktops and a set of cameras working alongside people on the assembly line.

TONY GABBERT: And we have these things everywhere out here.

HSU: Tony Gabbert is director of manufacturing operations. He explains these cameras have been trained to check for multiple things, if the right parts are on the right ovens, if everything's aligned. And if a problem is detected, the line shuts down and loud music plays.

GABBERT: And I can't remember what this zone. It may be Metallica or AC/DC.

HSU: Before the cameras, when they had people checking for errors, it took longer to respond.

GABBERT: You go, well, what's wrong? They go, I don't know, I think that doesn't look like the right gasket. Well, this gets it right every time.

HSU: Now, the goal here has always been to minimize errors and equipment downtime and to ensure every cooking appliance passes inspection and makes it to the consumer problem-free.

GABBERT: It's a journey on perfection.

HSU: Now, with AI, perfection seems almost within reach. Gabbert walks me over to a giant computer screen.

Oh, is this Brilliant Factory?

GABBERT: Latest and greatest.

HSU: Brilliant Factory is GE Appliances' data platform. It shows in granular detail how each one of the company's nine plants is running, down to the workstation. Tony Gabbert's boss, Bill Good, is visiting from company headquarters in Kentucky. He says he loves being able to see what's happening everywhere.

BILL GOOD: In the old days, I would call my plant manager, and I'd say, how you running today? Now I'll call them and say, why are you running so poorly (laughter)?

GABBERT: And I've received those phone calls - why is Line 1 running so poorly?

HSU: Collecting data through cameras and sensors is something GE Appliances has worked on for years. But now, having AI to analyze that data and raise a red flag if something looks off, that's game-changing. AI will flag if the data shows that a motor is running hot, a sign that it might give out. They can schedule a repair and avoid an unexpected breakdown, which is ideal because halting an assembly line can cost upwards of $300 per minute.

GOOD: The name of the game in manufacturing is speed, speed at which you see the problem, speed at which you solve the problem.

HSU: Plant managers can now start their day with an AI-generated report on not just where there might be a problem, but how to solve that problem. Good says that has shrunk the gap between manufacturing veterans like him, who've seen every problem in the book, and someone who may only have five years' experience. In fact...

GOOD: It can outthink me.

HSU: This is coming from someone who's been in manufacturing for four decades. But Good is not worried AI will replace him. Quite the opposite. He says, bottom line, AI is enabling GE Appliances to manufacture in America. Even with all this technology, the Georgia plant has not cut its workforce. They actually just added 600 new jobs as part of a major expansion.

GOOD: At least in the foreseeable future, I don't see AI replacing large populations of humans.

HSU: Although, the company is using AI to optimize staffing. They've been moving workers to different tasks as needed. They're also using AI to forecast market demand, which lets them make last-minute decisions about which models to make. Good says all of this helps GE Appliances, which is actually owned by the Chinese conglomerate Haier, to compete with cheaper imports.

GOOD: You have to be faster, better, more flexible.

HSU: That's the only thing he says that neutralizes the threat.

Andrea Hsu, NPR News, LaFayette, Georgia.
