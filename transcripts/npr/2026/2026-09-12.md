## OPINION: 25 years on, a nation still mourns

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

Yesterday marked a quarter century since the 9/11 terrorist attacks. I still remember - I will always remember - walking the streets of Lower Manhattan through rows of faces and photographs pasted on lampposts and walls with urgent, wrenching words. Have you seen my wife? Last seen 8:20 a.m. Tuesday. Have you seen our daughter, our father, my best friend? The question marks pierced into you like hooks.

The attacks of September 11, 2001, were attacks on America. But America's made up of the world. The 2,977 people who were killed that day included people from more than 90 nations - Canada, India, Great Britain, South Korea, the Dominican Republic and more. That's America.

This week, I remembered a poem dedicated to the 43 members of the Hotel Employees and Restaurant Employees International Union who came to work that morning at Windows on the World restaurant atop the World Trade Center's North Tower. None of them survived the tower's collapse. The poem is called "Alabanza: In Praise Of Local 100" by Martin Espada. And it reads, in part...

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

MARTIN ESPADA: (Reading) Praise Manhattan from 107 flights up, like Atlantis glimpsed through the windows of an ancient aquarium. Praise the great windows where immigrants from the kitchen could squint and almost see their world. Hear the chant of nations - Ecuador, Mexico, Republica Dominicana, Haiti, Yemen, Ghana, Bangladesh. Alabanza.

Praise the kitchen in the morning, where the gas burned blue on every stove and exhaust fans fired their diminutive propellers, hands cracked eggs with quick thumbs or sliced open cartons to build an altar of cans. Alabanza.

Praise the busboy's music, the chime-chime of his dishes and silverware in the tub. Alabanza.

Praise the dish-dog, the dishwasher who worked that morning because another dishwasher could not stop coughing or because he needed overtime to pile the sacks of rice and beans for a family floating away on some Caribbean island plagued by frogs. Alabanza.

Praise the waitress who heard the radio in the kitchen and sang to herself about a man gone. Alabanza.

SIMON: Martin Espada reading from his poem "Alabanza: In Praise Of Local 100." Twenty-five years later, we still shudder to think of that day and pause to praise those who are gone.

## Miami crash raises concerns about Amazon's jet operator

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

This week's Amazon cargo plane crash in Miami - it's raising concerns about the little-known carrier that operated the flight. Former employees at the company 21 Air were sounding alarms about the carrier's safety practices years before the accident. The crash is also prompting questions about why the company was flying for Amazon at all. NPR's Joel Rose has been following the story. Joel, thanks so much for being with us.

JOEL ROSE, BYLINE: Hey, Scott.

SIMON: Before we start, we ought to note that Amazon is a financial supporter of NPR and pays to distribute some of our content. What do we know about the crash so far?

ROSE: Investigators have not given a probable cause yet, but they have released some evidence from the cockpit voice recorder and the flight data recorder. And that picture looks bad for the pilots. One pilot told the other that they were going too fast. There were multiple automatic warnings in the cockpit, but the pilots went ahead with this landing anyway, hurdling about 1,300 feet past the end of the runway, crashing into two vehicles on the ground. Five people were killed. Five more were injured.

SIMON: What do other pilots say?

ROSE: I talked to Ross Aimer. He's a former airline captain for United Airlines, now an aviation industry consultant. Here's some of what he told me.

ROSS AIMER: In my 60 years of flying, I've never seen such a terrible performance from a crew member. It was a very, very unstable approach from the beginning, from get-go.

ROSE: Aimer says that pilots in this situation are trained to go around and attempt the landing again. And he does not understand why the pilots in this case did not do that, though it could be a case of inexperience. The captain was certified on the Boeing 767 for less than five months, the first officer for only a year and a half. Aimer says that cargo airlines, like 21 Air, in this case, can be a stepping stone for pilots who are on their way to other airlines. But typically, an inexperienced pilot would be paired with a more experienced one.

SIMON: Joel, you talked to a former pilot at 21 Air. What did he have to say?

AIMER: Yes. I talked to Karl Seuring. He's a veteran pilot and the former president of 21 Air's pilot union. Seuring said that the carrier had trouble attracting good pilots because it did not pay as much as other carriers.

KARL SEURING: My perspective on that is if we want to get experienced pilots here, we have to be willing to compensate them. And then, unfortunately, you know, when that was lower than standard, then the guys would simply go elsewhere with opportunities that look more promising.

ROSE: Seuring - I should note - was fired in 2022 - he says, in retaliation for raising concerns about safety. He has now filed a whistleblower lawsuit against the company. As part of that case, several other former employees say they, too, were concerned and that the company discouraged them from reporting their safety concerns. The company disputed those allegations in testimony for the case. 21 Air did not respond to our request for comment, but they did give a statement to The Wall Street Journal. Quote, "our entire organization has focused on deploying the right training, protocols and procedures in all aspects of operational safety," unquote. The company also said it would evaluate the findings of the investigators in this case.

SIMON: Joel, how much does Amazon rely on companies like 21 Air?

ROSE: So Amazon has a network of contract carriers - at the moment, there are nine of them -with more than a hundred jets, according to the aviation analytics company Cirium. And that includes some pretty familiar names, Alaska and Allegiant airlines among them. Critics, though, wonder why the company is using some of these smaller carriers, including 21 Air, which Captain Ross Aimer called a, quote, "fly-by-night operation." Amazon vigorously disputed that description. In a statement, the company said that this is standard practice across the aviation industry and that Amazon contracts with carriers that are certified by safety regulators at the Federal Aviation Administration. An Amazon spokesperson said, quote, "nothing is more important to us than safety," unquote.

SIMON: What's the next step for the investigation into the Miami crash?

ROSE: Amazon and 21 Air both say that they are cooperating with the National Transportation Safety Board. The chair, Jennifer Homendy, says investigators are seeking to understand how these two companies work together, what sort of safety policies are in place. Homendy said this week that she was not familiar with the safety complaints about 21 Air, but she did urge anyone with anything relevant to share to get in touch. Typically, the NTSB releases a preliminary report within about a month of an accident.

SIMON: NPR's Joel Rose. Thanks so much.

ROSE: You're welcome.

## Neighbor turned on neighbor after Charlie Kirk’s killing. Is the divide here to stay?

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

It's been a year since a gunman killed hard-right political activist Charlie Kirk on the campus of Utah Valley University. Immediately after, there came countless stories of people who were publicly shamed, threatened or fired from their jobs because they shared opinions perceived to be unsympathetic to Charlie Kirk. The speed and scale of it was surprising, even to people whose profession is to try to protect people from harassment. NPR domestic extremism correspondent Odette Yousef reports on worries that what happened may represent a new normal.

ODETTE YOUSEF, BYLINE: In the center of UVU's main campus, there's a beautiful courtyard with a staggered, grassy terrace. Fountains fill the space with the calming sound of trickling water. UVU is a commuter school. The campus is filled with spaces where students can sit, study or scroll between classes. Collin Grannis says that two years ago, this courtyard used to be one of those spaces too.

COLLIN GRANNIS: It used to be very lively - people doing homework, people taking their food to eat outside, people to hang out.

YOUSEF: Grannis, who graduated over the summer, says it's not like that anymore. He says since Kirk was shot in this courtyard during a live Turning Point USA event, the space has emptied out. People associate it with the killing. Kevin Haslem, a sophomore, says he remembers right after Kirk was shot a brief moment of unity when the campus came together out of shock and out of concern for each other. That's passed.

KEVIN HASLEM: I feel like when people start to talk about it, when people start to say what they really think about it, there is a lot of - it disrupts the peace.

YOUSEF: At a national level, though, it seemed that there was never even that brief moment.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

JD VANCE: So when you see someone celebrating Charlie's murder, call them out, and hell, call their employer.

YOUSEF: The Monday after the murder, Vice President JD Vance sat in the host chair for Kirk's podcast.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

VANCE: We don't believe in political violence, but we do believe in civility, and there is no civility in the celebration of political assassination.

SHAUNA DILLAVOU: We were on calls for hours, for days. We were at a conference. We didn't go to a session.

YOUSEF: Shauna Dillavou is CEO of Brightlines, a doxing prevention service. Doxing is often defined as the public sharing of someone's personal identifying information without their consent. So things like a person's name, address, phone number, employer or email. But Dillavou says it's not just that.

DILLAVOU: The idea with doxing is not that the information about you exists on the line. It does. It's that someone goes and finds it and shares it with the intent that someone takes up the mantle to come after that person. So it's this call to, like, terrorize someone.

YOUSEF: And for that reason, Dillavou says doxing is often mischaracterized as just a form of harassment when, in fact, she considers it to be far more serious than that.

DILLAVOU: Doxing is stochastic terrorism.

YOUSEF: That is, a kind of terrorism that becomes more likely because a person with high reach has put out the call to target somebody. Dillavou has done doxing prevention work for the better part of a decade. Over the last two decades, she's seen the practice of doxing jump from one arena to the next. It started in China, then jumped to gaming and violent extremist subcultures in the U.S., then in the last decade to American politics.

But for regular, everyday people, Dillavou says, the likelihood of getting doxed still felt low. She says that for the most part, if you didn't draw the attention of mega-accounts on social media, like Libs of TikTok or Elon Musk or Laura Loomer or President Trump, you'd be OK.

DILLAVOU: All you had to do to avoid the pain of being doxed and being targeted this way was, like, avoid the eye of Sauron.

YOUSEF: But after Kirk's assassination, Dillavou said that was no longer true.

DILLAVOU: You didn't need to be one of the big accounts, obviously, for anyone to find you. Your neighbor found you, or your friends found you. A comment in, like, a closed group chat, that would get screenshotted and passed up the chain. And that was the big shift, like, the neighbor-on-neighbor, like, trusted-circle, inner-sanctum violence.

KAT GREEN: It's a limited pool of people who help with that right now, and everybody was slammed.

YOUSEF: Kat Green, the founder of a nonprofit called Endora, helps people who've been targeted. Green says that one of the most problematic developments after Kirk's killing was the appearance of websites devoted to outing anyone deemed unsympathetic to Kirk's death. One of these was called charliesmurderers.com. The sites invited the public to send in names and other identifying information. Then they posted it to the public. Green says hundreds of people were listed.

GREEN: So we all started working together to see what we could learn about the sites and who was behind them.

YOUSEF: Ultimately, charliesmurderers.com went offline. For many who've worked in this space, this democratization of doxing was the culmination of a decade of rising political harassment, violence and intimidation in the U.S. In the case of the post-Kirk assassination doxing, it was not a law enforcement response that ended it. It wasn't a social consensus that maybe this was bad for our political discourse. Again, Kat Green.

GREEN: That really only subsided once people started pushing back legally and once younger audiences on the internet made a joke out of it and just flooded the zone with the memeification of Charlie Kirk.

YOUSEF: Yes. Gen Alpha, internet brainrot and AI slop - content that flooded the zone with so much garbage, it became impossible to wade through or find targets to dox.

Odette Yousef, NPR News.

SIMON: And that story was made possible in part by the Pulitzer Center on Crisis Reporting.
