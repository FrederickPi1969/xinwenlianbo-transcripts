## For voters from immigrant families, voting has more weight

A MARTÍNEZ, HOST:

Young voters are often worried about their future. Will they be able to buy a home? How will they pay off their student loans? Do they have the money to start a family? The thought process is a little different for some young people from immigrant families. NPR's Saige Miller spoke with two of them.

SAIGE MILLER, BYLINE: Nicole Solis-Sison moved to America when she was 7 years old.

NICOLE SOLIS-SISON: My parents and my immediate family were fleeing government in the Philippines. So we came here to seek asylum.

MILLER: The government was so intent on going after her family that it even asked the U.S. government to deport them back to the Philippines. That's when an agent with the Immigration and Naturalization Services Agency, which has since been renamed to Immigration and Customs Enforcement, or ICE, showed up at her house.

SOLIS-SISON: That's when this particular INS agent suggested that we should file for asylum, given that we were on U.S. soil.

MILLER: After more than two decades navigating the immigration system, Solis-Sison became an American citizen last year. Now at the age of 34, she is voting in her very first election in New York.

SOLIS-SISON: This is, like, such a milestone year for me that I'm a first-time voter. I created my own voter guide and voter diary.

MILLER: Her immigrant experience and the agency that helped find a path for her family to stay in the U.S. are on her mind a lot right now as she gets ready to vote.

SOLIS-SISON: I feel like I do have a weight to protect people who are going through this process who are getting left behind and harmed, because when I tell you - I've only gotten out of it with, like, a sliver of hope.

MILLER: Twenty-four-year-old Deisy Espana of Wisconsin also feels the weight of her family's immigrant history. She's from a mixed-status family. Born in the U.S., she's always been a citizen, but her parents are from Mexico. And while she was growing up, they were undocumented. Espana said she didn't always understand the risks of her parents' undocumented status, like when her parents got pulled over while driving.

DEISY ESPANA: My mom would start, like, doing the sign of the cross or, like, praying. And, like, I never understood, like, what that fear was until I got much older and realized that, you know, they were scared that they were going to get turned over to ICE.

MILLER: Her mom recently obtained a green card. ICE has been in the news a lot recently since President Trump returned to office. Every time Espana encounters someone whose family member has been taken by ICE or reads about an ICE-related death, the same thing runs through her head.

ESPANA: That could have been my dad.

MILLER: Especially now, with the Trump administration's hard-line stance on immigration and the surge of ICE agents arresting people that look like her parents, Espana feels like her vote holds more weight. And she carries more than just her views into the ballot box.

ESPANA: I'm voting for, like, my parents, right? Yeah. I have to do it for my parents, who can't vote.

MILLER: That's her message for other young voters in similar situations - don't just vote for yourself. Vote to protect your family.

Saige Miller, NPR News.

## What's behind scary high candy prices this Halloween

JUANA SUMMERS, HOST:

For people stocking up on candy this Halloween, it is a spooky season in the grocery store. We're talking about the price for all those chocolates and gummy bears 'cause they keep going up. Here to talk about what is going on in the candy aisle are NPR's retail correspondent Alina Selyukh and Joe Hernandez, who covers food and affordability. I am already hungry. Hi to both of you.

ALINA SELYUKH, BYLINE: Hello, hello.

JOE HERNANDEZ, BYLINE: Hey there.

SUMMERS: All right, y'all, what is happening with these candy prices?

HERNANDEZ: Yeah, so glad to be here - unfortunately, just to rain on the Halloween parade a little bit. But candy prices are going up for a bunch of reasons. The cost of sugar has been high, but mainly it's a story about expensive chocolate. So cocoa harvests have been really hurt by drought and heavy rains in places like West Africa, where most cocoa grows. David Branch, who tracks the cocoa market for the Wells Fargo Agri-Food Institute, says things are actually improving, but it's too late for this Halloween because the cocoa for this chocolate was bought six to 12 months ago when prices were much higher.

DAVID BRANCH: The current price of cocoa is typically not reflected until next year's production for Halloween. The candy on the shelves right now, chocolate candy, that cocoa was purchased in 2025.

SELYUKH: Actually, just this morning, executives at Hershey had a call with investors, and they were saying cocoa prices are improving. They're on their way. But right as that's happening, there's now a new cost that they did not see coming for this year, which is a price of gas and diesel. Those prices are high because of the war in Iran, and they're making it much more expensive to ship stuff, both the supplies from abroad, but also the actual candy across the country on trucks.

HERNANDEZ: So overall, the cost of candy and gum is up 7.7% compared to a year ago. That's according to the latest federal data. And prices are up over 18% from two years ago.

SUMMERS: OK, got it. So how are people dealing with these higher costs? And I'm talking here about both shoppers and the makers.

HERNANDEZ: So the shoppers are buying less chocolate. We have data from this firm called Circana, which tracks sales in supermarkets across the country. And they're seeing pretty clearly that people are buying less chocolate candy every year for several years running, but at the same time, in dollars, the sales have been up, which means people are spending more but getting less chocolate candy.

SELYUKH: There's a bit of a flavor shift, actually, even in Halloween baskets. We're seeing more gummy candy, more sour candy. You have brands like KitKat and Reese's launching candy that's, like, coated in white cream instead of cocoa, calling it ghosts or witch's brew. So, like, it's green-colored marshmallow coating, not using the pricey cocoa.

HERNANDEZ: And those producers are watching all kinds of other costs, too, so possible new weather disruptions from El Niño. And they're also still dealing with the trickle down of President Trump's tariffs, which are affecting things like candy wrappers and packaging, for example. I talked to Enna Grazier. She owns a small-batch chocolate company in New Hampshire. She gets a lot of her supplies from Canada.

ENNA GRAZIER: A lot of the holiday packaging and some of the ingredients I needed for this holiday have a 50% tariff.

HERNANDEZ: And she says she's had to look elsewhere for some of that packaging, and because of all those costs put together, she's had to raise prices on some of her sweets over the past year and a half.

SUMMERS: So I mean, how does all of this bode for sales this season? I love Halloween, and I hesitate to ask, but is this going to be a bummer of a Halloween?

SELYUKH: So for shoppers maybe a little bit, for retailers maybe not so much - the National Retail Federation estimates that shoppers per person will spend about $115 this Halloween, which is virtually the same as last year, and that's for candy, decorations, costumes. Don't forget costumes for dogs, which is a big thing in America.

SUMMERS: (Laughter) Oh, yeah.

SELYUKH: Pumpkins and hot dogs are the top sellers this year. So the retailers are expecting another record Halloween with a total of $13.5 billion in sales. But the reason it's a little bit of a bummer for shoppers is that, like Joe was saying, people are spending a bit more to buy a bit less.

SUMMERS: OK. And just zooming out before we let y'all go, what does this say, big picture, about prices and how consumers are feeling right now?

SELYUKH: Well, prices are high, and consumers feel down, but they keep spending. Today, we got the latest data on GDP, which is a broad measure of economic activity, and it showed the economy growing at an annual rate of 3.4% July through September. And that's really strong. People's wages are not keeping up with inflation, but people are employed. They are cutting back on some stuff like travel by plane because the gas prices are making it so expensive, but they're still celebrating. People are still buying 12-foot animatronic skeletons at Home Depot. And those are, like, $300. So all of this has retailers fairly optimistic for the holiday season overall.

SUMMERS: I have to say, owning one of those massive skeletons is kind of my dream. That is NPR's Alina Selyukh and Joe Hernandez. Thanks to both of you.

SELYUKH: Thank you.

HERNANDEZ: You got it.

## New research looks at teen mental health and AI chatbot use

SCOTT DETROW, HOST:

AI chatbots are becoming more and more a part of childhood and adolescence.

ARJUN SHARDA: I started using my first artificial intelligence chatbot in around 2022, shortly after the release of OpenAI's ChatGPT.

MCKENZIE CRAIG: I started using AI in about eighth grade. It was introduced to us through our school.

DETROW: That was 15-year-old Arjun Sharda in Round Rock, Texas, and 17-year-old McKenzie Craig (ph) in Raynham, Massachusetts. A new survey finds that a majority of American middle and high school students regularly use chatbots for help with homework and for emotional support. As NPR's Rhitu Chatterjee reports, some AI use was linked to poor mental health.

RHITU CHATTERJEE, BYLINE: Arjun Sharda, the 15-year-old in Texas, says he mostly uses ChatGPT or Gemini to study for exams.

ARJUN: I use it to make myself practice sample questions, sample test questions that I will answer with my own work and help me understand concepts.

CHATTERJEE: He also turns to chatbots when he's stressed.

ARJUN: I've done that in the past, with tests, for example. I was so scared of failing one of my first chemistry tests on conversions and moles that I kept asking ChatGPT, will I be OK? Will my GPA still be fine?

CHATTERJEE: He says his 6-year-old brother is already talking to chatbots as well. Sharda's use of chatbots mirrors what the Jed Foundation found in a survey of more than 5,000 tweens and teens. Fifty-eight percent said they use chatbots for help with schoolwork. A similar number turn to chatbots when they're sad, stressed, lonely or overwhelmed. Psychologist Kayla Anderson is vice president of research and evaluation at the Jed Foundation.

KAYLA ANDERSON: We were able to look at how young people are using AI and then how that correlates separately with indicators of well-being, history of suicidal ideation and attempts and poor mental health.

CHATTERJEE: Using chatbots for companionship or when distressed was linked with worse mental health symptoms overall.

ANDERSON: Higher levels of loneliness, less resilience, history of suicidal ideation and attempts.

CHATTERJEE: But she adds...

ANDERSON: It's unclear based on these data whether AI use is contributing to poor mental health and well-being or whether students who are already in distress in certain ways are more likely to turn to AI.

CHATTERJEE: Preliminary data from other research suggests that it's likely both, says Anne Maheux.

ANNE MAHEUX: Kids who do have worse mental health are seeking out AI to help them. But that AI use may be exacerbating those mental health challenges instead of helping with those challenges.

CHATTERJEE: Maheux is a developmental psychologist at the University of North Carolina-Chapel Hill. She and her colleagues are finding that middle schoolers who use chatbots for companionship grow lonelier over time. In another study, they found similar results among young adults.

MAHEUX: So the theory here is that AI is essentially solving momentary challenges and problems for people in ways that are very helpful in the moment but are actually eroding one's capacity to navigate normative daily stressors and challenges over time.

CHATTERJEE: And that leads to worse mental health. Rhitu Chatterjee, NPR News.
