## Up First briefing: Pete Hegseth; Jim Jordan; Iran-backed terrorists

MICHEL MARTIN, HOST:

House Republicans blocked an effort to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth.

STEVE INSKEEP, HOST:

Now Congress moves on to war powers and defense spending. What can Republicans get done before Democrats take over?

MARTIN: I'm Michel Martin with Steve Inskeep, and this is UP FIRST from NPR News.

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)

MARTIN: House Republicans chose a new leader, Ohio's Jim Jordan.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

JIM JORDAN: When Democrats offer more government, we'll offer more freedom, and we will bring that fight every single day.

MARTIN: Jordan built his reputation on partisan fighting. What does his rise say about Republicans' approach to being in the minority?

INSKEEP: Also the Justice Department says three Iran-backed operatives planned nearly 20 terror plots across Europe and the United States. That includes one targeting a British airbase used by American forces. How secure are U.S. military sites? Stay with us. We've got the news you need to start your day.

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)

INSKEEP: Critics of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth missed an opportunity to put the House of Representatives on the record.

MARTIN: A lone Republican lawmaker, Thomas Massie, sponsored a resolution to impeach Hegseth over the Iran War and other issues. Every other Republican present voted to set the matter aside, and that was just enough in a House that Republicans control for a few more weeks.

INSKEEP: NPR Congress reporter Eric McDaniel was at the Capitol and is now here in Studio 31. Eric, thanks for coming by.

ERIC MCDANIEL, BYLINE: Of course.

INSKEEP: What exactly was that vote yesterday?

MCDANIEL: Well, they referred it to committee, right? It was basically a parliamentary maneuver to avoid taking up the impeachment charges and voting on them. Republicans had a simple majority of folks who were actually in attendance at the vote last night, and that was enough to quash it, right? It was the Congress version of making the bill wait on, like, an endless customer support hold.

INSKEEP: Or I'm thinking (laughter) - I'm thinking about that old "Schoolhouse Rock!" thing.

MCDANIEL: Yeah, yeah. Right.

INSKEEP: I'm just a bill. Got stuck in committee. Republicans stood in Trump's corner here - in Hegseth's corner - even after a big election loss that had a lot to do with the war in Iran.

MCDANIEL: You know, it's a really good point, and it's something I raised beforehand with a Republican lawmaker. On Monday afternoon, well before the vote, actually, I hopped on the phone with Warren Davidson. He's a Republican congressman from Ohio. And he told me he thinks this war, this conflict, is unconstitutional because it was never authorized by Congress, who, of course, have the power to regulate military conflict, but he doesn't blame Hegseth for that.

WARREN DAVIDSON: Trying to scapegoat Pete Hegseth is just a kind of status quo politics in Washington is, oh, we got to find somebody who wasn't on the ballot to take the heat for the way that the election turned out.

MCDANIEL: He argues, basically, if Congress has a problem with the war, use the power of the purse and refuse to fund it. He also would like to see the administration articulate a plan.

INSKEEP: What about the other charges here involving killing people who'd been on boats in the Caribbean?

MCDANIEL: Yeah. You're talking about these boat bombings the administration claims disrupt drug trafficking. There was also a charge for suppressing free speech after the Pentagon tried to punish Mark Kelly - he's a Democratic senator from Arizona - for a video telling service members they are allowed and, in fact, must refuse illegal orders. So a lot of different stuff in there.

Hegseth ultimately put out a video last week calling the whole effort a sham. He said, it didn't work against President Trump, and it won't work with me. Trump also helped to whip votes on this. Here's what the White House said in a statement. Quote, "the administration strongly opposes this impeachment resolution against Secretary Hegseth - a rogue, politically motivated effort to undermine our warfighters, our nation's Constitution and our national security."

INSKEEP: Somebody else seems to have helped Hegseth here. Do I understand this correctly? Some Republicans were absent. Democrats actually had enough people physically in Washington, maybe, or at least, you know, on the rolls to pass this, but some were not on the floor for the vote.

MCDANIEL: Yeah. I mean, definitely on the rolls, this was always going to be a game of attendance, like we talked about earlier this week...

INSKEEP: Yeah.

MCDANIEL: ...Once Republicans got their clocks cleaned in the midterms, it was going to be a game to see if they could get themselves back in town. And ultimately, they beat Democrats on attendance performance, as we saw in the vote. Hakeem Jeffries obviously supported this impeachment against Hegseth, so we'll see what happens now.

INSKEEP: Although this strikes to - seems like a lack of seriousness to not show up. What happens now?

MCDANIEL: So, next, Steve, these charges aren't going anywhere, but this was always going to face a steep climb. But it was always kind of a messaging effort, too, right? And there are lots of those still to come. The administration asked for an unprecedented $1.5 trillion in defense money. There's also a separate December 11 government funding deadline. There could be new war powers votes, some of which have already attracted - before the midterm elections - Republican support.

So still lots of points of leverage left coming for Iran war opponents to extract concessions from the administration, anything from Hegseth's exit to winding down the conflict or, I guess, any number of other things. But what happens next, it isn't going to happen by impeaching or removing the Secretary of Defense. Though that could change when the new Congress is sworn in under Democratic control in January.

INSKEEP: NPR's Eric McDaniel. Thanks so much.

MCDANIEL: Thank you. House Republicans are preparing for life in the minority come January, and they will have a new leader.

MARTIN: Yeah. That's Ohio Congressman Jim Jordan. He was selected yesterday as Republicans' top leader in the House after a vote by secret ballot.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

JORDAN: When Democrats offer more government, we'll offer more freedom. And we will bring that fight in committee, on the floor and on the stump every single day.

MARTIN: Jordan will replace Speaker Mike Johnson, who, after last week's midterm losses, says he is stepping down as the top House Republican.

INSKEEP: NPR congressional correspondent Barbara Sprunt is also here in Studio 31. Barbara, thanks for coming by as well.

BARBARA SPRUNT, BYLINE: My pleasure.

INSKEEP: OK. So there were two contenders to replace Mike Johnson. One is Jim Jordan. The other is Steve Scalise, who's the No. 2 Republican in the House and has been in or around leadership for many years. What was Jordan's pitch here?

SPRUNT: Well, Jordan positioned himself as a fighter. You know, he told his colleagues before the vote that offense, not defense, wins elections. He called himself battle-tested. He's a veteran of the Judiciary and Oversight Committees, two committees that are going to play a very big role in the new Congress. Democrats already have a long list of targets there. And Jordan used that in his pitch to his colleagues, you know, that his leadership experience on both committees would help him navigate those impending investigations.

INSKEEP: And apparently enough Republicans agreed with that pitch. So what does that say about the party's expected strategy?

SPRUNT: Well, I see this election as sort of a choice between continuity and change. Jordan is certainly the change candidate. He's the co-founder of the ultraconservative Freedom Caucus, which, as we all know, was often a thorn in the side of Republican leadership. Scalise is seen as more of a moderate, an extension of that leadership era of Johnson. And so the vote tells me that House Republicans wanted a clean break from that leadership era. I think folks are craving a new chapter after their massive loss last week.

I also think the choice of Jordan is illuminating when you take it alongside the placement of Stephen Miller as the president's new chief of staff. Both have a very combative approach to politics, so much so that I remember former House Speaker John Boehner calling Jordan a legislative terrorist. So the elevation of these two suggests to me that Republicans in the minority are preparing to lean into the fight rather than leaning into compromise.

INSKEEP: Just a reminder, the guy calling Jordan a legislative terrorist is himself a Republican.

SPRUNT: There we go.

INSKEEP: So the guy has sometimes criticized his own party and angered people in his own party. Now, what about the leadership elections in the U.S. Senate?

SPRUNT: Well, Senate Republicans held their own closed-door vote yesterday to pick their leaders. There was a small group who wanted to delay the vote, take more time to process the election results. But they ultimately moved ahead with the vote. John Thune, now the majority leader in the Senate, was reelected.

INSKEEP: What about on the Democratic side?

SPRUNT: Well, there'll be elections next week. The current Democratic leaders are expected to stay in place. That's Hakeem Jeffries in the House, Chuck Schumer in the Senate, both New Yorkers. It's not to say that there aren't some rumblings going on.

INSKEEP: Yeah.

SPRUNT: No one has come forward to challenge them directly for these top jobs, but there is a group of senators who have said, you know, they want new leadership. That includes Bernie Sanders of Vermont. No surprise there. Elissa Slotkin of Michigan. There are some senator-elects as well, like Annie Andrews of South Carolina, who have said during the campaign they want to see new change.

INSKEEP: I wonder if lawmakers are trying to respond to the rumblings of voters who seem unhappy with current leadership, would like to see, in some cases, Schumer go. But Schumer just coordinated a massive election win.

SPRUNT: Well, therein lies the tension, right? But I think that speaks to the challenge is that a lot of Democrats feel that they got a message loud and clear from voters, and if they don't respond in a way that reflects that sort of hunger for change in leadership, they feel that they may fall short.

INSKEEP: NPR's Barbara Sprunt. Thank you.

SPRUNT: You're welcome.

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)

INSKEEP: Federal prosecutors have indicted three Iran-backed operatives. They were allegedly involved in nearly 20 terrorist attacks and attempted attacks across Europe and the United States.

MARTIN: According to the DOJ, some attacks were directed live over FaceTime video from Iraq. In one of those instances, one of the men allegedly targeted a British airbase housing American troops. Separately, also in the U.K., British police have arrested a U.S. soldier on suspicion of spying for Russia.

INSKEEP: Willem Marx is on the line from London and will try to help us figure this out. Willem, welcome.

WILLEM MARX: Thank you so much, Steve. Thanks for having me on.

INSKEEP: OK. Starting with the charges from the U.S. Department of Justice, who are the three men charged, and what are the allegations against them?

MARX: So the three men are Mohammad al-Saadi. He's a 32-year-old Iranian Iraqi dual national; Mohsen Mohajeri, who's a 48-year-old Iranian; and Mohammad Ali al-Hassani, who's a 35-year-old Iraqi. Federal prosecutors in Manhattan say they work for Iran's Revolutionary Guard and the Iraqi militia, Kata'ib Hezbollah. Both of those are designated terrorist groups. Seven of the attacks they're accused of took place here in Britain. They included arson, explosives and a near-fatal stabbing of two Jewish men here in London.

The details are pretty extraordinary. Prosecutors say that back in April, the men sat in a room and directed one of these attacks remotely in real time over FaceTime, as you mentioned, telling the attacker when to light and throw what seems to have been some kind of incendiary device.

INSKEEP: Wow.

MARX: And the next day, one of the men, Mohammad Ali al-Hassani, allegedly told another man attacking a London synagogue to, quote, "light it up like a Christmas tree."

INSKEEP: Amazing. And then there's this plot at a British airbase, and this is the one we've heard more about. Americans were stationed at that base. What happened there?

MARX: Yeah. So that's RAF Fairford. It's west of London in Gloucestershire, the county where U.S. military aircraft personnel have based for a long time. That plot was foiled back in late September on 26 September. Prosecutors there say that al-Hassani, operating from Iran at the time, paid people here in Britain to film the base's perimeter, to buy some vans, trucks, some barrels of fuel, research chemical accelerants and then buy some metal objects that could serve as shrapnel.

Those men involved in that were stopped at a security roadblock, and here's the really extraordinary part. At that time, al-Saadi, one of these other alleged defendants, had already been brought into federal custody in New York to face terrorism related charges, and prosecutors say he used calls with family back in Iraq to pass coded messages to help fund that plot. Al-Saadi remains in federal custody over in New York. The Justice Department hasn't said where the other two men are currently being held.

INSKEEP: Wait a minute. Just to confirm, this man allegedly helped to coordinate a terror plot while he was in federal custody?

MARX: Yeah, using a phone, talking to his mother and sister and trying to get money to one of the other men.

INSKEEP: OK. Separately, then, a U.S. soldier arrested there in Britain on suspicion of spying for Russia. What happened there?

MARX: Well, we don't have a lot of details yet. He hasn't actually been named. British counterterrorism police said a 41-year-old member of the U.S. Army was arrested Tuesday under Britain's National Security Act. Police here in the U.K. say the allegations relate to Russia. Officers are searching two locations. He's now being held here in London at a police station. He's not yet been formally charged. And the head of the U.S. Army's counterintelligence command - that's Brigadier General Sean Stinchon - described him as a, quote, "senior soldier, entrusted with sensitive information" and called the case, quote, "a sobering reminder that insider threats are real and persistent."

INSKEEP: Are these various cases in any way connected?

MARX: Doesn't seem so. British police clear that the spying investigation is not linked to those Iran-related incidents, but they do, of course, raise some serious questions about security at American military bases here in Britain and the safety of people living nearby.

INSKEEP: Willem Marx reporting from London. Thanks so much.

MARX: Thank you.
