## Tennessee’s failed execution ignites a medical ethics debate

SACHA PFEIFFER, HOST:

The death penalty is legal in 27 states, and all of them use lethal injection as the primary method. That means medical professionals must store, test and inject the drugs. Tennessee's next execution is coming up on Wednesday. And as Catherine Sweeney from member station WPLN reports, some doctors want healthcare workers to be removed from the process entirely. And a note to listeners - this story describes the lethal injection procedure used in the death penalty.

CATHERINE SWEENEY, BYLINE: Back in May, Tennessee halted the execution of Tony Carruthers. A physician failed to place a central line in his chest. In a letter to the governor, dozens of doctors and nurses from across the state said the execution was horrifyingly botched and should never happen again. Dr. Robert Quinn was among them.

ROBERT QUINN: The healthcare professionals who agree to take part in Tennessee's executions are those willing to set aside their professional ethics.

SWEENEY: He says that for one thing, the Hippocratic oath says, do no harm, and ethical codes, like the one put out by the American Medical Association, explicitly bar doctors from participating in the death penalty. The letter calls on Governor Bill Lee to place a moratorium on the death penalty and overhaul the state's execution method. They don't think executions should resemble medicine or involve doctors, nurses, pharmacists, or anyone else in healthcare. Dr. John Greer also signed the letter.

JOHN GREER: We do not want to participate and should not participate in legally authorized executions.

SWEENEY: Under Tennessee's protocol, other health workers, like nurses or paramedics, place IVs into the prisoner's arms. If they can't find a vein, a doctor must step in to insert a plastic tube, or central line, in a deeper vein. Regarding Carruthers' failed execution in May, the doctor said what he experienced was torturous. The IV team tried to access veins in his arms and failed. They tried his hands and his feet. Then the doctor tried a central line in Carruthers' chest and shoulder. His attorney, Maria DeLiberato, said blood was oozing from his puncture wounds.

MARIA DELIBERATO: Tony was moaning and groaning in pain this entire time.

SWEENEY: Finally, the governor's office called the prison. The governor was ordering them to stop. He gave Carruthers a one-year reprieve. The physician in Carruthers' execution, Dr. Mark Fowler, hadn't placed a central line in 13 years according to legal records. Reached by phone, Fowler told NPR that the doctor does not participate in the execution except to declare the time of death and did not have further comment. The doctors who signed the letter don't have an execution method that they prefer over lethal injection. Greer says even asking doctors to help improve the process still leaves them in an ethical quandary.

GREER: To do this well, you'd need somebody who's well-trained and perhaps in the medical profession, but we forbid that from occurring. So there's a paradox.

SWEENEY: Some Republican state senators have also called for a moratorium on executions and an overhaul. They said the state's incompetence gives critics of capital punishment more ammunition. At a press conference this summer, Governor Bill Lee, whose final term ends in January, said he doesn't want a moratorium.

(SOUNDBITE OF PRESS CONFERENCE)

BILL LEE: Department of Correction did exactly what they should. It should not affect executions in the future.

SWEENEY: Tennessee is slated to execute a woman named Christa Pike on September 30. Her attorneys say she has a blood disorder that makes it likely she will need a central line. For NPR News, I'm Catherine Sweeney in Nashville.

PFEIFFER: This story comes from NPR's partnership with WPLN and KFF Health News.

## Takeaways from Trump's summit with AI leaders

MICHEL MARTIN, HOST:

For more on President Trump's AI summit, we've called Aalok Mehta. He directs AI projects and research at the Center for Strategic and International Studies. He previously served as the responsible AI policy lead at Google. Good morning. Thanks for joining us.

AALOK MEHTA: Thanks for having me.

MARTIN: As we just heard from Emily, President Trump said that AI leaders agreed that there should be, quote, "tremendous self-regulation" at AI companies. And he said that there's already regulation by the FBI and DOJ. Can you tell us what that might look like?

MEHTA: Yeah. So in this new accord that the AI companies signed, they agreed to four levels of oversight. So essentially, they'll implement robust internal controls. And then they will have progressively layered sets of oversight, including new measures like having an independent evaluator inside the companies and having more oversight directly from company boards. And what this is doing is building on top of a number of sort of measures that companies have already taken voluntarily to address risk from their models.

MARTIN: But - OK. But what's the enforcement mechanism, other than President Trump saying it would be, quote, "morally binding" for companies to monitor their AI models?

MEHTA: There are laws on the books. So the primary law that would apply here is that the FTC has authority to regulate unfair and deceptive trade practices. And so what happens is when a company publicly commits to doing something, they have then sort of signaled to the world that that is their intent. And the FTC can then say that if the company doesn't follow through on its promises, it is acting in a deceptive way. And so this is the primary enforcement mechanism that I'm aware of that can be used when a company says it does something and then doesn't follow through on those commitments.

MARTIN: I understand. But what is the company's incentive to do that, other than - oops?

MEHTA: They have lots of incentives. I think the biggest incentive is that they want people to use their technology. They want people in the U.S. to use their technology, and they want people around the world to use their technology. And people will feel more comfortable doing that if they trust that the product they're using is safe and that they trust the company that is issuing that product. And if you look at polling around the U.S. and in other places in the world, like Europe, right now people are very skeptical about AI. They're skeptical about - that it will make their lives better. And so part of this is driven by the fact that they want to build trust in the underlying technology.

I think part of it is also because a lot of people at these companies genuinely worry about these things. In many cases, they have access to models that are more advanced than the public. And so they're seeing things that we haven't seen yet. And so that is also part of the - part of what is driving these decisions and part of what is underlying their thinking.

MARTIN: So - well, a couple questions about that, though. More broadly, is the government equipped to independently evaluate AI model safety and effectiveness?

MEHTA: In this case, they are largely relying on the companies to sort of implement these measures themselves as a form of self-regulation. And as we heard from the Anthropic CEO, there are lots of details to be figured out. So part of the work that will happen over the next months and years is for companies to figure out how to implement this in a way that is robust and useful and actually addresses safety issues. I think the thought is maybe in the future, this could become enshrined in some sort of regulation or law, but that is not contemplated right now in the near future.

There are various parts of the government that can help provide technical insights. So there's, for example, an institute within the Department of Commerce that has technical talent and does do testing on AI models. And it's unclear how that testing will factor into what the companies are going to be implementing.

MARTIN: Well, before we let you go, I think you note - and polling has indicated - that there's a lack of public trust in this technology and in these companies at this moment. But there were no AI safety groups or independent regulators present at the White House meeting. You know, there's a saying in Washington - personnel is policy. But what does that tell us?

MEHTA: It tells us - it's really consistent with the way that the Trump administration has approached the AI industry basically since the administration started, which is that its primary concern is competition with China. And it really wants to remove obstacles to the development of AI because it - the administration is concerned that any regulations will slow down the development of U.S. technology and put the U.S. at a disadvantage.

MARTIN: All right. That's Aalok Mehta, director of the Wadhwani AI Center at the Center for Strategic and International Studies. Thank you so much.

MEHTA: Thank you.

## 'NAZA' filmmakers on what they learned making the doc and the backlash it received

LEILA FADEL, HOST:

Israel's military approved high numbers of civilian deaths for each suspected Hamas member it targeted in Gaza. That's the claim in a new documentary from two Oscar-winning Israeli filmmakers, and it features the testimonies of 24 anonymous Israeli intelligence officers and soldiers. They describe a remote targeting system that includes the use of artificial intelligence, a system they say is responsible for the mass killings of Palestinian civilians. The film is called NAZA.

YUVAL ABRAHAM: NAZA is a military acronym.

FADEL: That's Yuval Abraham, an Israeli investigative journalist and one of the filmmakers.

ABRAHAM: It stands for two Hebrew words, nezek agavi in Hebrew, which could loosely be translated to collateral damage. It's the words that Israeli intelligence officers use when describing the number of civilians that they know or they expect are going to be killed in an airstrike. So just to give you an example, one soldier says in the film, in the army, we don't say 15 women and children are going to die in this strike. We say 15 NAZA.

FADEL: And this is Abraham's fellow filmmaker Rachel Szor.

RACHEL SZOR: Palestinian journalists have been reporting this for the last years, and many people in the world know, but it's probably the first time that people from inside the system are talking and describing the systems that they've used.

FADEL: Only a few audiences have actually seen the film at festivals. Today is its U.S. release. And it's not yet available in Israel, but that has not stopped the backlash and threats. Right-wingers are calling for the filmmakers' execution. The government is threatening legal action.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

PRIME MINISTER BENJAMIN NETANYAHU: (Non-English language spoken).

FADEL: That's Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu proposing legislation that would revoke the citizenship of anyone the government deems defamed the Israeli military. I spoke with Yuval Abraham and Rachel Szor in New York.

ABRAHAM: When you hear your own prime minister saying things like that your film is like a range of ballistic missiles fired at the country, like, it's clear what he's trying to say. I mean, people who shoot missiles at a country are people that Israel will kill, you know? So it's really unhinged in a way, and it's important to say that it will not deter us. The film is coming out, even if Netanyahu is unhappy about it.

ABRAHAM: Yeah.
