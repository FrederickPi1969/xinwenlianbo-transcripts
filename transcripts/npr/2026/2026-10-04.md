## Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro face off in Brazil runoff

MICHEL MARTIN, HOST:

Brazil's presidential election will go to a runoff later this month after the right made significant gains in Sunday's national vote.

A MARTÍNEZ, HOST:

Flavio Bolsonaro, the son of Brazil's former far-right president, had a strong night. He outperformed the country's veteran leftist incumbent, Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva.

MARTIN: NPR South America correspondent Carrie Kahn joins us from Rio with more on what we learned from the first round of voting. Good morning, Carrie.

CARRIE KAHN, BYLINE: Good morning.

MARTIN: So polls had said that the results would be very close. So just how close was it?

KAHN: It was close. Flavio Bolsonaro won 47% of the vote. He's the 45-year-old eldest son of Jair Bolsonaro, who was barred from running after being convicted of plotting a coup to overturn the 2022 election. The current president, Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, received 45%. Polls had actually shown Lula with a small advantage going into yesterday's national elections, but Bolsonaro really outperformed and surprised many.

ALAN BELMANCHI: (Non-English language spoken).

BELMANCHI: (Non-English language spoken).

KAHN: He said he's been robbed three times in the last few years and lost his car. He lost everything. He said he's sick of it and says Flavio Bolsonaro will do more to crack down on criminals than Lula. Agosto Felix da Silva (ph) was sitting a bit further down the seawall, drinking a beer with his family. And he told me he's so tired of President Lula and is thrilled that Brazilians voted for a change and to get the economy growing again.

AGOSTO FELIX DA SILVA: (Non-English language spoken).

KAHN: Lula will have to find a way to close the gap in this runoff. He's not going to really be able to win over the 8% of voters who went for the other candidates. Lula will have to get new votes from those who sat out yesterday's election, and that's going to be very hard. Many Brazilians are just disillusioned with politics, and we're hoping for new younger candidates to vote for. Supporters of Lula gathered at a downtown plaza here in Rio last night, and the mood was somber. Twenty-nine-year-old Jasmine Suarez (ph) is a local DJ.

JASMINE SUAREZ: (Non-English language spoken).

MARTIN: You know, there have already been a number of right-wing victories in Latin America. President Trump has taken credit for that shift. Is that what is happening in Brazil?

KAHN: Brazil would be a huge win for the right, for sure. Lula tried to repeatedly use Trump's increasing interventions in Latin America as a campaign issue, but voters were more interested in domestic issues like crime and the faltering economy. Trump may have been part of the backdrop, but for voters here, the issues at home were more immediate.

MARTIN: That is Carrie Kahn in Rio. Carrie, thank you.

KAHN: You're welcome.

## Supreme Court starts a new term with a case about who pays for climate damage

ELISSA NADWORNY, HOST:

The Supreme Court kicks off its new term tomorrow, and it's likely to be packed with controversy. The justices will consider major cases on religion in schools, semi-automatic rifles and immigration - a central part of President Trump's second term agenda. But President Trump says that the court hasn't done enough to support his policies. Speaking on CBS' "Face The Nation" today, Justice Samuel Alito says political attacks from either party on the court can do real harm.

(SOUNDBITE OF TV SHOW, "FACE THE NATION")

SAMUEL ALITO: I think it's damaging. I think it can be quite damaging.

NADWORNY: In the first case of the new term that will be argued tomorrow, oil giants ExxonMobil and Suncor Energy are asking the justices to throw out a lawsuit brought by Boulder County, Colorado. Colorado Public Radio's Ishan Thakore has more on this blockbuster climate case.

ISHAN THAKORE, BYLINE: Paul Chinowsky has been coming to the Nighthawk Equestrian Center for years. Here, he can ride and tend horses far away from the worries of the world.

PAUL CHINOWSKY: It's just a great place to come out, and it's quiet and just unwind a little bit.

THAKORE: The center sits in Boulder's foothills, which are still dusty and dry after a record warm summer.

CHINOWSKY: Everything is just kindling. And it reminds me a lot of how dry it was before the Marshall fire.

THAKORE: The 2021 Marshall fire was Colorado's most destructive. It raced from grasslands like these well into the suburbs miles away. Chinowsky studies the costs towns face to prepare for climate-driven disasters like that. He's pegged some of Boulder's costs to adapt to fires, floods and extreme heat at well over $100 million.

CHINOWSKY: They don't have those funds to do all of these things in front of them, and that's the challenge.

THAKORE: The Marshall fire made Chinowsky's work more urgent because he survived it. Firefighters battled the blaze from his back porch. To prevent more people from experiencing what he did, he says cities are going to have to spend a lot to fortify themselves. And if they don't...

CHINOWSKY: The people who are going to suffer are going to be the next LA, the next Boulder, the next Lahaina, the next Texas floods.

THAKORE: In 2018, the city and county of Boulder sued Exxon and Suncor. The lawsuit says the companies hid the risks of burning fossil fuels. The strategy - it's similar to how tobacco companies got sued in the '90s. Boulder wants the oil companies to pay a share of its climate-driven costs. Michael Gerrard is the founder of the Sabin Center for Climate Change Law at Columbia University.

MICHAEL GERRARD: They're not seeking emissions regulations. They're just seeking money damages.

THAKORE: Opponents like Todd Zywicki, a law professor at George Mason University, say Boulder is actually trying a backdoor way to regulate and reduce fossil fuel use around the world.

TODD ZYWICKI: What this case is about is a bunch of one-percenter hippies in Boulder County who want to tell the rest of us how we have to live and want to tell poor people around the world that they should not be allowed to have access to inexpensive fossil fuels.

THAKORE: In its legal briefs, Boulder says it's not trying to limit fossil fuel use. Instead, Boulder wants to go to trial to seek accountability from oil companies. For eight years, Boulder and the companies have battled over procedural questions. The Supreme Court will not actually rule on the merits of Boulder's suit. The court is considering whether it can even be heard in state court because opponents say Boulder's claims are trumped by federal laws like the Clean Air Act. Alejandro Camacho, a UCLA law professor, said an expansive ruling that favors the companies could hamper more than two dozen similar climate accountability suits nationwide.

ALEJANDRO CAMACHO: A broad ruling against the county ends essentially all of them, and the public, frankly - all of us - end up footing the bill before the case can even be heard.

THAKORE: A ruling could also close the door on similar future cases being brought in federal court. Chinowsky wants Boulder's case to get in front of a jury. There, both sides will have a chance to ask, who will pay to prepare the county for disasters and how much exactly? For now, that's still an open question. For NPR News, I'm Ishan Thakore in Boulder.

The stakes go well beyond Brazil's borders, and the outcome could test President Trump's efforts to expand U.S. influence across the hemisphere. ## Brazil’s election could shift its ties with the U.S. and China

CARRIE KAHN, BYLINE: Joining us now is Brian Winter. He's the editor in chief of Americas Quarterly. He's a seasoned analyst of Latin American politics, who lived in Brazil for many, many years. Thanks so much for coming on with us.

BRIAN WINTER: Thanks for having me.

KAHN: Let's start with Brazil's relationship with the U.S. President Trump has made it clear that he sees Brazil as strategically important. I think you described Brazil as Trump's biggest prize. Why does this Brazilian election matter so much to Washington?

WINTER: Over the last two years or so, we've seen numerous countries in Latin America swing to the right. In fact, of the last 16 elections in the region, 13 have been won by right or center-right candidates. And of course, Brazil is Latin America's biggest country, and President Trump has gotten in the middle of some of these elections, including Brazil's. He has some personal ties with the Bolsonaro family. So this is the big prize for President Trump. He's eager to see Brazil swing right.

KAHN: But when you say he's eager to see it, what would he do with a right-wing government in Brazil now?

WINTER: You know, this is the White House that has been most focused on Latin America in 40 years - the involvement in the border with Mexico, the capture of Nicolás Maduro earlier this year, the financial bailout of Javier Milei's Argentina last year, among other examples. The Trump administration believes that Latin America is absolutely key to a couple of issues, including stopping irregular immigration and reducing the flow of drugs into the United States.

And of course, it's also true that President Trump likes having other leaders around the world who think the same way that he does and who will join at meetings like CPAC and other meetings designed to sort of show that his brand of right-wing politics is on the up and up throughout the world.

KAHN: Yet, he hasn't come out and publicly outright endorsed Bolsonaro. Why do you think that's so? If this is his big prize, why not just come out and say it?

WINTER: President Trump's story with Brazil over the last year or so has been, frankly, a bit of a telenovela. It's true, as you know, that President Trump announced these big tariffs on Brazil in July of 2025. The tariffs ended up damaging the U.S. economy, but they also helped President Lula and probably politically damaged the Bolsonaro family.

President Trump may also be hedging his bets a bit because it's also true that it's the world's second-biggest reserves of rare Earths. And there are other people in the U.S. government who are thinking less about ideology and trying to be more pragmatic, saying things like, look, this is a big, important country, and it's better if we have a more institutional relationship rather than trying to get very involved in this election.

KAHN: Let's look at the economics of this now, too, given Brazil's size. You know, it's the largest economy in Latin America. It's a member of the G20, with GDP greater than Canada or Mexico. But the economy has been faltering, and that's big on voters' minds.

WINTER: There are so many parallels with the United States. While the headline numbers for the Brazilian economy are pretty good - GDP is growing at a good pace, unemployment's at a historic low - if you talk to Brazilians and you look at the polls, people are actually pretty upset about the way things are going. They feel like their salary doesn't go as far. Affordability is a huge issue.

KAHN: Yeah. And I'm just curious - you know, China is Brazil's other largest trading partner. Could a Bolsonaro government really pivot toward the U.S. and toward Trump?

WINTER: There would probably be more alignment with the United States under Flavio Bolsonaro, especially on security - there, there's no doubt. But on economics, it's more complicated for Brazil because China has been their No. 1 trading partner for several years now. It's the market that receives most of their soy and iron ore and many of the other commodities that they produce.

And there's a track record here. Flavio Bolsonaro's father, Jair Bolsonaro, when he was president from 2018 to 2022, he talked a big anti-China game during his campaign. But once he got into office, many people, including Brazil's agricultural lobby, prevailed on him to kind of leave the status quo in place. Daniel Noboa, the president of Ecuador, also a very strong U.S. ally, just made a state visit to China. Leaders have been hesitant to choose between Washington and Beijing, no matter their ideological orientation.

KAHN: And finally, do you see Brazil's place in the world changing depending on who becomes the next leader?

WINTER: Look, Brazil is an important country. It's the world's sixth-biggest country by population. It has so much in terms of natural resources. It's home to about two-thirds of the Amazon, the world's biggest rainforest. That's critical to everybody on the planet. It's also true that under President Lula deforestation has fallen sharply. So that's important.

SCOTT NEUMAN, BYLINE: Hi, Elissa.

During my reporting this past week, I spoke with Ari Peskoe. He's the director of the Harvard Electricity Law Initiative. He says that, ultimately, everyone is shouldering the burden for the billions of dollars in new upgrades to the electrical grid.

ARI PESKOE: Everyone pays those costs. They're spread across the region to every business and resident that has an electricity meter.

NEUMAN: Now, in many cases, the pace and scale of the demand is coming at - it's really breakneck speed, and that makes all of this unpredictable. The demand for new transmission lines, substations and other infrastructure is ramping up faster than the utilities can keep up in many cases. And that creates an enormous planning challenge. But, you know, Peskoe brings up another interesting point. He says that the speed that all of this is happening can actually drive up the cost of these improvements.
