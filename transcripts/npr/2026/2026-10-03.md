## Opinion: A Hawk is not the same as a Hawkeye

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

Senator Eric Schmitt of Missouri may not have been exactly hawk-eyed at a Senate Judiciary Committee hearing this week when he asked former special counsel Jack Smith if he had gone to an Atlanta Hawks basketball game in February of 2024. Schmitt implied that Smith met up in Atlanta with a district attorney who was investigating election fraud charges against then-former President Trump.

Jack Smith said, I definitely did not go to a Hawks game. I am sure of that. And Senator Schmitt replied, I think you've already perjured yourself. Then he called him a villain and more names I'd rather not repeat at breakfast time on a Saturday morning.

Further questioning from Senator Amy Klobuchar of Minnesota established that special counsel Smith had been to a basketball game that night, but not in Atlanta. He was in College Park, Maryland, to watch the Terps take on Caitlin Clark and the University of Iowa - the Iowa Hawkeyes, not the Atlanta Hawks. Senator Klobuchar, a Democrat, suggested to Senator Schmitt, a Republican - that might be the confusion over the names of the team, but perhaps you should have looked at it more carefully. I think people should have their facts straight before they accuse people.

The names of sports teams can be confounding. If I invite you to a Panthers game, would you expect to meet me for a kickoff in North Carolina or a puck drop in Florida? But was Senator Schmitt's misstatement an innocent gaffe to be laughed off, like Vice President Dan Quayle inadvertently giving the wrong spelling for potato to a New Jersey sixth-grader, or was it a public effort to discredit Jack Smith with information that would not be hard to fact-check?

We had information, of course, about him being at a basketball game, Senator Schmitt told KTVI-TV in St. Louis the next day. My job is to ask the questions, and his job is to answer the questions. But I guess the one thing that Jack Smith and I can agree on is that we're a fan of college sports and college basketball. This is as close as the senator came to saying he'd made a mistake.

For the record, the Iowa Hawkeyes won that night, 93 to 85. Caitlin Clark scored 38 points. And I'll bet everyone who watched remembers exactly where they were that night.

## Economist Betsey Stevenson breaks down the gender gap in recent hiring reports

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

The U.S. Labor Department reports that the economy added fewer new jobs last month than expected, and unemployment rose a bit. It's now at 4.2%. We're joined now by Betsey Stevenson, a professor of economics and public policy at the University of Michigan. She was, of course, a member of President Obama's Council of Economic Advisers. Thanks so much for being with us.

BETSEY STEVENSON: Oh, it's great talking with you this morning.

SIMON: Twenty-nine thousand new jobs. What do you see in these latest numbers?

STEVENSON: Well, what I see is a continuation of a low-fire, low-hire labor market. And that is a labor market where if you have a job, you're doing OK. You can hang on to it, but it's hard to get a new job. And that hurts not only the people who need to enter the labor market or have lost their job, but it actually also hurts the people who are employed because most wage gains - big wage gains - come from changing jobs. So that was the other thing we saw in this report, is that, on average, people's wages are not keeping up with inflation.

SIMON: In recent months, a majority of the new jobs have gone to women, though in this report, it was more equal than previous months. What's going on?

STEVENSON: Well, you and I have talked about this before. So I looked at this longer trend because you're right. This month, it was more even. Fourteen thousand of the 29,000 jobs went to women. But if we actually look since July, a hundred percent of the jobs have gone to women, and we now see that women hold 50.1% of the jobs. And this is almost completely explained by the fact that almost all the hiring in our labor market is being driven by healthcare. That is a area where we still need people, and women are the majority of the workers in that industry, and it is growing. That is the engine of job growth, which is very confusing for some people because AI is the engine of GDP growth, but it has not been an engine for job growth.

SIMON: Unemployment among Black workers rose sharply - full percentage point. That is now more than double the rate for white workers. What do you see in that?

STEVENSON: Well, what I see is a series of patterns that reflect a labor market that we're not in a recession, but I wouldn't call this a strong labor market. And what happens in a weak labor market is all sorts of workers who are used to being able to churn and change jobs frequently find themselves getting left out. And when we break down the unemployment rate to groups like Black workers, we do see a lot more volatility. So when you see a big jump in a month - I always want to have a little bit of caution. That's why you're hearing some caution in my voice. But I think it's symbolic of a labor market that matches the K-shaped economy.

People who are doing well are still doing well. And any kind of worker who's struggling to get their foot in the door, keep their foot in the door, get a promotion to the next thing - those are the people who are really struggling right now. And I think that's why we're seeing this real increasing anger over the labor market, because there's no openings and breaks for people to get. I mean, obviously, there's still job openings, but what we've seen is a decline in the availability of opportunities, which has led fewer people to quit jobs. If you're afraid to quit your job, Scott, because you think you'll never find another one, then you can't create another opening for someone else.

SIMON: And that has the effect of depressing wages, which doesn't keep up with inflation.

STEVENSON: Exactly. And it's just a vicious cycle that leaves everybody sort of worse off. We're all much better off in the labor market if we can all chase our dreams, find those new opportunities. The opportunity we're leaving for something else is someone else's opportunity to get ahead.

SIMON: What other data are you looking for between now and Election Day?

STEVENSON: Well, I'm looking at consumer confidence because there's always this, you know, disconnect. People say that they are really unhappy about the economy, but we did see some job growth in leisure and hospitality and people going out to restaurants. So the question is, are they showing behavior that's indicative of being frightened? Because that will start to slow the economy down. But I do think it's time for every policymaker to have jobs and affordability front of mind, because that's certainly the front of mind of most Americans.

SIMON: Betsey Stevenson, economist and professor at the University of Michigan. Thank you so much for being with us again.

STEVENSON: I loved talking with you.

## Former foster youth with disabilities tell their stories

STEVE INSKEEP, HOST:

The numbers for children with disabilities in foster care are bleak. An NPR investigation obtained federal data and found that children with disabilities stay in foster care the longest. They're also more likely to live in institutional settings and not with foster families. To get beyond the numbers, NPR investigative correspondent Joseph Shapiro asked former foster youth with disabilities for their own stories.

JOSEPH SHAPIRO, BYLINE: Tyrone Laundersville was 9 years old the day he got into the family car, and his mother and father drove him to foster care. He didn't understand what was happening. At first, he was excited.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

TYRONE LAUNDERSVILLE: I just remember feeling, like, in that exact moment as a child being sent away, you know, all of this on a car ride, and, like, well, we're going somewhere fun.

SHAPIRO: Their destination was a psychiatric residential treatment center in Iowa. Most children in foster care live with families, with relatives or with other foster parents, but about 12% live in group homes and institutions, and those kids are far more likely to be disabled, like Laundersville, who was diagnosed with fetal alcohol spectrum disorder. At 9, his occasional screaming, aggression and property destruction were things his parents just couldn't figure out. The hope was that he'd get treated at that facility and come home. Instead, he kept getting moved from place to place.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

LAUNDERSVILLE: I started to believe that nobody wanted to deal with me. And being a child who's nonstop moved around in a facility in that situation from 9 years old until I was 18 - it's really hard.

SHAPIRO: This is from a speech. Laundersville explained how going to locked-down residential treatment centers, somewhere he needed permission just to use the bathroom, only made his behaviors worse.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

LAUNDERSVILLE: And I didn't know how to properly express myself after the many years of just not being heard. I just wanted someone to understand me and not judge me for my behaviors and the actions that I've always had my whole life.

SHAPIRO: In Massachusetts, Marissa Pike was 12 when she was sent into foster care and moved to 30 places in less than 10 years. Some were residential treatment centers and psychiatric hospitals. Pike was diagnosed with oppositional defiance disorder (ph) and other mental health conditions. One finding from our investigation was that these places use therapies that are often harmful to kids with behavioral disabilities. Like Pike, she was misdiagnosed. She's autistic.

MARISSA PIKE: I was labeled as confrontational. I was labeled as aggressive and defiant. And meanwhile, I had sensory issues.

SHAPIRO: Those sensory issues are common with autism. She felt overwhelmed by loud noise or being touched. Staff didn't understand that something as ordinary as playing dodgeball could cause her meltdowns.

PIKE: For me, it was like, OK, I just did dodgeball, and now I'm - like, I need time to decompress because dodgeball is vicious with kids.

SHAPIRO: But she didn't get the time to decompress. When she refused to come inside right away, staffers grabbed her, which only made her more upset. Many times, she was put in restraint, held down by one, two or three staffers.

PIKE: A restraint for hours or numerous restraints, which would then lead to a 911 response, and I would go to the hospital.

SHAPIRO: To the hospital because a prolonged period and restraint can be dangerous. In Baltimore, Rasheeda McCrae, who is blind, says she was placed in a house with lots of steps that made it difficult for her to get around. Her foster mother didn't know how to include her in activities with the other foster girls in the house.

RASHEEDA MCCRAE: They could see to do certain things or watch TV or go ride bikes or whatever down the street. But for me, I just stayed in my room because I felt uncomfortable and different from everybody else.

SHAPIRO: Former foster youth told us of adults who did make a difference - a foster parent, the staffer who listened or a doctor, a caseworker. Often, that key adult was someone totally outside the foster care system, like for Marissa Pike. A woman she met on a walk spotted Pike's autism spectrum disorder, or ASD.

PIKE: And it was her that inquired about ASD. She was like, my boss has a son with Asperger's, and Marissa presents a lot like him.

SHAPIRO: That new friend called Pike's caseworker. Pike says getting the proper diagnosis changed her life. Now Pike's a foster parent. Foster parent of the year in Lynn, Massachusetts, she's adopted three of those foster kids. She rides ambulances as an emergency medical technician, and she's in school, too, getting her master's degree in social work. When Tyrone Laundersville, who missed his parents in Iowa, turned 18 and left foster care, he was homeless. The mother of friends at his high school said, come stay with us. The woman was very poor. She had trouble heating her rundown farmhouse in winter. I checked in with Laundersville recently.

LAUNDERSVILLE: She taught me how to be the person that I am today.

SHAPIRO: She taught him how to cook, how to ride a bus, how to apply for a job - basic life skills he didn't learn after years in foster care. And he says she helped him finally figure out how to regulate his bad moods.

LAUNDERSVILLE: I had behaviors, still, but she lived on a farm. And she told me, if you're going to break my [expletive], go out to the barn. So I would go out to the barn and punch hay bales. And honestly, she gave me a space to where I could still let out my anger, figure out myself and how to control myself, while also still supporting me and just being that loving person.

SHAPIRO: Now Laundersville lives on his own. He's in college, too. He and the other former foster youth have one thing in common. They want to use their lived experience to make foster care better for disabled kids today.

Joseph Shapiro, NPR News.

## How Miranda Lambert learned to love smooth sounds (and write pop hits)

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

OK. OK. A country singer walks onto a disco dance floor - not a setup for a joke. Miranda Lambert, one of the most successful and respected stars in Nashville, has just released her new album, "Crisco," which, yes, indeed, is a sincere and deeply invested mash-up of country and disco. And as reporter Jewly Hight of Nashville Public Radio discovered, it is the end result of an evolution years in the making.

JEWLY HIGHT, BYLINE: When Miranda Lambert introduced herself more than two decades ago, she was a steely-eyed young Texas singer-songwriter with me-against-the-world attitude.

(SOUNDBITE OF SONG, "KEROSENE")

MIRANDA LAMBERT: (Singing) Forget your high society. I'm soaking it in kerosene.

HIGHT: Her hard-edged vision of country music was all undiluted twang and salty wit.

(SOUNDBITE OF SONG, "KEROSENE")

LAMBERT: (Singing) Watch them burn. Teach them what they need to learn. Ha.

HIGHT: Gradually, she fine-tuned her approach enough to achieve broader goals.

(SOUNDBITE OF MIRANDA LAMBERT SONG, "MAMA'S BROKEN HEART")

HIGHT: Winning over country radio, headlining arenas and earning platinum sales, critical acclaim and just about every award the country music industry hands out many times over, she managed to become a superstar without shedding her riveting idiosyncrasies.

(SOUNDBITE OF SONG, "MAMA'S BROKEN HEART")

LAMBERT: (Singing) How you look. Go and fix your makeup, girl. It's just a break-up. Run and hide your...

HIGHT: What didn't interest her at all was pop crossover, which makes 2026 an interesting time for Lambert. For one, because she had a hand in creating the biggest song of the year in any genre.

(SOUNDBITE OF SONG, "CHOOSIN' TEXAS")

ELLA LANGLEY: (Singing) She's from Texas. I can tell by the way he's two-stepping round the room.

HIGHT: Lambert co-wrote and co-produced "Choosin' Texas" by young country contender Ella Langley. And it's broken pop chart records, to Lambert's delight.

LAMBERT: If this is what brings people to country music, and they can stay a while and understand that we've been cool the whole time and they just figured that out (laughter), let's go. I love that.

(SOUNDBITE OF SONG, "CHOOSIN' TEXAS")

LANGLEY: (Singing) Way to leave. Drinking Jack all by myself. He's choosing Texas. I can tell.

HIGHT: "Choosin' Texas" is a throwback ballad that nods to early '80s urban cowboy sleekness, which means it has a few things in common with Lambert's own 2026 album.

(SOUNDBITE OF SONG, "CRISCO")

LAMBERT: (Singing) Baby, we've been cooking with Crisco. Ain't we mixing country and disco?

HIGHT: "Crisco" summons the sensibilities of an earlier era when country was open to absorbing outside influence.

LAMBERT: A country-disco record - that wasn't on my bingo card (laughter).

HIGHT: The exploration that ultimately brought Lambert there began when she partied at WorldPride in New York in 2019 with her brother and his husband.

(SOUNDBITE OF MIRANDA LAMBERT SONG, "TEQUILA DOES (TELEMITRY REMIX)")

LAMBERT: All they were playing was, like, Reba and Dolly and Loretta and all these remixes. And everyone was dancing their ass off. And I was like, I always say I want to be them, but I haven't done that.

HIGHT: In 2021, Lambert took the step of commissioning a remix of her song "Tequila Does" from Nashville producer and DJ Jesse Frasure, a specialist in bridging country and pop.

(SOUNDBITE OF SONG, "TEQUILA DOES (TELEMITRY REMIX)")

LAMBERT: (Singing) 'Cause he don't love me like tequila does, tequila does. Nobody can.

HIGHT: That kicked off an unlikely songwriting partnership. With the help of Chris Stapleton, Lambert and Frasure wrote a duet, inspired by Kenny Rogers and Dolly Parton's early '80s classic "Islands In The Stream" that set the tone for "Crisco."

(SOUNDBITE OF SONG, "A SONG TO SING")

MIRANDA LAMBERT AND CHRIS STAPLETON: (Singing) You are a part of me. Baby, you're the heart of me.

HIGHT: Lambert knows that feel-good, soft-rocking sound sometimes referred to as countrypolitan might be a surprise to longtime fans.

LAMBERT: It's not three chords and cry in my beer, 'cause that's my norm, or burn your house down. But that song just set us on a path.

HIGHT: To guide the making of "Crisco," Frasure created a playlist that he dubbed Miranda-politan.

JESSE FRASURE: Oh, we even have some ABBA, and obviously "Rhinestone Cowboy." This is the Harry Nilsson song. This is the Bee Gees all over this. Tons of Eagles vibes. I needed to have either a Dusty or Nancy Sinatra feel on it. Obviously, Fleetwood, but specific Fleetwood. So it's not all countrypolitan. Some stuff we put on this list was just to kind of help us live in the melodic sensibilities and the chord changes of that era.

HIGHT: The immersion continued in the studio. Frasure made it part of his production duties to arrive early and install disco balls. Lambert and Frasure thoroughly enjoyed getting swept up in an aesthetically exacting creative process - one that yielded extravagant silken textures, sweeping string arrangements and supreme polish. All of that listening to lush vintage sounds shifted Lambert's attention towards crafting romantic widescreen melodies she hadn't attempted before.

LAMBERT: Not with this much intention and drama.

(SOUNDBITE OF SONG, "TO EVERYTHING")

LAMBERT: (Singing) I don't care where I fit or where I'm going after. Don't know if it's right or if it's wrong.

HIGHT: Hearing her conjure dance-floor fantasies may seem like quite the departure. But it's also her way of carrying on the expansive vision of Dolly Parton and other female country stars by reaching out to her LGBTQ+ fans with this club-ready music.

(SOUNDBITE OF SONG, "SUNSET MARQUIS")

LAMBERT: (Singing) How do you feel about taking a ride? I know a place where time stands still out in California.

HIGHT: And like Parton before her, Lambert relishes the thought of inspiring drag performances.

LAMBERT: If I make it into, like, drag shows, and I'm a staple there, I feel like that's a different level of iconic. And I'm chasing it, for sure.

HIGHT: Meanwhile, Lambert's been helping an artist from country's next generation achieve a true pop culture breakthrough. She contributed mightily to Ella Langley's album "Dandelion" as writer and producer. And as Langley weathers the whirlwind that comes with its blockbuster single, "Choosin' Texas," topping the all-genre Billboard Hot 100 chart longer than any other song in history, Lambert's cheering her on.

LAMBERT: I want to sit here at home on my porch with a glass of wine and FaceTime her wherever she is in the world and hear about all of her journeys, because I went on some of those already. And I'm a little bit tired.

HIGHT: For all her success over the last two decades, having any sort of pop chart presence is as new for Lambert as dabbling in disco.

LAMBERT: You don't get very many firsts when you're 23 years in. You just don't.

HIGHT: Lambert's still looking for her next big thing. She's developed an interest in helping new artists find their footing, and not just Langley. She signs rising Texas talent to her record label imprint and packs her schedule with songwriting sessions for others.

LAMBERT: I didn't really have, like, other artists writing songs with me or sewing into my life in a mentorship way. I feel like I'm supposed to do it.

HIGHT: Frasure's seen how rare it is for a big name like Lambert to embrace a supporting role.

FRASURE: The amount of intimidation if you write with a young person, and they're like, oh, Miranda Lambert's in the room. And she has the ability to make them feel comfortable.

HIGHT: That says a lot about Lambert's mentality these days.

LAMBERT: I feel like I'm the same exact person. I just calmed down a little bit (laughter).

HIGHT: And not opposed to smoothing things out musically.

For NPR News, I'm Jewly Hight in Nashville.

(SOUNDBITE OF SONG, "TWO THINGS CAN BE TRUE")

LAMBERT: (Singing) I'm in your arms. She's on your mind.

## Art world titan buys the last remaining U.S. fine china company

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

The words fine china may evoke visions of flowery white dinner plates and gravy boats that gather dust in the dining room cabinets of grandparents across the land. Little of that finery is still produced in the United States. NPR's Chloe Veltman recently visited the last remaining porcelain factory in this country and met the art world celebrity who just bought it.

CHLOE VELTMAN, BYLINE: The new and former bosses of Pickard China are strolling through the factory together.

(SOUNDBITE OF PLATE BANGING ON SURFACE)

ANDY PICKARD MORGAN: Plates are really strong when they're vertical.

VELTMAN: The porcelain plate Andy Pickard Morgan is slamming against the top of a work surface doesn't shatter. The plate recently came out of the kiln and is waiting to be glazed.

PICKARD MORGAN: We completely vitrify our china on our bisque fire, so it's as hard as it's ever going to be now.

VELTMAN: Pickard Morgan is the fourth generation of his family to lead Pickard China, a 133-year-old manufacturer of high-end porcelain, based about 50 miles outside Chicago in Antioch, Illinois. Pickard's customers include the White House, U.S. Embassies, fancy hotels and higher-end retailers. Pickard Morgan has been at the helm for 20 years.

THEASTER GATES: You see these belts?

VELTMAN: Uh-huh.

GATES: Those belts rotate. And you mind if I push that button?

(SOUNDBITE OF MACHINE STARTING UP)

VELTMAN: This is artist Theaster Gates' seventh or eighth visit to the Pickard factory. He's the new boss, and he already knows how to operate some of the hulking, clay-splattered machines.

(SOUNDBITE OF MACHINE WHIRRING)

GATES: Loads of fun.

VELTMAN: Gates, though soft-spoken and bespectacled, is a contemporary art world titan, whose name often shows up on the industry's most influential lists. He moves in high-powered circles, counting the Obamas among his friends. And Gates has a long-term partnership with Prada, creating exhibitions, serving on its diversity council and appearing in videos for the brand.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

GATES: The work is intentionally seductive. It wants you to touch it. It wants you to hold it. It wants you to drink from it.

VELTMAN: His art installations, made out of salvaged materials, sell for hundreds of thousands of dollars and appear in major museums. The match between a hip, contemporary artist and an old-fashioned porcelain business might seem strange, but Gates' vision of what an artist is and does is expansive.

GATES: I'm interested in the most outer limits of what we might consider art.

VELTMAN: Gates himself is a master ceramicist, though he hasn't, up to this point, worked with porcelain. He focuses on Japanese and African American stoneware traditions and has worked to highlight little-known Black potters. He recently mounted a show at the prestigious Gagosian Gallery in New York about the 19th century enslaved ceramicist David Drake. Drake's story is so important to Gates, he's written and performed songs about him.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

THEASTER GATES AND UNIDENTIFIED MUSICAL ARTIST: (Singing) In my veins was all we claim, my veins...

VELTMAN: When Gates was working on a dinnerware project with Prada, he needed a large-scale porcelain manufacturer, so he turns to Pickard China. It was good timing for the Pickard family. Andy Pickard Morgan told Gates that he was thinking of retiring, and no one in the family wanted to take over. So he asked the artist if he'd be interested in buying the business. Pickard Morgan would stay on for a few years to help.

PICKARD MORGAN: What meant the most to me was finding someone like Theaster and not some turnaround company that was going to come in and lay people off and try to sell Pickard a year and a half later for a profit.

VELTMAN: He trusted that Theaster Gates wouldn't do that. Gates said, at first, he felt apprehensive about taking on this new project.

GATES: I remember getting off the phone and thinking, am I ready to totally pivot my life from a pretty posh artistic practice - where I have a lot of autonomy, I have a small team, everything is well-greased - to be a part of continuing a legacy of an artisanal manufacturer that's been doing something for 130 years.

VELTMAN: Keeping history alive is a long-term interest for Gates. He's known for restoring and repurposing defunct buildings and businesses, mainly on Chicago's South Side. One example - he created a cultural center out of a condemned bank building. The Stony Island Arts Bank now holds thousands of books relating to Black history and culture from Johnson Publishing, the company behind the trailblazing Black lifestyle magazines Ebony and Jet. Gates acquired the volumes in 2011. Linda Johnson Rice is Johnson Publishing's chairman and CEO.

LINDA JOHNSON RICE: He's someone who has a deep and profound respect for building upon legacy and preserving legacy.

VELTMAN: Johnson Rice says that's why the artist's purchase of Pickard China makes sense. But she also says breathing new creative life into dormant commercial assets is not the same thing as running an existing company.

JOHNSON RICE: These businesses are - for lack of a better word - static. And this business that he's just acquired is an ongoing business. Welcome to the real world of business, Theaster.

VELTMAN: It's fair to say that the fine china industry isn't doing well. It's been in a long decline. Philip Scranton is an emeritus historian of American industrial development at Rutgers University.

PHILIP SCRANTON: There's a kind of cultural cliff that things have fallen off in terms of this sort of commodity.

VELTMAN: Scranton says escalating manufacturing costs and the spread of casual lifestyles since the 1970s caused all of the other fine china factories that once thrived in the United States to go bust or move operations overseas.

SCRANTON: And I am not sure whether there's a way of recovering that market.

VELTMAN: The new president of Pickard China certainly has a lot to contend with. The factory needs a new roof and HVAC system, among other urgent repairs. And Gates plans to guide the creative strategy for the company while building a team to manage daily operations.

(SOUNDBITE OF MACHINES HUMMING)

VELTMAN: On the factory floor, Pickard Morgan and Gates continue to talk about their plans, like, what should they do with the ancient-looking brick kiln Pickard Morgan's grandfather installed in 1947?

PICKARD MORGAN: It was a storied piece of equipment, for sure, for a long, long time. But it's dormant now. And we have a more modern system here.

GATES: There's always this question of, should you tear it down and build it new? And then, should you invest the energy in the history of the thing? And I'm still kind of undecided.

VELTMAN: Theaster Gates says it would be fun to keep the old kiln because, he says, it's in his nature to want to hold on to granddad's things.

Chloe Veltman, NPR News, Antioch, Illinois.
