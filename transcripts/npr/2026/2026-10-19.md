## Solar power is changing agriculture in Pakistan, but some fear it could cause issues

MICHEL MARTIN, HOST:

Solar power is transforming agriculture in Pakistan. Farmers are using it to run water pumps that lower their electricity costs and boost production. But in a country already facing water scarcity and extreme weather, some fear this will create new problems. Betsy Joles has more.

BETSY JOLES, BYLINE: Muhammad Abdullah (ph) stands alongside a tub full of minnows. It's part of the fish farm he helps run in Sallhoke Chatha, in Pakistan's Punjab province.

MUHAMMAD ABDULLAH: Maripa (ph) silver, bighead grass.

JOLES: He lists off some of the species wriggling in the water below. Once these fish are mature enough, they'll be moved to man-made ponds outside. The water in them needs to be changed frequently. Otherwise, the fish will die. For that, Abdullah uses well water pumped from the ground by a solar-powered motor. The pumps run for around 11 hours a day in the summer, cutting the farm's energy bill by about half.

ABDULLAH: (Non-English language spoken).

JOLES: Only those who have a lot of money can work without solar, Abdullah says. Many in this area got into fish farming because staple crops like wheat and corn didn't grow well there. Thanks to solar, access to groundwater has become much cheaper. But Mudassar Kharal, another fish farmer from the area, says no one is really keeping an eye on how much of it is being used.

MUDASSAR KHARAL: There is no check and balance.

JOLES: Some Pakistani water experts warned that solar-powered pumps could worsen the country's over-extraction of groundwater. Bashir Ahmad, director of the Pakistan Agricultural Research Council, says their use is particularly concerning in areas that are otherwise unfit for farming.

BASHIR AHMAD: Adoption of this saltwater pumping in those areas is really scary.

JOLES: Since 2023, there's been a push to cultivate some unproductive lands under the army-backed Green Pakistan Initiative. It's mostly focused on large-scale corporate farming and promotes the use of solar-powered pumps in arid parts of the country like Balochistan. Proponents believe it will help bring farming up to date with high-efficiency irrigation. Critics say it only adds to water woes. Here's news about a thousand acres of barren land being developed under the project from Pakistani broadcaster ARY.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

UNIDENTIFIED REPORTER: Green Pakistan Initiative (non-English language spoken).

JOLES: Pakistan's agriculture industry is supported by a river-fed canal system. But the country's reliance on groundwater has steadily increased in the past few decades as pressure on farmers has grown. Researcher Khalid Jamil wanted to find out whether farmers using solar to power their pumps were extracting more groundwater than those using diesel. His Ph.D. findings, published in the Journal of Agriculture and Food Research last year, found that in 77% of cases surveyed, they were.

KHALID JAMIL: It's just human behavior. If you get something free, you do not feel the need to save it.

JOLES: That means extra stress on the already overburdened aquifer that Pakistan draws from.

(SOUNDBITE OF HORSE NEIGHING)

JOLES: The paradox is that for individual farmers using solar, water has become one less thing to worry about.

(SOUNDBITE OF ENGINE CHUGGING)

JOLES: In the Punjabi village of Kot Ratta Sakhi, farmer Ibrar Hussain (ph) still relies on diesel-powered pumps to irrigate his rice fields. Solar panels are top of his mind.

IBRAR HUSSAIN: (Non-English language spoken).

JOLES: "Everyone has put them up. We also want to put them up and benefit from them," he says. The question is whether Pakistan can afford any unintended consequences.

For NPR News, I'm Betsy Joles in Punjab Province, Pakistan.

## Rep. Don Beyer, D-Va., on Trump waiving sanctions on Russia

LEILA FADEL, HOST:

Let's get a view from Capitol Hill on President Trump's decision to waive new Russia sanctions. Democratic Representative Don Beyer of Virginia is on the line. Congressman, good morning and welcome back to the program.

DON BEYER: Thank you, Leila, very much.

FADEL: So you've called President Trump's move a, quote, "surrender to Putin." What makes it a surrender, in your view?

BEYER: Well, back to the historic mistake that Zelenskyy has just mentioned. So far, it's been like Munich in 1938, where Trump has just done his best to appease Putin again and again. But now we unfortunately passed this Lindsey Graham bill to put sanctions on Russia and give Trump basically unlimited tariff authority against anybody who wanted. And the first thing he'd done is waive those sanctions on Russia.

FADEL: Well, let me ask you about that because in the executive order, the president also says that the sanctions law on Russia and Iran, which is named for Lindsey Graham, it's supposed to cut off revenue for Russia's war in Ukraine, but actually gives him the power to waive the sanctions. That's right?

BEYER: It does, which was - we pointed out at the time this is a bad bill. But not only did he waive the sanctions, but he also announced, you know, a $4 billion purchase of diesel fuel, which was clearly illegal with the '19 - or 2022 law that said we can't buy any of that oil from Russia.

FADEL: In your statement, you call on Congress to fix this flawed law. What would that look like?

BEYER: Well, just right away, before we passed the Lindsey Graham bill, the House had already passed a bill that placed the sanctions on Russia, did not give Trump the ability to waive them, and also didn't give him this new tariff authority. We need to bring that bill back right away.

FADEL: Now, in a Truth Social post on Sunday, President Trump justified his decision by saying he wants to avoid driving Russia away from the bargaining table. Does relaxing sanctions on Russia make it easier for the U.S. to negotiate peace between Russia and Ukraine?

BEYER: That strategy certainly hasn't worked for the last 4 1/2 years. Also Trump has gone after Zelenskyy again and again, pointing out that he doesn't want peace. The news that I read says Zelenskyy has said again he will do anything he can to stop attacking Russia as long as Russia stops attacking them.

FADEL: Now, Secretary of State Marco Rubio told reporters on Saturday that waiving sanctions is about the global economy. Let's just listen to what he said.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

MARCO RUBIO: The problem is these sanctions were not just sanctions on Russia. They include tariffs on a number of countries - India, for example, and others. FADEL: Congressman, does this make sense to you?

BEYER: That's laughable. This is the same man, the same president, who's imposed, for example, 50% tariffs on India at one time, 39% against Switzerland.
