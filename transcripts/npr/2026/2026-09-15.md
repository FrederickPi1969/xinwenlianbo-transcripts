## In Florida, textbook publishers are minimizing or removing climate change coverage

A MARTÍNEZ, HOST:

Students in Florida are reading watered-down versions of some science textbooks. Authors blew the whistle in 2024, saying the state pushed back on facts about human-caused climate change. Jessica Meszaros of member station WUSF and the Hechinger Report uncovered emails showing the state's demands.

JESSICA MESZAROS, BYLINE: Climate change was swapped for changes in the environment in the Florida version of McGraw Hill's marine science book. The word polluting vanished from in front of coal-burning power plants in an environmental science text by publisher Cengage that's only available in Florida. And detailed facts were crossed out in both books, like how a World Health Organization study shows climate change is killing at least 150,000 people per year, or how college students helped their school buy renewable electricity.

Ken Miller has authored one of the nation's most popular biology textbooks for nearly 30 years. But two years ago, he says, something shifted.

KEN MILLER: The Florida Department of Education was pressuring publishers to reduce or eliminate coverage of climate change as a condition for getting on the approved list.

MESZAROS: Being on that list is required to sell textbooks to public schools in Florida. State education officials asked Miller's publisher, Savvas Learning Company, to strike the words - caused by human activity - when his text referred to climate change. Textbook publishers Cengage and McGraw Hill both say it's typical to make different versions for states while maintaining the integrity of the content. Miller says he couldn't fight his editors or publisher on the Florida changes.

MILLER: We don't want our books to be affected by political considerations one way or another. What we care about is what's going on in science.

MESZAROS: The books still cover the topic of climate change, but they minimize the usage of the term and what causes it. The Florida Department of Education said in a statement that review of instructional materials is guided by state law. Florida's Republican legislature decided two years ago to strike the term climate change from state statutes. The law did not require adjustments to climate change education. But just two months after Governor Ron DeSantis signed the bill, the Education Department started to make requests for textbook edits.

UNIDENTIFIED TEACHER #1: Now we can't teach the kids about climate change.

MESZAROS: This Central Florida middle school science teacher asked to remain anonymous and to change his voice. He's afraid of retaliation from parents, his school board and the state.

UNIDENTIFIED TEACHER #1: We're in a state that is arguably the most affected by climate change and will continue to be, and DeSantis is just pretending like it doesn't exist.

MESZAROS: A high school English teacher from the Tampa Bay region also asked to speak anonymously because school leadership told her not to speak on controversial topics.

UNIDENTIFIED TEACHER #2: There is this culture of self-censorship that I've seen developing over the past few years I've been teaching, where we're trying to predict what is going to be a problem.

MESZAROS: Now Florida is quietly updating its science standards for the first time in nearly 20 years. They still include climate change for now, so some fear climate education could take another hit. Texas has also rejected textbooks because they focus on human causes or suggest policy fixes to combat climate change. But then you have other states, like California, New Jersey and Hawaii, which want more climate content in classrooms. What may be emerging is a two-tiered system where what students learn about climate change depends on what side of a state line they live on.

For NPR News, I'm Jessica Meszaros in Tampa.

## For thousands of Haitians in the U.S., end of protected status brings fear, grief and defiance

LEILA FADEL, HOST:

For 16 years, many Haitians in the U.S. have had temporary protected status. That meant they could live and work legally, safe from the violence and natural disasters in Haiti. But now they're facing deportation after the Trump administration revoked their status this summer. NPR's Sergio Martínez-Beltrán reports from south Florida, home to one of the nation's largest Haitian communities.

SERGIO MARTÍNEZ-BELTRÁN, BYLINE: Herlain, a registered nurse from Haiti, barely goes out these days. And when she does, she changes her route. She lost her TPS in July, so that's why she asked NPR to only use her first name.

HERLAIN: You would think I was on the run. Who am I on the run from? This is crazy.

MARTÍNEZ-BELTRÁN: Her anxiety over her future in the U.S. is messing with her most deeply held values. Take one example. On a recent super hot and humid Miami day, a salesman knocked on her door and asked for some water. Herlain said no. This moment haunts her.

HERLAIN: Because that's not me. I'm a nurse. I help people. But I couldn't even give him some water because I didn't trust him.

MARTÍNEZ-BELTRÁN: Herlain, along with nearly 350,000 Haitians in the U.S., have been living in anxious limbo since July, at risk of being deported since Trump ended TPS for them. The Department of Homeland Security told NPR in a statement, quote, "temporary protected status is exactly that - temporary," adding that Congress never intended it to be permanent. On social media, DHS has been posting videos of Haitians boarding deportation planes.

REGINALD JEAN-MARY: The government knows deep down that they cannot deport 350,000 people.

MARTÍNEZ-BELTRÁN: That's Father Reginald Jean-Mary, a priest at the Catholic Notre-Dame d'Haiti Church in Miami, where he has served his community for 25 years.

JEAN-MARY: It is evident to me it is psychological warfare.

MARTÍNEZ-BELTRÁN: Father Reggie says depression is rampant among his parishioners, who fear going back to Haiti right now. The Trump administration has claimed conditions have improved in the countries it has ended TPS. But that's not true in Haiti. Their heavily armed gangs have taken over the capital and are rapidly expanding. Recently, dozens of people were massacred, including some sheltering in a church. Father Reggie admits all of this has tested his faith.

JEAN-MARY: Oh, it does. Oh, yes. You ask yourself, God, until when? Until when this is going to stop? When you look at what's going on in Haiti, and you say, God, where are you?

MARTÍNEZ-BELTRÁN: Just one day before we arrived in Miami, Pierre Damas Bel, a 20-year-old Haitian man in Ohio, died by apparent suicide after losing his TPS and being outfitted with an ankle monitor.

JEAN-MARY: We look like the forsaken people.

UNIDENTIFIED PERSON: (Singing) They shall anoint the Lord.

MARTÍNEZ-BELTRÁN: Inside the church, a few dozen worshippers gather to sing prayers around a statue of St. Michel. Some hold photos of Damas Bel. Father Reggie says prayer service attendance has plummeted. He knows of 14 parishioners who have been detained. The church adult learning school started the year with 231 students.

JEAN-MARY: And yesterday, I went to the school. It's only 17. This is a community being deconstructed completely.

MARTÍNEZ-BELTRÁN: Walking past the community center in Little Haiti and its main business corridor, it's almost hard to imagine that this area was bustling just months ago.

And I was expecting people out and about, shopping, getting some food, talking to each other. And instead, the businesses are closed. Their shutters are down. The laundromat is empty. It's just nobody. A little bit of a ghost town over here.

A popular restaurant in the area had most of its staff picked up by ICE. A nearby barber shop closed after the owner got detained. People are so afraid to go out that a food bank has opened at the church. But Farah Larreux is not hiding. She says she does not have any other option but to speak up.

FARAH LARREUX: Because I don't want to give up. I want to look at Trump in his eyes and tell him, like, how evil he is.

MARTÍNEZ-BELTRÁN: The former broadcaster came to the U.S. from Haiti after being openly critical about political corruption there. She says going back to Haiti now would be a death sentence.

LARREUX: It's temporary protected status. So you forget the second word, protect.

MARTÍNEZ-BELTRÁN: The State Department has told U.S. citizens to not travel to Haiti because it's too dangerous.

LARREUX: So what is the message, that my life has no value? Or my life has less value than the life of an American citizen?

MARTÍNEZ-BELTRÁN: She hopes another legal pathway opens up for immigrants. But under Trump, few legal options now remain for Haitians who want to continue to build a life in the U.S.

Sergio Martínez-Beltrán, NPR News, Miami.
