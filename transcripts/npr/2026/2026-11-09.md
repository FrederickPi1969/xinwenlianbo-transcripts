## Why a South Korean city is turning to a U.S. college to outsmart AI?

AILSA CHANG, HOST:

In developed Asian countries like Japan and South Korea, young students regularly outperform their American counterparts in reading proficiency. The test scores have been on the decline in recent years, and educators worry that AI could make things worse. NPR's Anthony Kuhn reports on how educators in South Korea are responding by importing an educational model from the U.S.

(CROSSTALK)

ANTHONY KUHN, BYLINE: Students play ball at a public elementary school playground. It's about 50 miles east of Seoul in the city of Chuncheon, where Seoul residents come on weekends to enjoy its mountains, rivers and lakes. Last year, the school began using an educational model based on great books.

UNIDENTIFIED PERSON: (Speaking Korean).

KUHN: In one classroom, students are discussing Mary Shelley's novel. "Frankenstein." The teacher asks, Is the Frankenstein monster human? Student Ma Yeonwoo speaks up.

MA YEONWOO: I think we can call him a human being with no soul.

KUHN: The discussion then turns to whether artificial intelligence can be considered human.

MA: Because, like, human invented AI. And AI, like, they are smart, but they don't have creativity.

KUHN: The students read Aristotle, Shakespeare and Korean author Han Kang. Ma Yeonwoo says he enjoys debating and learning from his peers.

MA: If only the teacher talks, we can have one idea, but if we all debate together, like, we can get many ideas together.

KUHN: The model comes from St. John's College, which has its main campus in Annapolis, Maryland. St. John's teachers don't lecture. There are no majors or elective courses. St. John's graduate Lee Yonghwa is a professor of English language and literature at Incheon National University, where he co-founded South Korea's first great books program in 2019. He says the students do not seek answers from external sources like the internet.

LEE YONGHWA: Instead, we engage in collective inquiry, with books and our own reasoning capacity serving as the one common ground and authority. And consequently, students must learn for themselves.

KUHN: It's a different approach from education in South Korea and much of East Asia, which, Lee says, is highly competitive and stressful.

LEE: We all feel that this system leaves students with no time to read and think. Instead, it forces them to memorize facts all day long for all sorts of tests.

KUHN: The aim of memorizing facts is to pass college entrance exams and get into the best universities. Now, Lee says, students' increasing reliance on AI is weakening even those skills.

LEE: Korean students are even losing their old capacity to memorize facts because they collect facts and ideas mainly from AI.

KUHN: And if they can't reason and think independently, he adds, they can't judge the quality of the results that AI generates for them.

(SOUNDBITE OF STUDENTS PLAYING THE RECORDER)

KUHN: In music class, students play the recorder. The great books model can't replace South Korea's test-centered system, but it can supplement it. Last year, the city of Chuncheon established an academic partnership with St. John's College. Yook Dong-han is Chuncheon's mayor.

YOOK DONG-HAN: (Through interpreter) I believe this represents a small yet revolutionary achievement, a breakthrough that challenges established practices.

KUHN: He says that although it's just getting started, students' enthusiasm for the program has already exceeded his expectations.

YOOK: (Through interpreter) I hope that as this initiative spreads, first locally and then beyond Chuncheon, it will motivate us to reflect on our existing education system and engage in fundamental rethinking amidst the rapid technological advancements of today's society.

UNIDENTIFIED SCHOOL STUDENT: An unjust law is a code that is out of harmony with the moral law.

KUHN: In a Chuncheon high school classroom, students are reading Martin Luther King Jr.'s 1963 "Letter From A Birmingham Jail." They discuss King's ideas about what kind of laws are just and unjust and which laws citizens should obey or disobey. Lee Yonghwa says he's impressed that students connected King's letter to their own country, such as in 2024, when citizens and lawmakers defeated former President Yoon Suk Yeol's attempt to put South Korea under martial law. That fits the aim of the great books model - to create more independent thinkers, more civic consciousness and more democratic societies. Anthony Kuhn, NPR News, Chuncheon, South Korea.

## Supreme Court weighs effort to shield federal law enforcement

LEILA FADEL, HOST:

The Supreme Court is hearing a case today that could determine whether federal law enforcement can be held to account when they violate people's rights. Lawyers for a prison nurse are asking justices to overturn precedent that's been on the books for half a century. NPR Supreme Court correspondent Carrie Johnson reports.

CARRIE JOHNSON, BYLINE: Five years ago, a gang riot broke out inside a federal prison in Hawaii. Kekai Watanabe was beaten with a makeshift weapon known as a lock in a sock. When he complained about serious back pain, a nurse told him to, quote, "stop being a crybaby," and the prison gave him over-the-counter painkillers. Seven months later, doctors diagnosed Watanabe with a fractured tailbone, so he sued the nurse for violating his Eighth Amendment right against cruel or unusual punishment. Today, the Supreme Court will consider whether to toss out his case and make it all but impossible to sue an individual federal law enforcement officer for damages. Steve Vladeck's a law professor at Georgetown.

STEVE VLADECK: I think what's at stake here is basically whether we, as a country, are going to hold federal law enforcement officers accountable when they violate clearly established constitutional rights. I mean, it really is, I think, that stark.

JOHNSON: It's already hard for these claims to proceed because the court requires people to show the facts in their cases are identical to precedent. In the federal prisons, the landmark case dates to 1980, and the prisoner in that lawsuit died from untreated asthma. The nurse's lawyers say Watanabe, the inmate from Hawaii, survived the gang attack in 2021, so his case is different enough that it should be dismissed. Mary Hampton Mason worked at the Justice Department nearly 35 years. Mason says rogue officers are rare, and problems are usually systemic.

MARY HAMPTON MASON: It's a result of agency culture. It's a result of training. But these failures are really institutional.

JOHNSON: The Supreme Court created a right to sue federal officers for wrongdoing in a 1971 case known as Bivens. But Mason says that's been hollowed out for years, and the court should finally overrule it because it gives plaintiffs false hope.

MASON: In fact, in the last 20 years, I'm only aware of one Bivens case that has resulted in a judgment for the plaintiffs that didn't get reversed on appeal. And that's in thousands and thousands and thousands of cases filed.

JOHNSON: But supporters of the prisoner say that number does not account for settlements or how these lawsuits can deter other bad behavior. Greg Cui is a lawyer at the MacArthur Justice Center.

GREG CUI: And that's why damages are so important, because they are a way to try to fix things, to give a person the means to pay for medical care or rebuild their life.

JOHNSON: Cui and other legal experts say the Supreme Court agreed to hear this case at a time when abuses by federal immigration agents and others are in the spotlight. Again, Steve Vladeck.

VLADECK: A world with no Bivens is a world in which federal law enforcement officers would enjoy absolute immunity, where no matter how egregious their conduct is, no one can hold them liable. As alarming a prospect as that may have been five years ago, it should be an especially terrifying one today. And it ought to be one that even this Supreme Court is worried about endorsing.

JOHNSON: Both sides agree Congress has the power to weigh in and give people whose rights have been violated by federal officers a clear way to sue. But so far, lawmakers have not advanced legislation to hold those people to account.

Carrie Johnson, NPR News.
