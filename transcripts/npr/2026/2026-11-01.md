## Supreme Court hears Colorado pre-K case on religious freedom

LEILA FADEL, HOST:

The Supreme Court hears a case tomorrow that pits the Catholic Church against taxpayer-funded school programs for 4-year-olds in Colorado. It centers on this question - can religious schools refuse to admit the children of gay and trans parents, even though that violates state antidiscrimination law? NPR legal affairs correspondent Nina Totenberg reports.

NINA TOTENBERG, BYLINE: The very first line of the Bill of Rights says, Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion or prohibiting the free exercise thereof. As early as the founding, Thomas Jefferson described the clause as creating a wall of separation between church and state. The Supreme Court formally embraced that idea in the 1940s, making it the law of the land until roughly 10 years ago. That's when the court's new conservative supermajority made an increasingly dramatic doctrinal U-turn, focusing not on the separation of church and state, but on that second clause of the First Amendment protecting the free exercise of religion. Michigan State University Law professor Frank Ravitch has written extensively about the religion clauses.

FRANK RAVITCH: It's literally the opposite of what the law was not that long ago. It is just a complete shift.

TOTENBERG: Indeed, for example, in 1983, the Supreme Court, by an 8-to-1 vote, ruled that Bob Jones University should lose its tax-exempt status because it prohibited interracial dating and marriage on religious grounds. But it's unclear that the court will reach a similar conclusion now. Professor Douglas Laycock, author of multiple books on religious liberty, says race has a unique place in U.S. history.

DOUGLAS LAYCOCK: There's nothing like 250 years of slavery, civil war, 750,000 dead, three constitutional amendments. Nothing like that for any other group.

TOTENBERG: As for the Colorado case, Laycock acknowledges that the Denver Archdiocese is discriminating.

LAYCOCK: The question is whether that discrimination is a protected exercise of religion.

TOTENBERG: Rather than focusing on separation of church and state, the current court instead has focused on the rights of religious groups when pitted against state and federal laws that ban discrimination based on race, sex, religion, etc. It's the sex part that has thrown a monkey wrench into Colorado's extraordinarily broad program to ensure that virtually all 4-year-olds in the state get a pre-K education. Steve Fenberg was the state Senate president in Colorado when the pre-K program was enacted, complete with a hefty $6,300 benefit for each child enrolled in the program every year, money that comes from a tobacco tax.

STEVE FENBERG: The law really was about implementing a program that said that we want every kid, no matter where you live, to be able to have access to universal preschool.

TOTENBERG: To accomplish that, the state did something most other states have not. It created a broad plan, including religious schools as long as the schools abide by a state law that bars discrimination based on race, religion, sexual orientation, gender identity, etc. Indeed, the Catholic Church initially endorsed the program, and Catholic charities continues to participate along with other religious groups. Again, former state Senate president Fenberg.

FENBERG: The caveat is you can't discriminate, and you need to allow all students to participate.

TOTENBERG: The caveat is where things went awry. The Denver Archdiocese and two parishes challenged the law in court, represented by the Becket Fund for Religious Liberty. CEO Mark Rienzi maintains that the Colorado law flatly discriminates against Catholic schools.

MARK RIENZI: They're only excluding religious schools that are organized around beliefs that Colorado doesn't like, and that's the constitutional problem. Not everybody has to be Catholic to show up, but they do have to agree to support Catholic teachings and not publicly contradict Catholic teachings. And that's the way these schools are set up.

TOTENBERG: Therefore, they want an exemption from the state's antidiscrimination law, which makes it illegal to refuse entrance to the school for 4-year-olds because their parents or other family members are gay or trans. Of course, not all Catholic programs march to the same drummer.

TIM UHL: I don't agree with the sky's falling.

TOTENBERG: Tim Uhl has held multiple leadership positions for Catholic school systems in seven states. He's currently the interim director of mission and ministry at Notre Dame High School in San Jose, California, and is among a group of Catholic school directors who filed a brief siding with Colorado.

RAVITCH: We do admit students that are not Catholic and families that are not Catholic. They understand that even if they don't agree with certain doctrine, that doesn't mean they're going to be disruptive. You know, diversity of opinion and diversity of thought is part of an educational experience.

TOTENBERG: That said, however, the Supreme Court's conservative supermajority, including President Trump's three appointees, have sent strong signals that they're headed towards revolutionizing religious rights in ways not widely embraced before. Last year, the court was evenly divided as to whether taxpayer-funded charter schools could include religious schools and teachings. Because Justice Amy Coney Barrett recused herself over a potential conflict of interest, the justices dropped that case. Now, the Colorado case is before the court, and Barrett has not felt a necessity to recuse in this case. Berkeley Law School dean Erwin Chemerinsky.

ERWIN CHEMERINSKY: The question is, how far will the Supreme Court go in requiring the government to pay for religious schools? Then there's the issue of will the government have to fund religious charter schools. We don't know how far the court's willing to go.

TOTENBERG: After all, charter schools were designed to be public schools that operate independently under a performance-based contract with state or local government agencies that provide the money. There are some 8,000 charter schools nationally and 276 in Colorado, and they have not, until now, been run by religious groups. Just a few weeks ago, however, a federal district judge in Oklahoma allowed a taxpayer-funded Jewish charter school, quote, "grounded in enduring Jewish values," to open its doors using $2.6 million in state funding that is projected to grow to 8.3 million in state funds by 2030. Of course, much depends on the Supreme Court's decision in the Colorado case.

Nina Totenberg, NPR News, Washington.

## Haley Stevens endorses GOP candidate in Michigan over Abdul El-Sayed

SACHA PFEIFFER, HOST:

More than 50 million voters have already cast ballots ahead of Election Day on Tuesday. And in the final days of that campaign between Democrat Abdul El-Sayed and Republican former Congressman Mike Rogers - they've been heated. Just today, El-Sayed's former rival in the Democratic primary revoked her endorsement of him to back the Republican instead.

NPR congressional reporter Sam Gringlas is in Michigan and is with me now on the line. And, Sam, this is injecting quite a lot of drama into this race at the last minute. We know there's been tension between El-Sayed and his Republican opponent - that's to be expected. Why is there also tension between El-Sayed and his former Democratic primary rival?

And while a lot of Democrats have since rallied around El-Sayed, some fractures within the party have not totally healed. This devolved into El-Sayed and Stevens trading seething statements and a last-minute joint appearance with influential Congressman Jim Clyburn falling apart. Then, this morning, Stevens instead joined Rogers at a church service in Detroit and later endorsed him on social media.

GRINGLAS: Yeah. I mean, at an early vote location in Macomb County, Jason Kerr (ph) told me he voted for Trump in 2024 and now is voting for El-Sayed. Kerr said he is frustrated with how Trump has handled his second term.

JASON KERR: I'm just surprised by some of the decisions have been made, making statements that, like, oh, things are actually not bad with inflation and stuff like that. Numbers are numbers. It's difficult to ignore facts, no matter how you feel. And that gets difficult for me when messaging isn't matching what's actually going on.

GRINGLAS: A big part of Rogers' closing statement is that El-Sayed is dangerous and un-American, casting the Senate race as, like, existential. El-Sayed is pledging to push Medicare for All and curb money in politics.

PFEIFFER: Sam, why has this race attracted outsized attention?

GRINGLAS: This race has turned into a test of how an anti-establishment candidate from the party's left flank will face in a premiere Midwestern purple state. So no matter how this turns out, expect pundits to try and extrapolate some bigger lesson, especially since Michigan will play a prime role in picking a presidential nominee in 2028.

PFEIFFER: That is NPR congressional reporter Sam Gringlas in Detroit. Thanks, Sam.

GRINGLAS: Thanks.

## Gulf region on edge as Qatar leads talks with Iran to stave off new U.S. attacks

## Democrats are running on affordability. Are voters buying it?

SACHA PFEIFFER, HOST:

Affordability is at the top of many people's minds as they vote in this year's midterms. Last year, Republicans were betting that economic issues would be on their side after massive tax cuts. Instead, so far, the cost of living seems to be driving a surge of voting in favor of Democrats - that's based on early voting reports from some states. Here to tell us about how voters are being influenced by the pressure they're feeling on their wallets is NPR's personal finance reporter Stephan Bisaha. And Stephan, we know from that famous James Carville quote, "it's the economy, stupid," that the economy often plays a big role in how people vote. But this year feels different or more intense. Tell us why.

STEPHAN BISAHA, BYLINE: Yeah. Well, what is different in this year is just how straight a line voters are able to draw between the economic pain they feel and the party in charge. It is true that people often vote based on how their wallet is doing, which can often be a little unfair to incumbents. Elected officials usually only have so much control over how the economy is actually doing. But this year, the price voters are seeing at the checkout aisle and at the pump can be directly tied to President Trump and the U.S. war against Iran, which has driven up energy prices. Take Owen Smith (ph). He's a registered Republican in Des Moines, Iowa. He split his ticket on the local level, but nationally, he voted for Democrats because of the war.

OWEN SMITH: It cost me like 80 bucks to fill up my tank, and that's just not sustainable. And it seems like it was a completely avoidable conflict that has just rocketed up our prices and mine personally. And so I don't support the candidates who are supportive of the president's policy in that area.

BISAHA: Yeah. He was fired up enough about this that two weeks ago, he got in line the first day of early voting and waited a half hour to cast that ballot.

PFEIFFER: Stephan, what about the tax cuts from last year I mentioned earlier that Republicans hoped would help taxpayers and would also help their party's prospects?

BISAHA: Yeah. That was the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, and it did help. It did tax reforms, like no tax on tips. People also generally got bigger tax refunds than past filing seasons. The average refund was about $340 more this year than last year. Republicans had initially predicted much bigger refunds, but it is still a 12% increase from last year - so a good-sized jump. But an NPR/PBS and Marist (ph) poll from September found significantly more registered voters believe Democrats would do a better job handling the economy than Republicans. So those tax refunds just don't seem to be helping Republicans at the ballot box.

PFEIFFER: And is that because taxes are one category of cost, but what people pay for day-to-day is a different one?

BISAHA: Yeah. Well, I mean, a lot of this tax windfall is being used to cover those higher costs, those higher prices. Consumer prices in September were up 3.7% from a year earlier, according to the Labor Department. The tax refunds and lower taxes Americans are paying has helped them cover some of those rising costs. And it's likely a big part of the reason consumer spending has continued to grow, and that's good for the overall economy. But Americans are not feeling good about those prices and don't like having to watch their budget so closely.

There also seems to be a lot of disappointment because President Trump got elected on the promise of lowering prices. So lower taxes in your paycheck - they're just also not as noticeable as prices on the store shelf. It's also easy to forget about the tax refund you got in the spring when you're voting in the fall. That's what Owen Smith says. He's the voter we heard from earlier.

SMITH: I do my taxes, like everyone else, one time a year. So any impact that that may have had on my thoughts at the time of voting is way in the rear view compared to the gas prices that I'm seeing every single day.

PFEIFFER: What about the real estate market? Interest rates high, house prices high, and yet, last year Republicans and Democrats came together, passed a big housing bill meant to help with that. How much, if at all, has that made a dent in housing affordability?

BISAHA: Yeah. Unfortunately not. That bipartisan bill became law in July and was really meant to show voters that their elected leaders care about housing and want to fix it. The bill was basically classic supply and demand. They wanted to encourage more home building - that boosts supply, meets demand, lowers prices. But the problem is there's so many different ideas packed into that law that it takes a long time to go into effect, so it might be years before you even feel this, like, affecting home prices.

This also gets back to what I said earlier. Washington can only do so much to affect the economy, including housing prices. The exception, of course, again, is President Trump and the war he started against Iran. That drove up inflation, which drove up the cost of borrowing, and that is making it more expensive to get a mortgage.

PFEIFFER: That is NPR's personal finance reporter, Stephan Bisaha. Thank you.

BISAHA: Thanks, Sacha.
