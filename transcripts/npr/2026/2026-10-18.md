## Trump defers federal diesel taxes to help farmers, but states issue warnings

AYESHA RASCOE, HOST:

President Trump signed an executive order at a campaign rally in Nebraska earlier this month designed to appeal to rural voters - making diesel cheaper. But, turns out, it's not so simple, and it's causing a lot of confusion, as South Dakota Public Broadcasting's Jackson Dircks explains.

(SOUNDBITE OF TRUCK IDLING)

JACKSON DIRCKS, BYLINE: At a farm co-op in Salem, South Dakota, population 1,300, Gerald Otte is topping off his big, white 18-wheeler full of soybeans. It burns diesel.

(SOUNDBITE OF TRUCK RUNNING)

GERALD OTTE: It's a lot. I mean, it's $2.50 that we're paying more. You know, we were at 3.50, and now we're at 5.89. It's a lot.

DIRCKS: This gas station is in farm country. So Otte can choose between two kinds of diesel. One dyed red is 24.4 cents a gallon cheaper. That's because it's exempt from federal highway taxes. It's the same as regular diesel, except for its color. It can only legally be used off-road, like in tractors. Trump's executive order says it's now OK to fill up highway vehicles with red diesel through the end of the year.

(SOUNDBITE OF TRUCK RUNNING)

OTTE: I think that pickup just did.

DIRCKS: But Otte says his company told him to keep using regular non-dyed diesel.

OTTE: They said I shouldn't do it because they're probably going to end up coming back and taxing us on that red fuel eventually.

DIRCKS: That's because...

MOLLY NIXON: The president really doesn't have the legal authority to say to the IRS, don't collect the tax. Don't assess the tax.

DIRCKS: Molly Nixon is a senior fellow in executive power at the Cato Institute, a libertarian-leaning think tank. Trump's order only defers payment of federal taxes. So companies like Otte's are worried if they buy the red, untaxed diesel now, they're going to get a bill for it later.

NIXON: So there's sort of a recognition here that actually getting rid of the tax might take more than any agency can do at this point - might require legislation.

DIRCKS: South Dakota's governor is also telling people not to fill up their highway vehicles on dyed diesel. He's a Republican Trump fan who's up for reelection in a very pro-Trump state. But...

LARRY RHODEN: At the end of the day, when the dust settles, we're going to take our time and make sure that nobody's caught in the backwash of unintended consequences because it is very confusing.

DIRCKS: And just like Trump is telling the IRS not to penalize people for using dyed diesel, Rhoden's directing state officials not to check gas tanks for it throughout the year. State Democrats, though, want Rhoden to call a special session to suspend the state's 28 cents per gallon diesel tax. South Dakota's state's Trucking Association sent out a flyer advising not to change fuel practices because of the uncertainty about potential tax liability.

Back at the co-op in Salem, Scott fills up a big diesel truck he's driving to a grain mill. He's not using the cheaper, red-dyed diesel either.

SCOTT: Yeah. I heard so - that Trump OK'd it, but I don't know if I totally trust on that, you know, unless they gave you a piece of paper.

DIRCKS: Scott requested we only use his first name because he said he's had negative experiences after being interviewed by the media and is concerned about his reputation in a small town. Trump has won this county by close to 70% in the last three elections, but Scott is frustrated.

SCOTT: I think it's asinine when you got to pay over a thousand dollars to fill your truck up. Yeah, I remember that happening a few years ago, but this is just ridiculous, I think.

DIRCKS: On Wednesday, the IRS said it won't inspect vehicles or impose penalties on people using cheaper dyed diesel on highways for the rest of the year. It says it, quote, "anticipates that it will offer further guidance" about postponing the tax and "explore avenues to eliminate" the requirement to pay it. For NPR News, I'm Jackson Dircks in Salem, South Dakota.

(SOUNDBITE OF OSMAN'S "LOOK AT THE SKY")

Early voting starts in parts of Florida tomorrow. ## The American Girl doll turns 40

AYESHA RASCOE, HOST:

In 1986, Pleasant Rowland launched Pleasant Company with three dolls - Samantha, Molly and Kirsten. Her vision was a series of books and dolls for girls about girls. It became an empire - a catalog, a magazine, stores, movies and hundreds more dolls - known as American Girl. Now 40 years later, there's a new chapter in OG American Girl Samantha's story. So NPR's Samantha Balaban took her Samantha doll to the American Girl store in New York to investigate.

EMILY FRIEDRICHSEN: Is she yours? Oh, my goodness.

SAMANTHA BALABAN, BYLINE: Meet Samantha Parkington. She's a 9-year-old orphan living with her wealthy grandmother in the fictional town of Mount Bedford, New York, in 1904. And her hair is a mess.

FRIEDRICHSEN: She is officially tangle-free. Hooray.

BALABAN: Samantha Parkington is, of course, both a children's book character and a doll. One of the most popular American Girl dolls sold to girls like me in the 1990s.

FRIEDRICHSEN: Going to brush out the bangs.

BALABAN: She came on my birthday, dressed in a checked taffeta dress and a big bow in her shiny brown synthetic hair.

FRIEDRICHSEN: Do you mind if we give her bangs a little trim? Is that OK?

BALABAN: Her hair is partially why we're here today, getting a fresh blowout from Emily Friedrichsen, a stylist at the American Girl salon.

FRIEDRICHSEN: We've got her nice little bow in the back, and she is looking...

UNIDENTIFIED PERSON #1: Yeah.

FRIEDRICHSEN: ...Gorgeous. Do we want to put the hat back on?

BALABAN: In the salon chair next to my Samantha is another Samantha, getting her hair styled into a twist.

FIONA DAVIS: I got my doll soon after I got the job. So she watched over my shoulder as I typed.

BALABAN: That's Fiona Davis, the author of several historical fiction novels set in New York, including "The Stolen Queen" and "The Lions Of Fifth Avenue." For its 40th anniversary this fall, American Girl asked her to write a new historical fiction book for adults. It's called "Samantha: The Next Chapter."

DAVIS: In the book, she's 25 in 1920, and she learns that her grandmother's inheritance has been stolen. She basically goes from socialite to shopgirl overnight.

BALABAN: In the cafe at the American Girl store, Davis says, while she didn't grow up with her Samantha doll, she knew about the character from her niece.

DAVIS: And she explained to me that because the doll came with these stories, you became the doll. You became Samantha. And she talked about how she was very shy when she was a young girl. And by becoming Samantha, who's a very tenacious young girl, she was able to adopt those characteristics, as well. And on top of that, she said it made her a big history nerd, which, of course, I appreciate.

BALABAN: To write Samantha's next chapter, Davis began the same way she begins every book - by researching the past.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

PLEASANT ROWLAND: Somewhere in the archives of this company is a postcard written in green ink.

BALABAN: Pleasant Rowland giving a speech celebrating the 25th anniversary of American Girl.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

ROWLAND: It said, as best I can remember, I've been down in Williamsburg this week and had an idea - a series of books about 9-year-old girls growing up at different times in American history.

AMANDA FITZSIMONS: Pleasant Rowland is beyond brilliant.

BALABAN: Amanda FitzSimons is the author of the new non fiction book "An American Girl: How A Billion-Dollar Doll And Its Unlikely Founder Changed Girlhood Forever." She says Rowland understood something that almost no one else in the toy industry did.

FITZSIMONS: Which is that girls that are between the ages of, like, 7 and 12 - they want to feel important and they want to feel catered to, and there wasn't a whole lot out there that was doing that. In fact, what a lot of marketers were doing was trying to make them feel older, but she just met them exactly where they were.

BALABAN: Each doll came with a story, which took place over six illustrated middle-grade books.

FITZSIMONS: It was the year they were 9, so it was Christmas, birthday, school. And the idea was that, you know, I'm looking at this girl and she's going through a lot of the same things I am, like this universality of girlhood.

BALABAN: Maxine Rose Schur wrote one of those original chapter books, "Samantha's Surprise: A Christmas Story."

MAXINE ROSE SCHUR: I did a lot of research in those days about Victorian Christmas - how people would decorate their homes, how they would celebrate. I had a lot of fun with it.

BALABAN: Part of the early success of the brand was the timing. Girl power was on the rise. In 1987, Congress declared March Women's History Month.

SCHUR: I think the marketing aspect was pretty brilliant, actually. Pleasant wanted to create little toys and auxiliary materials that could be sold to complement the sale of the book and the doll.

BALABAN: Schur intentionally wrote accessories into the story.

SCHUR: Maybe a certain type of little handkerchief or little music box.

BALABAN: Amanda FitzSimons says Pleasant Rowland was positioning American Girl as the anti Barbie.

FITZSIMONS: She was also marketing to parents.

BALABAN: Samantha's table and chair set was real wicker. Her ivory tablecloth was trimmed with Battenberg lace. Her lemonade goblets - not cups - were made by a family-owned glassware company in Ohio.

FITZSIMONS: The idea was really that these were things of quality for your children, that this was an investment.

BALABAN: And it sold. But the scope of the history covered in the American Girl books was limited, for the most part to white, Christian, middle- to upper-class America, says FitzSimons.

FITZSIMONS: In 1993, the company endeavored to rectify that lineup. And they made Addy Walker, the company's first nonwhite doll, and her backstory was that she was an enslaved person who escaped.

AISHA HARRIS, BYLINE: I just remember for Christmas, I had begged for the American Girl doll, and she was waiting underneath my tree.

BALABAN: Aisha Harris is co-host of NPR's Pop Culture Happy Hour and author of the 2016 Slate essay "The Making Of An American Girl."

HARRIS: It was so cool to have this doll who kind of looked like me and whose hair, unlike many other dolls that I played with, even, like, Black ones - like, her hair felt like mine. It was kind of coarse and not as straight.

BALABAN: She says Addy Walker's historical background was meaningful, and she was a fully realized and researched character.

HARRIS: There's a great detail about, like, making sure that she had sort of a space between her teeth, like, a gap between her teeth, because that's common for certain cultures, especially if you're coming from Western Africa.

BALABAN: The problem, Harris says in her essay, is that for nearly two decades, Addy was the only Black doll to choose from.

HARRIS: It's like, why is she the only enslaved person? And why is this the only representation that I have from this company? It's a very heavy burden to carry.

BALABAN: Today, there are more than 50 characters in the American Girl brand, which is now owned by Mattel. They come from all different backgrounds.

Deena Taylor is the senior director of global brand marketing for American Girl. She says the company is trying to tap into that intangible, priceless quality - nostalgia.

DEENA TAYLOR: Millennials right now, we're in that spot of wanting that familiarity of what we grew up with and wanting to pass it down to your nieces and your daughters and your friends kids so they can have that same experience.

BALABAN: And Samantha has grown up right alongside us. Maxine Rose Schur, who wrote part of Samantha's original story, says...

SCHUR: I hope she marches for women's suffrage, you know? I think she's a feisty girl. I don't see her taking a backseat to a man, for example. I hope not.

UNIDENTIFIED PERSON #2: Did you want this...

BALABAN: In the American Girl Store cafe, author Fiona Davis reads from the beginning of "Samantha: The Next Chapter."

DAVIS: (Reading) Samantha and Cornelia walked back to Grandmary's house after the tea, sharing a lively discussion about other ways they could assist the League of Women Voters. As they turned down...

BALABAN: No spoilers, but Samantha is moving into the future while remaining the same American Girl she's always been.

DAVIS: It's fun that she's not frozen in time. She's out there living her life just as her readers are. And what I wanted it to be is like you were taking a long journey with an old friend but going somewhere new.

BALABAN: Samantha Balaban, NPR News.
