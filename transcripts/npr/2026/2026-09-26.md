## OPINION: Trump's U.N. speech, a stark contrast with past presidents

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

The world took note when President Trump told the United Nations General Assembly this week that he had asked himself whether to make a deal with Iran or, quote, "do I annihilate the Islamic Republic?" "Do I drive them into hell with no chance of survival and no hope of future greatness or generations?" Those words seem especially forbidding from a leader who can command armies to march and missiles to fly.

On August 6, 1945, President Harry Truman told the world the United States had dropped the first atomic bomb on Hiroshima, Japan. It instantly killed an estimated 70,000 people. Some 40,000 more died later in Nagasaki, and tens of thousands more from radiation illnesses in the days, weeks, months and years thereafter. In his speech announcing the nuclear attack on Hiroshima, President Truman called it a harnessing of the basic power of the universe and added cautions about protecting the world from the danger of sudden destruction.

Nuclear weapons have not been used in war since, and Truman's successors have been pointedly measured when they talk about them. As President Ronald Reagan put it, a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought. When President John F. Kennedy ordered U.S. ships to blockade Cuba to prevent the Soviet Union from installing nuclear missiles in 1962, he told Americans, we will not prematurely or unnecessarily risk the costs of worldwide nuclear war, in which even the fruits of victory would be ashes in our mouth.

In 1974, President Gerald Ford told the U.N. General Assembly, a very stark reality has tempered America's actions for decades and must now temper the actions of all nations. Prevention of full-scale warfare in the nuclear age has become everybody's responsibility. Today's regional conflict must not become tomorrow's world disaster.

And in 2017, President Obama visited the Peace Park that now stands in Hiroshima and said, among those nations like my own that hold nuclear stockpiles, we must have the courage to escape the logic of fear and pursue a world without them.

All strong words from presidents sworn to protect the United States - so strong and measured, they might have felt it wasn't necessary to add threats of annihilation and no chance of survival.

## Powerful nor’easter brings winds, floods to East Coast

SCOTT SIMON, HOST:

Wild weather is affecting different parts of the U.S. Hurricane Nolo is approaching Hawaii - the fourth storm to affect the state this year. And on the East Coast, a strong nor'easter forms. It is a rare September showing of this kind of a powerful storm. NPR's Ava Berger has been covering this and joins us. Ava, thanks so much for being with us.

AVA BERGER, BYLINE: Hi, Scott.

SIMON: What can people expect from this nor'easter?

BERGER: So millions of people across the East Coast are in the path of this storm. I've already been seeing these huge videos of these huge waves crashing against the coastlines in New Jersey. It's flooding streets. We've seen big numbers of flights being canceled in the region. There's already power outages. Francis Tarasiewicz is a meteorologist at the National Weather Service in Boston. He's expecting coastal flooding, heavy rain and really high winds this weekend.

FRANCIS TARASIEWICZ: Wind gusts along the coast will be between 50 and 60 miles per hour, paired with those fully-leaved trees, as we are still in late September. We may see some instances of power outages across much of southern New England.

BERGER: And we'll see impact, Scott, from the mid-Atlantic all the way up through the coast of Maine. He said the worst of the storm is expected to hit areas in Massachusetts, Long Island, Connecticut.

SIMON: Is it unusual to have a nor'easter in September?

BERGER: It is unusual, but it has happened before. There's actually two interesting things about this storm that Tarasiewicz pointed out.

TARASIEWICZ: Really, the unique feature of it is that it's, A, long duration, and then, B, it's quite early in the season.

BERGER: So the storm is expected to last straight through the weekend - 48 hours - but we're actually not that far away from a normal nor'easter season, which is October through May.

SIMON: And at the same time, out in the Pacific, we're seeing hurricanes near Hawaii. What's happening there?

BERGER: Scientists are saying that these hurricanes are likely due to a powerful El Nino climate pattern. You may have heard it be called a super El Nino. This natural pattern - it occurs every few years - it tends to suppress hurricanes in the Atlantic while fueling more storms in the Pacific. Hawaii is bearing the brunt of this, and forecasters are saying that the big island could see up to 16 inches of rain.

SIMON: And why is that?

BERGER: So the El Nino makes the water in the eastern Pacific really hot. That warmer water can fuel hurricanes and turn them into these really powerful storms. Paul Roundy studies atmospheric science at the University at Albany. He says even scientists have been surprised at how big this El Nino is.

PAUL ROUNDY: I have heard some sense of shock. I think, for one thing, people didn't really quite comprehend how strong this El Nino event was going to get.

BERGER: So far, there's been 18 hurricanes in the Pacific - that's higher than average. And the Pacific hurricane season will continue through the end of November.

SIMON: And at the same time, I gather there's also Hurricane Polo, off of the coast of Mexico.

BERGER: Yes. And Polo is notable because of how insanely fast it intensified. In less than 24 hours, it went from a tropical storm to a Category 5 hurricane. Gabriel Vecchi studies hurricanes at Princeton University. He said, in his research, he's never seen anything like it.

GABRIEL VECCHI: I don't have any other hurricane that has increased in intensity as rapidly as Polo.

BERGER: And we're going to see Hurricane Polo through the weekend and into early next week. It could bring heavy rainfall late Sunday into Monday in the Baja California Peninsula area.

SIMON: At the same time, I haven't heard much about Atlantic hurricanes.

BERGER: That's because it's totally different in the Atlantic, especially during an El Nino. There have actually been no full-blown hurricanes yet this season in the Atlantic. That's the first time that's happened in more than a century. And during El Nino years, there's usually more wind shear in the Atlantic, so that's a change in wind speed and direction that can tear apart storms before they get more powerful.

SIMON: And is there a connection between the big El Nino we're experiencing and this nor'easter?

BERGER: The El Nino's effect on hurricanes is well understood, but we don't know as much, Scott, about how it could affect a nor'easter. Vecchi told me that he's not sure if there's a connection with these two storms, but an El Nino this big is probably impacting weather events in some way across the Pacific and the Atlantic.

SIMON: NPR's Ava Berger. Thanks so much for being with us.

BERGER: Thank you.
