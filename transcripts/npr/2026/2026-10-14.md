Zelenskyy says Ukraine has not received intelligence on Russian ballistic missile strikes over the last two nights.

MARTIN: NPR's Ukraine correspondent Joanna Kakissis spoke to President Zelenskyy in Kyiv on Tuesday and is with us now to tell us more about it. Hello.

KAKISSIS: Hello, Michel.

MARTIN: So what did Zelenskyy tell you about why intelligence-sharing is so important for Ukraine right now?

ZELENSKYY: And we had five ballistic missiles. The direction was Kyiv - Kyiv region.

KAKISSIS: Yeah.

ZELENSKYY: Yes. They targeted mostly railway, and we didn't get the information.

KAKISSIS: You did not get the information?

ZELENSKYY: No, we didn't get the information about ballistic missiles. This is really our weak place.

KAKISSIS: He said his military was told it was a technical glitch. I asked him if it was the U.S. that was withholding intel, and he did not want to elaborate, saying it was a sensitive matter. But he also said Ukraine has diversified its foreign intelligence sources after early 2025, when the Trump administration had paused sharing information with Kyiv.

ZELENSKYY: We get from the United States very necessary information, but not only from the United States.

KAKISSIS: Now, Zelenskyy says every bit of intelligence is important to Ukraine because stopping ballistic missiles is a priority.

MARTIN: Pope Leo has lung cancer. The Vatican shared the diagnosis on Tuesday after the pope had surgery at a hospital in Rome.

INSKEEP: The Vatican says he spent a peaceful night recovering. They are promising to release more information about his condition later today.

MARTIN: All Things Considered host Scott Detrow is the co-author of a new biography of Pope Leo that came out last week, and he's been following up on this latest chapter in the pontiff's life story. Hi, Scott.

SCOTT DETROW, BYLINE: Good morning, Michel.

MARTIN: So what do we know about the Pope's condition?

DETROW: The operation lasted about 90 minutes, and doctors removed the lower lobe of Pope Leo's right lung. So the next step is waiting to see what the pathology tests reveal about the exact nature of the cancer. But we talked to several lung experts who say that from what the Vatican has said, they're actually optimistic. Dr. Vin Gupta from the University of Washington says that the course of action that doctors took does suggest that they did catch it early and that the cancer has not spread.

VIN GUPTA: And so the fact that surgery was the first tool that they utilized is a really good sign because it suggests that they think this is early stage. It hasn't spread to, say, lymph nodes.

DETROW: And again, still waiting for that pathology report. But he says that if that is the case and if doctors monitor Leo regularly, there is a chance this really wouldn't affect his lifespan at all. Many people live without two full lungs, and one person who lived decades without them was actually Pope Francis. He had part of a lung removed at a young age.

MARTIN: So what comes next?

DETROW: Leo's going to remain hospitalized for several days. His schedule has been cleared for at least a week. This can be a painful surgery to recover from in the short term. But doctors say within two weeks, patients are often back to normal routines in many ways. That matters to Leo because he has a major trip scheduled to South America, including Peru, where he spent about 20 years of his life, in less than a month. And a Vatican official told me this surgery was actually purposely scheduled for now to give the pope time to recover and still make the trip if he can. They won't make that decision until they get more data and see how he's doing, but it's still a possibility he goes.

MARTIN: So do we know whether Pope Leo ever smoked?

DETROW: You know, obviously the big question when lung cancer is the diagnosis. I'll say I spent a year reporting on his life, talking to more than a hundred people who know him well, looking at pictures from all ages of his life, and I got no indication that he ever smoked. He's a healthy guy. He's active. He still plays tennis once a week.

But cancer does run in his family. His brother Louis is actually hospitalized right now, and Leo's mother, Mildred, died of cancer. She fought it for a long time. And in fact, reporting the book, we found a letter that the future pope wrote to friends in 1984, which was several years before she died. He writes in the letter, (reading) my mother continues to undergo chemotherapy treatments. She's a tough lady and handles it all well, which makes it a little easier for me, being so far from Chicago.

MARTIN: So, Scott, given all your reporting, do you have any sense of how Leo is reacting to this?

DETROW: I've been talking to several of his friends who have been in touch with him, and they say he's as responsive as ever. He's in good spirits. He's writing back to their notes right away. One constant theme in his life, going back to his seminary years, is a real trust in God and acceptance that he is not in charge of what happens, good or bad, that it's part of a plan that God has. And you've seen Leo really lean into that in big positive moments in his life, like his election to pope, but in a lot of challenging moments, too. And I have to think that that is primarily how he is processing this news.

MARTIN: That is All Things Considered host Scott Detrow, who co-authored a new biography of Pope Leo. It's just out. It's called "American Pope." Scott, thank you so much.

DETROW: Thanks for having me.

MARTIN: President Trump has forced out his ambassador to Greece, Kimberly Guilfoyle. She is a prominent MAGA figure. She had a lengthy engagement to Trump's oldest son until the relationship ended.

INSKEEP: Guilfoyle faced unflattering stories about trying to profit from her position. Now, the president and his family have profited in many ways during his second term. In this case, someone who was no longer on her way into the Trump family was accused of demanding money for herself, which she denies.

MARTIN: NPR's diplomatic correspondent Michele Kelemen is following this story and is with us now to tell us more about it. Good morning, Michele.

MICHELE KELEMEN, BYLINE: Good morning, Michel.

MARTIN: So tell us what led to this removal.

KELEMEN: So The Wall Street Journal had a series of recent stories about Guilfoyle. And there were many allegations of misconduct, including that she asked a Trump donor for $100,000 to pay off a credit card bill in exchange for using her contacts within the administration. Democrats on Capitol Hill started writing letters to Secretary of State Marco Rubio calling for investigations, and this was happening while Rubio was in Athens. And he sounded annoyed when reporters asked him about it last week, as you can hear.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

MARCO RUBIO: We don't conduct these things in a press conference on a tarmac in a foreign country because you wrote a story or somebody else wrote a story 'cause some source gave it to you. So get that out of your head, 'cause that's never the way it's going to work with me. It's a game that's been played for a long time, but it's not a game we're going to play.

KELEMEN: But fast-forward to yesterday, when President Trump called for her to resign, saying he didn't like what he was reading about her. He said he didn't even talk to Guilfoyle, who announced on X that her time as ambassador is coming to an end.

MARTIN: So is this the end of the story?

KELEMEN: Not if Democrats have their way. Gregory Meeks, who's on the House Foreign Affairs Committee, says her resignation is not a substitute for accountability. He thinks that Congress and the inspector general need to conduct more oversight into her conduct and potential abuse of taxpayer dollars. Serving as an ambassador is a position of public trust, he told NPR in a statement, not a business opportunity. I also talked to retired career diplomat Eric Rubin, who says this looks really bad for the U.S. Take a listen.

ERIC RUBIN: I think Europeans are snickering. I think there are people in Greece who are snickering, and that's not good for us. We should have somebody who's respected and who carries the weight of the position.

KELEMEN: Guilfoyle says she worked tirelessly to advance Trump's America First agenda in Greece. She dismissed the allegations against her, saying she intends to vigorously defend her name, reputation and integrity.

MARTIN: And what does this mean for the embassy?

KELEMEN: Yeah. The State Department says there's an experienced deputy chief of mission who's ready to assume the role. That's happening in a lot of places around the world, Michel, where President Trump has not even named an ambassador. He's named very few career diplomats, and that's something that Rubin is really worried about.

RUBIN: We've always had political appointees as ambassadors. We've always had some with no apparent qualifications. But from a historical trend, which included the first Trump administration, of having at least 60% or so of our ambassadors be career professionals, we're now down to about 5%.

KELEMEN: Just a tiny fraction. And many embassies are run by, you know, the deputies, as I said. You know, all ambassadors, career and political, have to be vetted. They have to get through Senate confirmation. And that could get a lot tougher for Trump political appointees after all this news about Kimberly Guilfoyle.

MARTIN: NPR's Michele Kelemen. Michele, thank you.

KELEMEN: Thank you.
