## [Remarks] Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Indian External Affairs Minister Dr. S. Jaishankar at the Freedom 250 Reception — Marco Rubio

Ambassador Sergio Gor, U.S. Special Envoy for South and Central Asia

AMBASSADOR GOR:  Good evening, everybody, and welcome to this great celebration.  Secretary Rubio, Mrs. Rubio, Dr. Jaishankar, Secretary Misri, Chairman Jason Smith, distinguished guests, friends, and colleagues, it is a true privilege to be here with you as we celebrate 250 years of American independence.

There is no better place to mark this milestone than right here in India’s financial and cultural capital.  Looking out at this room, I see the leaders, innovators, and risk-takers who provide the drive and capital that power both of our nations.  More than 500 major American companies operate right here in beautiful Mumbai: tech giants like Google, Meta, and IBM; trusted household names like Procter & Gamble and Johnson & Johnson; pharmaceutical leaders like Pfizer, Abbott, Merck; and the world’s premier financial institution, including Citi, Bank of America, and JPMorganChase.

On Mumbai’s stock exchanges, American institutional investors represent the single largest bloc of foreign capital invested in Indian equities.  That is not an accident.  That is a massive vote of confidence in India’s economic trajectory.  None of this happened by accident, and none of it was built overnight.

Immediately following the American Revolution, U.S. merchant vessels set sail for Mumbai, trading American goods for Indian spices, textiles, and teakwood.  In 1792, President George Washington appointed our first consul to India.  And in 1838, President Martin Van Buren commissioned Philemon Parker as our first consul right here in Mumbai.  That represents 188 years of continuous diplomatic engagement and 250 years of commercial trade.  It proves that lasting partnerships are forged by people who cross oceans, take risks, innovate, and build something substantial.

Mr. Secretary, having you and the great Jeanette Rubio back in India tonight underscores this steadfast emphasis the United States places on this relationship.  When President Donald Trump sent me to India, he gave me a direct mandate: deliver results.  Don’t just talk about the future; make it happen.  In their recent discussions, President Trump and Prime Minister Modi reaffirmed that commitment.

Today, our bilateral trade has shattered records, reaching over $240 billion.  (Applause.)  And we have an ambitious goal.  We are laser-focused on driving that number past $500 billion in the next three years.  (Applause.)

The commercial frontier ahead of us is unmatched, spanning semiconductors, critical minerals, energy, defense, and, of course, super intelligence.  U.S.-India partnership does not move in a flat line; it climbs.  It begins in the ocean depths with critical data infrastructure.  It rises through our factories, labs, and research hubs.  And it extends into deep space through commercial exploration.  That is the trajectory of our alliance, rooted deep in fundamentals, accelerating upward at record speed, and heading straight for the stars.

Two hundred fifty years after our founders declared independence, the United States continues to build, lead, and win.  Tonight, we celebrate 250 years of American freedom: the freedom to innovate, to compete, and to prosper.  We celebrate tonight alongside a friend with whom we share far more than common values.  The United States and India aren’t just strategic partners; we’re architects of the world of tomorrow, building the future of innovation, security, and prosperity together.  (Applause.)

As the world’s oldest and largest democracies, we share a resolute foundation for cooperation, from trade and resilient supply chains to a free and open Indo-Pacific through the Quad.  In my travels across India, I’ve seen massive potential and an overwhelming drive for deeper ties with America.  It reinforces what I say everywhere I go:  The U.S.-India partnership is not just built in Delhi and Washington; it is built in Mumbai.  It is built in Ahmedabad, Hyderabad, Chennai, Goa, and the many cities that make India the engine of Indian prosperity.

Before I introduce Secretary Rubio to the stage, I want to thank the extraordinary team who brought this evening to life today.  Please put your hands together to everybody who put a lot of effort into making this happen, to our benefactors and supporters who are here with us tonight.  (Applause.)  Let’s also give a huge round of applause to our very own U.S. Navy musicians, the talented members of the 7th Fleet Band, the Orient Express, who are also celebrating with us tonight.  (Applause.)  Thank you for bringing American music, your energy, and the spirit of the Indo-Pacific to Mumbai.  And thank you, Madhura Padave, for that wonderful rendition of India’s national anthem also.  (Applause.)

I want to bring up somebody who wakes up every morning and who looks out for a way to make the United States better, somebody who the President trusts, someone that the world seeks for his incredible judgement.  Please give a very warm welcome for the second time this year – and I promise you he will be back next year – to my great friend, the Secretary of State, Marco Rubio.  (Applause.)

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Sergio left his phone.  Should I read what’s on it or – (laughter).  You want to leave it here?  All right.

Well, thank you.  It’s a great honor to be back with all of you here today, and I say “be back” because I was just here three or four months ago.  And it’s interesting, when I was here three or four months ago Sergio threw a big 250 party in Delhi, and now we’re here, another 250 party in Mumbai, and I’m starting to think all our embassy does here is throw parties.  Every – (laughter) – but we’ll see what they come up with next year.  You can’t celebrate 251 the same way, it’s just not the same ring to it, but it is an honor to be back and I’m glad to be back.

I said earlier today – we had this press conference, we had members of the media there obviously, and they were asking – somebody asked the question:  Why are you back in India after just four months?  And I said, well, number one, it happens to be the most populous nation on Earth.  It happens to be the oldest democracy on Earth.  It happens to be such a strong geopolitical partner to the United States.  But here’s where I ended, by saying is – I actually think we don’t come enough.  I actually think we don’t come enough.  (Applause.)

And it’s not just for the parties.  (Laughter.)  Because if you think about the story and the history of the 21st century, it’s being written in this region and it’s being written right now.  One day someone will write a book about the history of the 21st century, and there will be a chapter in there about other regions in the world, but the 21st century will be defined, for better and for worse, by what happens in this region.  We know that.  We can sense it; we can feel it.

And I just spoke to our team over at the consulate a few – about an hour ago, and I told them that.  I said sometimes when you’re living through history you don’t even realize you’re living through history, because it’s moving so fast and it’s happening so profoundly that sometimes you don’t stop and realize the moment that you’re in.  I truly believe that the relationship between the United States and India is defining in many ways, in a positive way, what the 21st century can look like.  (Applause.)

Now, just to prove to you that those are not idle words – because these are nice things to say in every country you go to, right?  But just to prove that those are not idle words, and I see the – our colleagues from Washington from the Congress are here.  They know these issues that are being debated, and I’m also very grateful they have a very strong congressional delegation here, a bipartisan congressional delegation.  Number one, I’m glad they’re here because they pass the budgets that fund all our agencies and – so don’t go back and say that all we do is throw parties, guys, because that’s – (laughter).  And – but the second is because they perceive it in Congress as well.

Think about the issues that today are dominating the world.  Number one, everyone recognizes that we are living through a moment of extraordinary technological innovation unlike anything man has ever seen.  We’ve seen technological advances in human history in the past.  We’ve seen what it does – it’s disruptive, positive and negative.  Industrialization was an incredible advance.  It pulled millions of people out of poverty.  It allowed countries to advance.  It had a positive impact.  It had some downsides to it too, and we spent decades dealing with it.

Automation and the information age also had a dramatic – think about how we live life today compared to 20, 30, 40 years ago.  The immediate and instant access to information, immediate and instant access to each other and to people – this was revolutionary.  It had positives and it had negatives to it as well.  But what we are living through now – in the age of super intelligence, in the age of quantum computing – is moving faster than any technological advance in all of human history.  And at its best, technological advance, what it allows us to do as human beings is it allows us to do human things better.

And so the challenge we face is how do we harness the power of super intelligence.  How do we harness the power of all of these things in a way that is beneficial, understanding that there will be downsides to it?  We have to mitigate the downsides, but we must dominate and accentuate the positive aspects of it.  I say this to you because, on that issue, the United States and India is aligned, perfectly aligned.  We view it the same way.  We have the same expertise.  We have the same talented people working on it from engineers to creative thinkers and innovators.  We are aligned and we want to see a world in which super intelligence is used to the betterment of man and one in which the harms that it could cause are mitigated.

If you look at geopolitics, no matter what you make, no matter what you innovate, it has to get from you to the places you’re sending it to unless you only want to sell to your own economy.  That requires free and unfettered ability to trade.  That requires open sea lanes, as an example, and in particular for the United States and India, a free and secure Indo-Pacific.  (Applause).  One in which no nation can claim dominance or ownership of international shipping lanes.  On that issue we are 100 percent and perfectly aligned.

I could go on and on, but the point is of course, no two nations on Earth will ever be perfectly aligned on every issue, and of course there will be times and points of disagreement.  But on the core issues that matter – the core issues that define the national interest of both India and the United States – the alignment between our countries is extraordinary, especially for two countries of this size, especially for two countries of this magnitude.  Because we can be aligned – and not that smaller countries don’t matter; of course they do.  But when you’re talking about a country that’s the – approximating 1.5 billion people and the largest economy in the world in the United States – when two powers like that can be aligned on these issues that are going to define the 21st century, then we have an opportunity together to write those pages in the history of the 21st century.

And so while today we celebrate here the 250th birthday of the United States for the 18th time here in India – (laughter) – but – I’m kidding.  They’ve only done it 12.  (Laughter.)  But – it’s a year-long celebration.  But while we’re here to commemorate and understand that, we also understand that what we are focused on collectively is not just to be proud of what America has achieved over 250 years, but we’re also here to celebrate what together we can achieve for the next 250 years.

The truth of the matter is that we are making decisions now in geopolitics and economics and trade and in commerce that will impact people that have not even been born, that will define the world in ways we cannot even fully comprehend or appreciate.  And what I’m excited about – what I challenge our team to be excited about at the Department of State; what I just challenged our team at the consulate here in Mumbai to be excited about – is be excited about the fact that today you are assigned to a place and you are working in a place where the United States is in partnership with another powerful, influential, and advanced country are helping to write the story of the 21st century in a way that betters the values that we share in common – values rooted in the dignity of the individual, values rooted in democratic principles, values rooted in the rule of law, and values always rooted in the belief that our job, for those who serve in public office, is to leave the next generation better off than ourselves.

And to me this is the best way to celebrate 250 years of the American miracle, is to find partners from around the world where together and working together we can take the things that made America exceptional as a nation and share it in partnership with friends like you.  And so we are grateful for the opportunity to be here tonight and to be with all of you.  I’ll see you again in three months because I’m coming now every three months, so thank you very much.  (Applause.)

MODERATOR:  Thank you, Mr. Secretary and Ambassador Gor.  We are privileged this evening to also hear from India’s Minister of External Affairs, His Excellency Dr. Jaishankar.  (Applause.)

EXTERNAL AFFAIRS MINISTER JAISHANKAR:  Secretary Marco Rubio, Mrs. Rubio, Chairman Jason Smith and members of the CODEL, Foreign Secretary, Ambassador Sergio Gor, dear friends, it’s a real pleasure to join you all in Mumbai at the celebrations of the 250th anniversary of American independence.  I’m glad that Secretary Rubio has come here, because Mumbai has both a special significance for India and a special place in our ties with the United States.

Mumbai is of course India’s financial capital, but it is very much more than that.  Bollywood is an integral element of its personality.  Mumbai is a center of learning and a hub for research and for technology.  Its traditions of entrepreneurship are deeply linked to the trade that its ports service.  The ethos of this city has many expressions, be it in sports or culture, media, or music.  Most of all, this is a cosmopolitan city that serves as a gateway to the world, and it is one that is deeply invested in our relationship with the United States.  Therefore, Secretary Rubio, it is particularly fitting that you celebrate this important anniversary here in Mumbai.  (Applause.)

Now, Mumbai’s connect with America started, some of you would be aware, with what was called the ice trade, trading in ice.  Today it is very much at the heart of our financial, commercial, and technology ties.  There is so much about the past that can be cited in that regard: that Swami Vivekananda set out from here on his famous journey to Chicago; that Leon Abbey brought jazz to this city – in fact, Secretary, to the very hotel where you are staying; that General Electric reactors have long contributed to the to the energy requirements of this city; or that in fact Starbucks opened its first Indian outlet here.  And now that one of your leading cricketers – Secretary, you won’t be counted as a friend of India until you really understand cricket – so one of your leading cricketers, Saurabh Netravalkar, actually hails from Mumbai.  (Applause.)

Friends, our ties have indeed come a long way.  History records that many Americans were supportive of India’s struggle for independence.  After that, whether it was infrastructure and education or food, agriculture, and technology, our partnership has made a real difference to India’s development.  Conversely, Indian talent and Indian skills have helped shape the tech era in the United States.  (Applause.)

Of course, we’ve had our share of issues, including the manner in which we approach the world and its challenges.  But in the last few decades, our partnership has steadily expanded, acquiring new dimensions and new facets.  The Quad, Mission 500, Pax Silica, and the FORGE Initiative are among some of the notable examples.  And growth in the fields of energy, in defense, and civil aviation sectors have actually been striking.

Now, like many other symbols of success and modernity, Mumbai too has been attacked by those who wish it ill.  The spirit of the Mumbaikar was demonstrated in the response to the 26/11 terrorist assault.  Six Americans also lost their lives at that time.  Secretary Rubio, all of Mumbai – and indeed, all of India – appreciates your laying a wreath today at the Taj Hotel’s 26/11 memorial.  (Applause.)

India also welcomes this administration’s decision to extradite Tahawwur Hussain Rana, who was one of the key culprits.  (Applause.)

We are clear on the right to defend ourselves against terrorism and expect that our partners show zero tolerance towards terrorism as well.  (Applause.)

As we mark this anniversary together, it is natural to reflect on the way ahead.  The world order is clearly in transition, and countries are responding accordingly.  Each one of us – each one of us – will naturally pursue our national interest.  In such an era, expanding our convergence is key to deepening our ties.  There are new opportunities in our partnership in trade, investment, technology, and energy, and these await us.  We have a shared stake in strengthening peace and stability in the Indo-Pacific.  Engaging regularly and identifying common interests will certainly help provide a pathway.

So, your presence here, Secretary Rubio, and the discussions that we’ve had earlier in the day hold great value.  So let me conclude by reiterating India’s best wishes to the United States on this landmark anniversary and, while doing so, reaffirm our commitment – our continued commitment – to advance the India-U.S. strategic partnership.  I thank you.  (Applause.)

MODERATOR:  Thank you, Your Excellency, for your incredible and inspiring words.  Ladies and gentlemen, please prepare your glasses as Ambassador Gor offers a toast to America.

AMBASSADOR GOR:  I want to thank everyone for being here.  If you’re in this room, it’s because you care about this relationship.  What we have seen over the next[1] 250 years has been incredible.  What we hope to see over the next 250 years will be even more incredible.  So please join me in raising a glass to the next 250 years of America, and happy birthday, USA.  Cheers.  (Applause.)

The United States will host the inaugural U.S.-Pacific Islands Conference of Police Ministers (COPMIN) in Los Angeles, California, on October 21–22, 2026.  U.S. Coast Guard Commandant Admiral Kevin Lunday will co-chair the ministerial with Palau Minister of Justice Jennifer Olegeriil and lead the U.S. delegation.  Assistant Secretary of State for International Narcotics and Law Enforcement Affairs F. Cartwright Weiland and senior officials from the Federal Bureau of Investigation, the Department of Homeland Security, the Drug Enforcement Administration, Homeland Security Investigations, U.S. Customs and Border Protection, PACOM/Joint Interagency Task Force West, the Department of the Treasury, and other agencies will join the delegation.

COPMIN will bring together police ministers and law enforcement leaders from across the Pacific Islands, joined by counterparts from Ecuador and the Philippines, alongside the delegation from the United States, to confront the transnational criminal networks that threaten the region. Narcoterrorists and transnational criminal organizations exploit the vastness of the Pacific Ocean to move fentanyl precursors, cocaine, methamphetamine, and other narcotics into communities in the Pacific and the United States.  These networks also run online scams and commit other cyber-enabled crimes that defraud our citizens while they launder their profits through underground banking networks. Additionally, distant-water fishing fleets engaging in illegal, unreported, and unregulated fishing deplete the ocean’s fish stocks.

Over two days, COPMIN will advance joint efforts to counter drug trafficking, strengthen maritime and port security, combat cyber-enabled fraud, and disrupt illicit finance. At a cross-regional roundtable, Ecuador and the Philippine Coast Guard will join U.S. and Pacific officials to examine how trafficking and illegal fishing networks span the Americas, Southeast Asia, and the Pacific. Pacific officials will visit the Port of Long Beach, where U.S. Customs and Border Protection will demonstrate drug detection technology.  They will also tour the harbor to see firsthand how U.S. port officials work to counter criminal networks trying to infiltrate the supply chain.

The United States has a direct and enduring stake in the security of the Pacific.  More than 1.9 million U.S. citizens and nationals live in Hawaii, Guam, American Samoa, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands, while tens of millions more live along our western seaboard, from California to Alaska.  We are committed to working with our Pacific partners to protect Americans from transnational crime.  When the Pacific is secure, America is safer.

## [Remarks] Secretary of State Marco Rubio Remarks to the Press — Marco Rubio

QUESTION:  So Mr. Secretary, this is your second visit with India in a couple of months, which is pretty remarkable.  What are your intentions here?  What is your goal?  And if I may ask a second side question, do you have any hope of concluding the trade deal with India, between India and —

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, first of all, I will point out that the foreign minister of India flew to the United States four times this year.  This is the most populous country in the world, it’s the largest democracy in the world, it’s an important commercial partner and business partner of the United States.  We also do a lot of defense cooperation.  They’re a key member of the Quad.  They’re a strong strategic partner.  So I actually don’t think we come enough, and I think we need to have more – we have.  We had – the Commerce Secretary’s been here, right?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  The FBI Director, the Vice President’s come, the Attorney General.  So this is a very important partner for us.  As far as the trade agreement is concerned, obviously the State Department doesn’t negotiate trade agreements, but we are very much involved in what’s happening.  In my perch as National Security Advisor, we care a lot about it.  We’re very, very close.  I would say we’re about as close as you can be without (inaudible) agreement.  I think there’s a small number of items to wrap up, so I think that will happen shortly – we’re hoping; it should.  And hopefully I’ve got some good news to announce fairly soon, and perhaps (inaudible) can sign it thereafter.

We’re – we feel very optimistic that we will have something in place here very soon.

QUESTION:  Sir, have you spoken to Zelenskyy recently?  And is there any way for the U.S. and Ukraine to repair its ties after this recent spat?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  I spoke to him today.  We had a 45-minute conversation.  Look, I’m seeing a lot of things in the media that are not accurate, so let me clear those up – as I did in my conversation with him.  One is the Department of War, everything they’ve been doing with Ukraine, remains the same.  That has not changed.

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Nothing has changed.  I mean, PURL’s a program in which when things are available for sale or sold to the Europeans, we in turn transfer it to them.  But I’ll get to munitions in a moment.  The second is intelligence sharing.  The President made it very clear he’s ordered no such changes.  So none of that has changed; none of it.  And I see reporting that it has, but it’s just not accurate.  It’s not true.  And so that was important to clarify.

Now, you ask about sales.  I know one of the things that President Zelenskyy has been talking about is that he’s been trying to buy interceptors, and I understand.  So does every other country in the world.  I also talked to Saudi Arabia today; they’d like interceptors.  Israel would like interceptors.  We would like interceptors.  Every country in the world would like interceptors, and many parts of the world are asking for that.  It’s probably the most in-demand product right now in the defense field.  So when a country doesn’t get interceptors from the United States, it’s not that we’ve denied it.  We have our own needs too.  We have minimum standards that we have to meet internally in our base presence all over the world.  That includes in our bases in Europe, as an example.  There’s minimum standards that our defense department, our Department of War, requires to protect these facilities, and obviously in the Middle East.

So all of this is being managed.  I know if you want – if you view Ukraine or you view any issue through a straw, and say I’m only focused on this one issue – well, you can’t do that.  That’s not how foreign policy and defense policy’s made.  It has to all be balanced in conjunction with one another.  We have a global defense footprint, and many of our installations around the world require defense, the air defense.

So there’s nothing being deliberately denied to Ukraine.  It’s simply – it’s a balance between our obligations to other partners, and frankly our own standards and requirements for our own facilities as well.

QUESTION:  As you mention, the Saudis have asked for interceptors.  Are you in a position – is the U.S. in a position to provide interceptors for every country in the world that needs them, or do you naturally need to —

SECRETARY RUBIO:  No, we’ve never been – no, no, we’ve never been in the position.  These interceptors are expensive to make.  They’re the best in the world, they work incredibly well, but they’re expensive.  They take time to make.  Now, the good news is that our production rate is increasing rapidly.  You’ve seen that now, and I think we feel very good about our stockpile of multiple munitions, including interceptors.  But it takes time to build these things up.  It takes time to make them.  These are highly sophisticated machinery, which obviously is what makes them so good, but also so hard to make.

So in the case of Saudi Arabia in particular, we obviously have an airfield presence there, and so we defend that presence.  We’re doing other things to help them; other countries are as well.  I’m just using – I’m not pointing to Saudi Arabia as a vulnerability.  I’m pointing it to you as an example, that virtually in every conversation I have today with some counterpart around the world revolves on one of two things.  Number one is the desire for air interceptors, and number two is trade.  I can help them a little bit on the interceptors; on the trade, that’s not my portfolio, but I obviously know it impacts our bilateral relations in multiple ways.

QUESTION:  Just to put a fine point on that, Mr. Secretary, what Zelenskyy has said and what’s been reported in some media outlets is that there was a batch of interceptors that was purchased by European (inaudible) by PURL that was awaiting – that is awaiting a final signature from Secretary Hegseth, and it has not received that signature and as such it hasn’t gone to Ukraine.  Is that inaccurate?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  No, I was talking – it could be the case, because these things are always reviewed through the context of whether they’re needed somewhere else, or whether even they need to remain in Europe.  We do have bases in Europe; our partners do as well.  You recently saw, for example – and I’m not just pointing to this, this is something that’s emerging – but you saw a threat against an airfield in the UK.  We have other large air presences in the region.  And then we have minimum requirements – not only (inaudible) but as part of our NATO presence.  So all these things are balanced on a daily basis.

I think the important takeaway from you is that there has not been some deliberate political directive to go out and harm Ukraine.  This is just not accurate.  Even this talk about I think on the 13th and again on the 18th there were air strikes in Ukraine that were not detected – that is not because America did not provide information.  I saw somebody associated with the Ukrainian Government say that people in Ukraine are denying because the U.S. is denying them information.  That’s scandalous.  That’s outrageous.  That’s just simply not true.  That’s not accurate.

Now, that there was a problem identified, that perhaps there was an internal issue in terms of them being able to take the information that was being provided and systemize and react to it?  That’s possible.  We’re looking at it.  Our understanding is that to the extent that happened on the 13th and on the 18th, it was a technical matter.  It was not the result of any sort of U.S. directive not to provide advance warning of a strike.  And we are providing the same warnings to them now that we always have.

QUESTION:  And so what’s going on with the U.S.-Ukraine relationship?  I mean, intel sharing, Starlink, interceptors – I mean, it seems like —

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, Starlink is a private commercial company.

QUESTION:  Right, right.  But it seems like there are some serious tensions between U.S. and Ukraine.  What is at the core of that?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, there’s no doubt, and that’s why we had that call today.

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, we had a call today.  I think at it’s core is the President is frustrated, because he – first of all, he doesn’t like to be blamed for things that aren’t true, including accusations that somehow he had ordered intelligence sharing to be cut off.  And that’s not true; he’s said that repeatedly.

All of that said, I think the President understands – and has said consistently from the very beginning – that the way this war needs to end is with a negotiated deal, and he wants to make one.  And he wants both sides to come to an agreement.  And so I certainly – but I think the President more than anything was responding to the insinuation that somehow he is doing things to deliberately harm Ukraine, which is not fair and not true.

SECRETARY RUBIO:  But he’s most certainly angry and frustrated at the lack of progress in those negotiations, after having dedicated almost two years to them now.

QUESTION:  So it’s not about the energy strikes?  Ukraine strikes on energy sites in Russia?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Oh, well, that’s a – there’s no doubt, we’ve expressed – but we’ve openly expressed to you repeatedly that we don’t think their energy strikes, for example, on shipping in the Black Sea – it goes back months now that we’ve been complaining about that.  We had American companies (inaudible) – what are we supposed to do when an American company calls and says we’ve had our cargo struck, and it’s happened multiple times?  So that was an irritant, there’s no doubt about it.

I think there’s no – there’s also the reality that the combination – it’s not just what’s happening there.  The Straits of Hormuz, there are nights where as much oil gets through there as pre-conflict, and most nights it’s at least 70 or 80 percent of it.  But it’s not just the Straits of Hormuz anymore; it’s pipelines in Saudi Arabia that have been targeted by the Houthis, and it is the war between Russia and Ukraine, and the attacks on refining capabilities that’s putting pressure – not on the United States alone.  You travel to any country in the world, and they will tell you that they’re having a diesel problem, that there’s a diesel problem in terms of access to affordable diesel that so much of their economies are powered by.  There are multiple countries around the world that are facing a very dangerous situation there.

So of course we’re concerned about what they strike.  Which is one of the reasons why this war needs to end, among others.  One of the reasons why this war needs to end is because too many people are dying.  One of the reasons why this war needs to end is because Ukraine’s electric grid is being devastated.  One of the reasons this war needs to end is because every month 20- or 25,000 Russians are slaughtered in – as they try to advance in Ukraine.  All these are bad things.  Mass death of this – the destruction, the death of civilians in Kyiv, as we’ve seen with some of these ballistic missile strikes.  This is a terrible, bloody, nasty war that needs to come to an end, and I don’t know anyone in the world who disagrees with what I’m saying.  And we’re trying to do the best we can to sort of bring both parties to a place where this war can end in a negotiated way, because right now as it currently stands, not only will more people continue to die and more things continue to be destroyed, but there’s a very real risk – there’s the real risk here of escalation, of it spreading to some other place.

QUESTION:  On the – one the subject of energy, what role can India play in terms of meeting the world’s energy demand?  Can it – can companies, Indian companies refine more Venezuelan crude?  Are there —

SECRETARY RUBIO:  They can, and we’ll talk about that tomorrow with Reliance.  They’ve done so in the past.  They have the capability to refine heavy crude.  They’ve done so in the past, and I think as more and more Venezuelan oil comes online, it’s something they can do.  Refining capacity – it’s not just being able to produce oil.  I think what a lot of people who have become experts in the energy field over the last few months are just learning, because of this conflict, refining capability’s a key part of it.  And India’s a refining superpower, and they have the ability to do that.  They have companies that know how to do that.

They also, by the way, have (inaudible) oil concessions in Venezuela; they can produce more themselves.  So all these are positive things.  And I’m not claiming to you that Venezuela alone can fill the hole that could be caused by some of the things that are happening around the world, but I think over time it most certainly can, and in the short term it can somewhat provide relief.  So that’s one of the things we’ll talk about with India.  We talked about it in the past, and they sound ready to – they have – they are really – they’re ready to do it, they’re willing to do it.  There might be a few impediments that stand in the way in terms of sanctions and so forth that we have to work through, pre-existing sanctions on the Maduro regime.

QUESTION:  Sir, President Trump suggested he was open to a summit with Xi and Putin, to talk not only about Ukraine, he said, but also nuclear issues.  Do you have indications that China is open to discussion on arms control issues?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, the only way you’re going to find out is to try.  I mean, I know what they’ve historically said.  But I don’t know why it would be a surprise – I think we – I think it’s time for – I’m not saying you individually, but people need to grow up when it comes to foreign policy a little bit, okay?  It is – I don’t think it’s unusual, frankly, for the three most powerful nuclear armed nations on Earth, for their leaders to communicate with one another.  I think it makes all the sense in the world.  They may have strong disagreements about – we also have a President, rightfully so, who does not consider talking to someone to be a concession.  I hear people on TV – oh, you’re rewarding so-and-so by talking to them.  Well, that’s absurd.

For example, how are you going to reach a peace agreement between Russia and Ukraine if you don’t talk to Russia and Ukraine?  You have to talk to them, right?  So I mean, that’s the bottom line of diplomacy and negotiations.  You have to be able to speak to the other side.

So I don’t think it’s – frankly, I think if there’s an opportunity, the President’s expressed an openness to do it for the United States, Russia, and China at the leader level to have a conversation about things like arms control, that would be a very positive thing for the world.  That should be something the world is enthused by.

Now, I imagine there’s a lot of differences and disagreements between the three countries that are probably not going to be resolved in a summit like that.  But there are a lot of important topics, and I think the world is a safer place when the leaders of the most powerful nuclear arsenals in the world are at least communicating with one another.

So I don’t know if China’s willing to discuss it.  I know what they’ve historically said, that they’ll talk about it when they catch up with everybody else.  But I think the fact we have a President that was willing to try is a good thing, something Americans should be proud and happy about.

QUESTION:  Mr. Secretary, tomorrow you’re going to Jamnagar, or – see where the Reliance Industries and their refinery, and the CEO of that.  Why is a U.S. Secretary of State to see the CEO of a refinery?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, we just – there’s – I believe somebody just asked me a question about whether – I think you just asked me a question about whether that’s —

SECRETARY RUBIO:  — we look at refine, and I just answered to you that in order to – for energy to become usable you have to refine it.  And so you have a company with a massive amount of refining capability – I think I should go talk to them, especially when it involves nation states, oil from another country, as Secretary of State.

I’m also here in my capacity as the National Security Advisor.  And so I have (inaudible) for everyone, and that is that energy is related to national security.  Countries fight wars over energy, as an example.  Economies collapse over lack of energy.  So we’re going to go see a company that has a tremendous amount of refining capability, so it makes all the sense in the world to talk to them about it.  And again, there’s – it’s very difficult to understand someone or see what the possibilities are if you’re not going to speak to them.

QUESTION:  To jump in on the oil, the Trump Administration said last year that they were upset with the amount of Russian oil that India was purchasing.  India, obviously, buys less oil than it did, but it’s still the number two purchaser in the world.  Are you satisfied with the degree to which India has reduced its Russian oil —

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Yeah, I mean, India – first of all, I think what India has expressed repeatedly is their desire to diversify their energy portfolio, which is a smart thing to do.  Obviously, it’s not easy to do that from one day to the next.  So I think they’ve made substantial progress in that regard, and we’re happy to see that.  But in the end, it’s not just about Russian oil; it’s about their desire to diversify.  And obviously, we think it could be a source of some of their energy inputs.  Obviously, we’ve discussed Venezuela and other countries, and I think it’s in our interest to have a strong ally who has a diverse energy portfolio.

QUESTION:  And you are going to – just to clarify – you are going to talk to Reliance, encourage them to enter production in Venezuela?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, we’re going to – I mean, we’ve talked to them about it in the past.  We’re going to go see what their hold-up is and what opportunities exist.  Obviously, I’m not going to – we’ll see how the conversation goes.  But you guys – this is a company that has had a presence in Venezuela in the past, and its refining capabilities are substantial.
