## [Remarks] Secretary of State Marco Rubio at a Press Availability — Marco Rubio

MODERATOR:  Thank you, guys.  The Secretary of State of the United States, Marco Rubio.

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Thank you.  It’s kind of strange.  I think you guys know who I am.  You wouldn’t be here if you didn’t know who I was.  So – but thank you.  Well, I want to thank the city of Atlanta.  They did a great job hosting us this week.  It’s one of America’s great business and innovative hubs, and it’s been an ideal location for us to not just showcase our country, but to focus on critical economic policy issues that are facing the world today.  Under President Trump’s leadership, the United States has established a clear mission for the G20 to get back to basics and to focus on the core purpose for why this forum was created in the first place.

So this week in Atlanta, at the foreign ministers’ level, we executed on that directive.  We focused on core economic, energy, technology, and supply chain challenges.  And we addressed practical solutions and real outcomes that benefit the citizens not just of the countries represented here today, but of the world.  We have a lot of complex, interconnected problems in the world right now that impact our countries collectively, and we have to work together to find solutions to those problems.  That’s why it’s important to get this group together to have a serious, focused meeting, and I’m happy to say that this week we covered a lot of ground.  And you’ll find everything we talked about in my chair statement that will be coming out, if it hasn’t come out already.

One of the main issues we addressed, of course, was freedom of navigation.  Safe passage of vessels in international waters is vital to the livelihood of the world, and we see the attacks – and that’s what they are.  Let me understand – and I underscored this in my comments; I’ll underscore it with you now.  We now live in a world where the Iranian regime actively and openly targets commercial vessels for attack – not naval vessels; we’re not talking about naval vessels, we’re not talking about submarines.  We are talking about them saying:  Here’s a commercial vessel with cargo, oil, natural gas, and we are going to try to sink it.  We are going to try – they are openly attacking vessels as a tool of statecraft.  That’s the world we live in.  And I think Central Command and the United States has done a phenomenal job of protecting this shipping.

And today, the amount of fuel getting through there is comparable with what was coming out before the conflict, but nonetheless we’ve had to defend these ships from drones and missiles almost every single night.  And we’re also seeing attacks not just in the straits now, but also in the Bab al-Mandeb, and the hostile coercion, of course, that we see – maybe not as kinetic but certainly still concerning – in the South China Sea and the Strait of Taiwan and elsewhere that also puts economies in danger.

One of the most severe consequences to sea lanes being under attack or under coercion is the rise of food security problems.  When energy and fertilizer and agricultural products can’t reach their destinations, it threatens the most vital necessity for each and every one of our nations and for all the nations of the world.  And I’m grateful that this group was able to have purposeful meetings on this subject, but obviously – and that we are aligned at least in our goal, in our understanding that it’s a serious problem, although I cannot say we are aligned on how to solve it.  My closing comments on critical minerals really could have applied to this as well, and that is that we all agree there’s a problem, but obviously there’s work to be done to find agreement on – collectively on solutions.

I’m also glad that on the sidelines of this G20 gathering, we were able to get the Quad group together again and discuss a myriad of topics, a myriad of issues.  The Quad is something we’ve invested a lot of time in.  I think this year alone we’ve met four or five times.  It was my first meeting as Secretary of State when I took over in January of 2025.  And we’re going to continue to work in that forum as it’s gone from a place where we just meet and talk about problems to a place where we identify areas to work together on, and are actually being able to act on that.

And hopefully we’ll be announcing very soon, if we haven’t already, that we will have a Quad leaders’ meeting in Manila in the – I guess today is the 31st – next month.  So those – these are the issues that are important to the United States and to all the countries here today.  We have a great relationship with all the members of the Quad, and our partnership enables us to address these challenges in an effective, unified way that benefits the shared national interests of our respective countries.

I also had great bilateral meetings.  For example, we had a trilateral meeting with Japan and South Korea.  We’ve done that a number of times as well.  We signed a memorandum of cooperation on transnational Arctic-Pacific partnership with them.  And together we’ll ensure that the Arctic remains a region free of dominion or coercion, and that it instead remains a place of peaceful relations and commerce.

We’re grateful for all of the invited participants who came to Atlanta here this week.  I want to recognize the Polish delegation in particular.  Poland as a country is making great strides economically as well as in the security domain because they put their national interests and their citizens first.  They are stronger; they are more robust as a nation.  And America, as an ally and a partner of Poland, therefore, is stronger as well.  President Trump believes very strongly that Poland deserves a seat at this table of global leaders, and we very much hope to see them – and other strong nations on the rise – invited back next year.

As we prepare for the summit in Doral, which is coming up now in December, I want to give my thanks and praise to my fellow cabinet members who have led extremely successful ministerials across the country leading up to this one.

The U.S. presidency of the G20 has been one of the many highlights of the America’s – America’s 250 – 250th birthday celebration.  We’ve successfully used this platform to build global initiatives that truly benefit the American people, which is ultimately our primary obligation.  So we are looking forward to another great G20 meeting that will be led by President Trump in Florida in December, and that will be a capstone to this year of American leadership.

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Yeah, I’m not an odds-maker.  I wouldn’t say more or less likely.  I would say there’s interest in facilitating it.  And by the way, it’s not new.  Like, Türkiye, for example, has been consistently and at one point did have an agreement in place in the past, that then of course fell apart.  So I wouldn’t say it’s – I don’t want to put or prognosticate the chances of it.  I do think it’s having an impact on many countries around the world; you heard that expressed today.  The African continent in particular is paying a high price for it, and there’s an energy component to it as well.

So I really hate to put, like, odds, because in foreign policy in particular odds are a hard thing to set.  But I do think there’s an incredible amount of interest in facilitating something, that we have nation-states that are involved, and India has also expressed an interest in perhaps floating some ideas.  And so we’re open to that.  We’ve been talking to countries.  But I can’t give you odds as to how likely it is to happen.  We’d like to see it happen.  It doesn’t solve the war problem, but at least it diminishes some of the impact – spillover impact that it’s having on the food security of vulnerable countries.

QUESTION:  Yeah.  My question is:  In your speech in Athens, you called for a revival of Western democratic values.

QUESTION:  And I was wondering, when it comes to food crisis or Ukrainian crisis, is there any of those values that the U.S., along with the Europeans, is trying to uphold in finding a solution on the Ukrainian conflict?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, I think – look, obviously, but I mean at the end of the day I think the Ukrainian-Russian issue is pretty singular right now, which is we’re trying to stop a very deadly war in which, on the one hand, Kyiv every single night is facing devastating attacks against its energy infrastructure, but also increasing number of civilians being killed; and even on the Russian side, you’re seeing 20,000 Russians killed a month.  It’s a very deadly and destructive war, and every month that goes by you’re just adding – I mean, think about how long it’s going to take.  When this stops, how long is it going to take Ukraine to rebuild its economy, rebuild its energy grid?  We also think it’s very costly for Russia as well.

So right now, I mean, yes, I mean, ultimately we would – I think embedded in all of this is values that we care a lot about.  But the primary value we care about is – it’s not really very complicated.  There’s a very nasty war going on, very destructive war, what appears to be in some ways of a stalemate that has the threat of escalation and spreading into other places.  We already see some of the ancillary effects of this crisis – as an example, the Black Sea; as an example, some of these irregular efforts at sabotage that have spread into other countries; drones that are being launched that are being intercepted by electronic means, and so they wander into the airspace of a NATO country.  There’s a lot of danger involved in that as well.

So from us, the singular focus is:  How can we – is there an opportunity for the United States, because we talk to both sides, to be able to facilitate a negotiated end to a very deadly, very costly, and very destructive war?  And that’s really what we’re focused on.  I think once – and that’s a pretty ambitious desire.  Everyone has encouraged us.  In every meeting we have, no matter what – be it the Europeans or others – we are encouraged to remain involved and engaged in this.  And so we’ve remained involved and engaged, but that’s really what we’re focused on there.  I don’t know how that ties into the speech I gave.

QUESTION:  Paris Huang, Voice of America.  Did you get a chance to convey the U.S.’s commitment to Taiwan and Ukraine to your Chinese and Russian counterparts?  Also, did you also get a chance to talk to them the potential bilateral meeting in APEC or even in Miami?  Thank you.

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, there’ll most certainly be a bilateral meeting at both.  I mean, they’ll meet in addition to being a part of these forums.  I’m certain that we’ll meet with regards to that.  So we really – we didn’t discuss it, those brief conversations about arrangements for both the attendance at G20 and at APEC.  So we talked about that, but that was the gist of it.  I mean, ultimately the point of this gathering, as I said, is we want – it’s not that geopolitical issues wouldn’t be discussed at G20 because I’m sure they are, and there were a lot of other bilateral meetings going on that we were not involved in between the different countries who were here.  And it’s why we structured the agenda the way we did, to leave plenty of time for some of these countries to interact with one another.  So I can’t speak to all of those meetings.

But the core of this meeting and the core of what the President’s vision has been for G20 is to bring the G20, which is primarily an economic forum, back to its core principle and purpose, and that’s what we tried to address in the agenda we outlined today.  That doesn’t mean that geopolitical issues weren’t discussed on the sidelines by different countries, but that’s what we focused on in both our agenda and in our engagements.

QUESTION:  Thank you, Mr. Secretary.  Majid Sattar from Allgemeine Zeitung, Germany.  I was wondering whether you fear that your job will become more difficult come January when you have to deal with a Democratic Congress.

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, first of all, we haven’t had an election yet so we don’t know what Congress will look like.  So second of all, no.  I mean, look, the foreign policy – Congress has a role to play in foreign policy; I played it for a very long time as a member of the Senate.  So I understand what Congress can and cannot do with regards to foreign policy.  But when it comes to issues of national security and foreign policy, the Executive Branch – and I learned this both ways; I learned it when we were in the majority and in the minority.  I learned it when Republicans had the White House and didn’t have the White House, that the majority – our foreign policy is largely directed by the Executive Branch.

Now, Congress has a role to play, primarily in the funding and the appropriations and in their oversight function.  But that’s just a normal part of our system.  I’ve been a part of it.  I’ve been a part of it on both sides.  I’ve been in the minority; I’ve been in the majority.  I’ve had a Democratic White House and I’ve had a Republican White House.  It won’t influence or change the President’s directives in terms of what he was elected to do.  The President was elected to execute the foreign policy of the United States.  That is a function of the Executive Branch.  It obviously will have oversight from the congressional branch and it will have funding from the congressional branch.  But that’s normal; administrations have played that in the past, with the president – the previous president, Biden, had a Republican Senate to deal with at one point.  President Obama had to deal with a Republican majority at some point as well during his – so it’s not unique to have divided power in our system, and we’ve always had a foreign policy.

And to my great frustration, when I was a member of the Senate, I realized that while there are things we could do, ultimately the foreign policy decisions are made by the Executive Branch and Congress plays a role.  There’s no doubt about it.  But the central role belongs to the Executive Branch.  It’s something I learned when I was in the Legislative Branch, and it’s something I know now.  But we’ll see how things play out.

QUESTION:  Thank you.  Mirtha Donastorg from The Atlanta Journal-Constitution.  So you’ve been –

QUESTION:  Thank you.  You’ve been lauding the city.  Last night at the reception you praised Atlanta and the region’s vibrant economy, calling it the economy of the future.  But when I talk to business owners and workers here, they’re struggling under high inflation and increased uncertainty from the administration’s economic policies.  So given the priorities of this meeting, did any concrete solutions come from your discussions that will help ease the economic burden people in this city and state and across the country are feeling?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Yeah, look, I think the way to cover the G20 is a little different than that.  And it’s the G20 is a gathering of the 20 – theoretically the 20 largest economies in the world that collectively represent 80-something percent of global GDP.  And the things we talked about today most certainly will have an impact, not just on Atlanta, not just on the United States, but the entire world; and not just next week or next month.  Some of these are long-term issues – as an example, supply chains; as an example, energy.  If access to energy is impeded or in any way we can’t generate sufficient energy, you can’t power your economy.  Energy costs are imbedded in the cost of everything we buy and everything we pay for, which is why the U.S.’s energy dominance and it’s self-sustainability policies are so important.  But none of these are magic elixirs that happen overnight.

Likewise in the case of supply chains, and I think we learned sort of the first inklings of – supply chains was something you just talked about in the business world but it really wasn’t widely discussed politically or in society until COVID.  And in COVID, people realized things we used to buy all the time are suddenly in short supply and you couldn’t get them because we realized that these supply chains are interlinked across the world, and if there’s a disruption halfway around the world, it could ultimately disrupt either how much you have to pay for something or how much access you have to it.

So I think the way to cover G20, it is not a forum in which you are going to come up with solutions that you are going to feel tomorrow or next week or solely in the United States.  It is a place where we are discussing issues that are going to impact and define the global economy – and Atlanta’s role in it, but the country’s role in it – for the next 20, 30, 40 years.  So these are big-picture things that we’re talking about long term.  Suffice it to say that whether it’s access to critical minerals necessary for technology, whether it’s access not just to energy to power what we do now but energy to power the things we need to do for a 21st century economy, or whether it’s supply chains across the board, these things are going to have a huge impact not just on what Atlanta looks like today but what Atlanta’s going to look like and how much it’s going to cost to live here and work here 10, 15, 20, 30 years down the road.  That really is what the G20 focuses on.

QUESTION:  Some time ago, an agreement was made between the U.S., Denmark, and Greenland regarding Greenland and the Arctic up there.  And for the last couple of weeks, the President has been very active in – on the campaign trail, saying that he was very glad that Denmark gave Greenland to the U.S.  And that is being perceived a little differently in Denmark and in Greenland.  So has the President misunderstood the agreement that was made, or has the people of Denmark and Greenland misunderstood?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Yeah, I’m not sure what comments you’re referring to, and I certainly haven’t followed how it’s been covered in Denmark.  Suffice it to say we have a very good deal, one the President is fully apprised of, he understands, and is very supportive of.  And it builds on what we’ve done there for a long time.  But one of the most important things that it does is it ensures that never do we have to worry about – irrespective of the decision Greenland makes to – if they one day go independent or stay, continue to be a part of the Kingdom of Denmark, it will never have to worry about it being a place where a potential adversary – through commercial means – can gain access to things that would be harmful to any of our countries.

I think just from a strategic standpoint and where it is geographically, which was what the President has always focused on when it comes to Greenland, is national security.  It has always played a critical role in national security.  It is more pronounced now, not just because of defenses against missiles but because of the Arctic.  And you now have a place that is not – we now have an agreement in place that will exist in perpetuity that ensures not just the equities of North America, meaning the United States and Canada, but all of our partners in NATO as well.
