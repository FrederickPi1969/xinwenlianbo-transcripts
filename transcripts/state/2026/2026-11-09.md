SECRETARY RUBIO:  — is an understanding that Ukraine will feel secure after the war.  And obviously, it has to be something that Russia agrees to as well in order for it to work.  So, I think that’s the conversations, and while it seems like, for some, it would look like we’re working backwards, the truth of the matter is those security guarantees are going to be key in order for Ukraine to be able to agree to something.  But it has to be something Russia agrees to as well.  I’m stating the obvious, but I think that’s why these talks are so critical.

QUESTION:  Is that something that the U.S. would put into a treaty, like the treaty guarantees here?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  I mean, potentially, but I don’t know if that’s – we’ve discussed it at that level of detail yet.  And as you know, treaties aren’t necessarily the easiest things to pass anymore.

SECRETARY RUBIO:  To get confirmed with the Senate and so forth.  I’m sorry, go ahead.

QUESTION:  Secretary, did you talk with Japanese foreign minister about ICC sanction today?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  He always raises it, obviously.  The main judge at the ICC is a Japanese citizen, so they raise the point, we make our point.  But we have a good relationship with Japan, but we understand that we have a difference of opinion when it comes to the ICC.

QUESTION:  Mr. Secretary, was there any further discussion today on the scale of the humanitarian situation in Gaza?  There’s been very severe flooding —

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Yeah, so the head of – the new head of the World Food Program, he used to work with the Department of Agriculture.  It’s now – came and gave us a brief on what needs to happen and the progress that we hope to make.  Obviously, there are agreements that were part of the ceasefire that we expect to be adhered to, and there is a growing humanitarian need that we want to address, and the World Food Program is uniquely positioned to be able to deliver that.  I think we’d like to see them continue to work out ways to work more closely with the UN program, because we think the World Food Program has the – what don’t want to see is redundancy of effort, and we don’t want to see inefficiency.

We actually think the World Food Program is able to deliver assistance in a way that perhaps the UN system on its own cannot do.  So, we’ve encouraged more coordination between the two, and that’s increasingly happening.  But – so it was raised today.

QUESTION:  On this very immediate issue, though, of getting more shelter in because there’s been terrible flooding – and I mean, five people were killed in a sandbag collapse.  You can see all the tents sort of blowing away.  I think two-thirds of the population are in tents.

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, we didn’t – and I know that instance today that you’re discussing where there was some sort of a – or some sand thing that collapsed and killed somebody.  That just was in the news today; I didn’t actually see that until after our meeting.  So that specific instance wasn’t raised.

What was raised was the need for wraparound services.  In essence, food alone is not enough.  And the goal of the World Food Program, by the way, is not ultimately just to continue to feed Gaza, but to eventually have Gaza have its own ability to produce its own food, and setting up of bakeries and other things that can create food for itself so that it becomes self-sustaining.  That’s the goal we all share.  But part of that, obviously, starts with shelter and the need for heat at some point here – and obviously given the winter approaching – and all these other needs that are necessary in order to sustain life.  All of that was part of the Board of Peace program.  All of that was part of the way the ceasefire came together.  It’s in some ways fallen behind schedule for a variety of reasons, but we expect it to catch up, and a lot of good work has gone into it.  And so, we’re optimistic that we can pull that together.

QUESTION:  What is the future of U.S. military aid to Israel?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, we’re currently negotiating a memorandum of understanding.  The 10-year memorandum certainly expires.  The Israelis —

QUESTION:  The President said it could expire – it could be done by the end of the year.  So, what’s the —

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Yeah, so the – but the Israeli system – the Israelis – Prime Minister Netanyahu has already stated that he wants to stop being a Foreign Military Financing country and go solely to foreign military sales.

QUESTION:  But he’s talking about a decade; the President mentioned a year.  I just wonder what U.S. policy is at the this point.

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, it’s what the President says, that he would like for the sales – he would like for the financing portion to end as soon as possible.  So, we’re working through that in terms of how we negotiate the memorandum of understanding.  But the President’s expectation is that Israel is now prosperous and rich enough to be able to be a sales country.  They can buy the weapons from us, but we no longer have to provide the financing.  It’s something the Israelis themselves have offered us.

QUESTION:  You think they’re ready to go it alone in – starting in 2027?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, that’s what the President’s has said we’re going to give them.  So ultimately, I think they could if they wanted to, yeah.  Absolutely.  They also produce weaponry, by the way, so part of the MOU would also involve the ability to co-produce on some things.  But on the question of financing, I think Israel’s clearly graduated beyond that at this point.  They themselves have expressed it.  We’ll negotiate the timeline.  The President wants it as soon as possible.  He thinks it makes sense to do it as soon as possible.

QUESTION:  We’re learning a little bit more about the articles of impeachment against Secretary Hegseth, with Republican Representative Massie saying that Hegseth used the Iran war and knowingly executed and implemented unlawful orders to initiate war against a foreign nation.  How would you respond to that, just the allegation of this being —

QUESTION:  Secretary, I know you’re not commenting on the current status of Nvidia, but can you explain – back in – when you guys went to China in, I believe it was May, Jensen Huang was initially not on that trip and then joined Air Force One kind of midway.  I believe it was in Alaska on the way to China.  Do you have any recollection of why he was initially left off or why he joined, or any of those details?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  No.  I mean, I have a lot of jobs, but the White House Travel Office isn’t one of them.  (Laughter.)  So I just – I mean, I don’t want to give you bad information.

QUESTION:  Can I ask about the UN secretary-general race?  Do you have a favorite?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  If I told you who the U.S. – if I told you who the U.S. wanted it, it would doom them to lose.

SECRETARY RUBIO:  No, there’s a couple really good candidates, people that we like, two or three, and the process will work itself out.  And I think we’ve expressed our views, but that – it’s an ongoing process.  So, we don’t have, like, a candidate that’s the U.S. candidate.  We see two or three very qualified people, and we’re going to let the voting continue on that front.  And at some point the world will make a decision on who they want, and we have our opinions.  And I talked about that with President Macron just a moment ago, but I think we’re just going to keep working it the way we are right now because other countries get a vote here as well.  So, we’re watching the process closely.

In the end, what we’re interested in more than anything else is that it be someone that’s committed to UN reform.  There’s real momentum behind UN reform.  I think it’s one of the things that’s going to save the UN, is that it reforms.  If it doesn’t, it’s going to be in a lot of trouble.  And we want someone as secretary-general that’s committed, truly committed, to reform.  And that’s the standard we’re using to judge all the candidates that are out there.

QUESTION:  Are you and President Macron on board, the same views on this issue?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  I can’t represent that his views are the same as mine on – I’m just saying that we – I think – I hope France is aligned with us on the need to reform the United Nations.  And I think there’s real momentum for that.  I have not gone into a single country or spoken to a single global leader that says, oh, the UN is perfect the way it is.  I think everyone generally agrees that the UN needs – the system needs to be reformed, that there was a lot of waste, that there was a lot of bloat, and that it needed to get back to its core principles.  I think it’s troubling for a lot of people that the UN really is not at the center of any of the global crises that are going on right now.  It has not played a meaningful role in major global crises in quite a while.  So, the whole system is in need of reform.  But I think the most immediate reform that people are concentrating on is that it’s lost its core principles and sort of grown so large and bloated and gotten to areas it was never intended to tackle.

So, we’ve made real progress over the last year on reform, and I think it’s important for the next secretary-general to be someone who’s committed to continuing to make those reforms, or the UN’s going to be in a lot of trouble.  I just – I’m being honest with you.  I just think that’s the – possible if we don’t fix it.

QUESTION:  Did you discuss with Macron the future of the mission to – the UN mission to Lebanon, UNIFIL?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  We did.  No, and I think the notion is – one of the things that we’re working on from our perspective is finding the ability to monitor the implementation of the pilot zones.  It doesn’t have to be a military mission.  It could be a civilian mission.  We have some ideas.  Because it’s important – these pilot zones, the Israelis leave; the Lebanese Armed Forces are supposed to come in and take them without Hizballah retaking them and using them to launch attacks.  We need a verification system trusted by everyone and trusted by both sides that both sides are complying with their obligations under the agreements that are in place.

So, we discussed a little bit some of the ideas we have on what that could look like.  We’re not prepared to sort of discuss it yet publicly, because obviously it’s sensitive and it would require the agreement of both the Lebanese and the Israelis in order for it to work.  But we discussed that process.

QUESTION:  But that could – that can involve UNIFIL?  Because the —

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, UNIFIL, as you know, is expiring, so something could replace it that could – that can be in that role.  But again, the details of that are still being worked on.  And I can’t really at this moment give you – other than to say that the goal remains that we do need some credible group on the ground that can verify who is – who’s complying with what on these agreements.  And that doesn’t necessarily have to be a military mission, which is what UNIFIL was in many ways.  It was military personnel.  It could be a civilian mission, but it’s a trusted verifier is what we’re searching for.  And we have some ideas about how to achieve it, and I think France could be helpful in that regard.

QUESTION:  Secretary, have you neutralized the Iranian threat against American diplomats overseas that last month U.S. intel warned about (inaudible)?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, look, we’re always concerned – yeah, we’ve addressed it by moving people away from the threat.  There’s a couple things that are a threat.  Obviously, we’re always worried about the safety and security of our personnel and their families overseas.  And so, when we see anything that could pose a potential threat beyond a certain threshold, we’re going to address it.  Beyond that, we’re also concerned about what’s going on with the Houthis in Saudi Arabia.  That could spiral at any moment.  In some cases, you’ve seen Saudi Arabia, for example, attack not just from Yemen, but you’ve also seen attacks come in from Iraq via the Iranian-backed militias.

So, there’s a – for us, what we always have to take into consideration is not just what’s happening now, it’s what could happen.  And if this spreads or metastasizes to other countries and airports are shut down, as an example, now we can’t get our people out and now – now they’re trapped.  So, there’s an internal process that goes on.  We evaluate – anytime that there’s any sort of danger involving facilities, we instigate – we initiate an internal process that does a security assessment of our personnel, of our ability to move them if things got bad, how many people are there, dependents and the like.  And then they issue recommendations done by our security folks and people on the ground.  And those recommendations come to me, and it was on the basis of that recommendation that we made the decisions that we made.  And it’s not just about Iran, frankly.  It’s also about what’s happening in Yemen with the Houthis.

QUESTION:  Secretary, could I ask you about the Western Hemisphere?

QUESTION:  Sorry.  Coming back to a question on the Western Hemisphere, Paraguay has been – under President Peña was a member of the Counter Cartels Coalition and the Shield of the Americas.  Do you have a view on the recent events and the removal of President Peña from office and the current political situation in Paraguay?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Yeah, but he’s been replaced by the vice president, who I also know, and is – I spoke to him on the plane ride here, and I’ve known him for a while, and a lot of people thought he would be the next president anyway.  So obviously we’ve had a good, constructive relationship with President Peña, but there’s an internal process going on in Paraguay that we’re not involved in and we’re not going to get involved in.  Our – I represent the United States of America.  What I’m interested in is our bilateral relationship and cooperation with Paraguay, and I expect that that will continue to be as strong as it’s ever been and will continue to grow the way it was growing.  I don’t believe this change will in any way negatively impact our relationship.  And that’s a good thing, and that’s an important thing.  So —

QUESTION:  On Cuba, are you sensing – sorry, one more.  Are you sensing any organic —

QUESTION:  — any organic effort on the ground in Cuba to actually oust the communist leadership there?

SECRETARY RUBIO:  That’s not the way to read it.  Here’s the problem with Cuba.

QUESTION:  — asked Cubans to reach out to the CIA, so I’m wondering if that’s delivered any results.

SECRETARY RUBIO:  Well, I wouldn’t tell you if I got a result from the CIA.  I mean – no, come on.  But I would – because then those people aren’t going to be doing very well.  So, all I can tell you is this.  Here’s the problem with Cuba.  The regime there is afraid of giving their people any kind of freedom, economic or otherwise, because they like to control them.  So, what the regime there is trying to do, and they’ve always done, is they want to have enough economic activities just so that they are like a third-world country, but not a fourth-world country – but they don’t want to lose control over people.  They don’t mind if the people don’t have a lot; they just want to make sure that whatever the people have, they depend on them 100 percent for it.  That’s always been the case.  How can they grow economically without losing control over their population?  That model doesn’t work.  And all it’s led to is millions of people leaving the country of Cuba, including many to the United States.  And that has to change, because that instability and that failed state that it’s created poses a threat to the national security and national interest of the United States.

So that’s what we’re focused on, is how can we get the people of Cuba more freedom – political freedom for certain, because our embargo on Cuba is codified in statute, and it requires political freedom in order to lift it; but also economic freedom.  We want the people of Cuba to have what virtually – the people of virtually every country in the Western Hemisphere has, and that is the ability to go out and make a living for themselves without depending on the government to provide it for them.  Even more troubling is that despite how bad things are in Cuba, there are insiders in that regime that have millions and millions of dollars stashed away in bank accounts all over the world, and that have come to – this is well documented, the military holding company GAESA, who’s been stealing what little they have from the Cuban people.  So, that also can’t continue.

So ultimately, our goal is for Cuba to have a better future, and we’re going to do everything we can to help put it on that path.  But in the end, it will require them to wake up and realize that there is no future on the path they’re on right now, and this is a very stubborn group of people over there, but we’ll keep working on it because it’s a priority.
